# The Price of the Digital Abstraction: Entropy and Encoding Overhead When Discrete Error Models Meet Continuous Quantum Hardware

## Abstract

Quantum error correction (QEC) theory assumes that noise on each physical device can be represented by a discrete set of Pauli errors acting on two-level systems, but superconducting transmons are anharmonic oscillators whose native dynamics are continuous and which are better described as qudits of dimension $d \approx 12$. This revision quantifies the information-theoretic overhead incurred when the continuous error model of such a device is forced into a discrete Pauli framework. Using analytic derivations with fully shown arithmetic, we compute (i) the classical register entropy required to label a digitized bosonic mode, $H_{\mathrm{reg}} = \log_2 12 = 3.584963$ bits; (ii) the dimension overhead of binary encoding a $d = 12$ qudit, $4/3$, with encoding efficiency $\eta = 0.896241$; (iii) the entropy distortion introduced by Pauli twirling of an amplitude-damping channel at $\gamma = 10^{-3}$, $\Delta H = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}$ bits (ratio $1.131489$), together with the exact law $\Delta H = 3\gamma/2$ valid for all $\gamma$; and (iv) the discarded Hilbert-space weight for a cold mode with $\bar{n} = 0.1$, $\approx 3.19 \times 10^{-13}$. Two characterizations in the prior version are corrected: the twirled entropy itself scales as $\gamma \log_2(1/\gamma)$, not linearly; only the twirling excess $\Delta H$ is exactly linear in $\gamma$, and the register quantity $\log_2 d$ is a floor on label information under a uniform distribution, not a code-independent floor on actual register entropy. The results give hardware designers and QEC architects explicit per-mode, per-cycle bookkeeping quantities with which to weigh radix choice, encoding efficiency, and entropy attribution.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum error correction is being deployed on superconducting processors whose physical devices are transmons: lithographically defined anharmonic oscillators with a dozen or more usable energy levels. The QEC layer that sits above these devices, however, treats each one as a qubit subject to a discrete error set $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$. Designers of large-scale quantum processors must therefore map continuous bosonic dynamics onto a discrete logical layer, and the costs of that mapping — discarded Hilbert-space weight, extra encoding qubits, and entropy bookkeeping that attributes errors to a discrete alphabet the device does not natively speak — do not appear in standard fault-tolerance threshold statements. If these hidden costs are large, they inflate the physical qubit budget, the measurement bandwidth, or the classical control and energy budget required to reach a target logical error rate, and thereby bear on the economic viability of quantum computing. If they are small, the digital abstraction is cheap and the engineering consensus in favor of qubit-based QEC is vindicated. Which of these situations holds is a decision-relevant question, and today it is answered mostly by intuition rather than by calculation.

This paper quantifies a slice of that hidden cost with explicit arithmetic. We treat the transmon as a qudit of dimension $d \approx 12$ — the same characterization adopted by the Rosetta analysis, which states that the transmon spectrum is discrete under Planck quantization and that the transmon is a qudit of $d \sim 12$, not a qubit [10] — and we compare the continuous amplitude-damping channel to its Pauli-twirled surrogate, deriving entropy and dimension overheads per mode and per correction cycle. The locale-framework analysis of quantum computing provides the conceptual frame: physical claims hold within a domain and fail at a boundary, and "a transmon is a qubit" is explicitly listed among the recurring claims of the field that carry such a boundary [9].
