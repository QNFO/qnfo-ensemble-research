# Why Vertices, Not Points?  A Quantitative Argument for Vertex‑Centred Braid Actions on Bruhat‑Tits Buildings  

## Abstract  
Bruhat‑Tits buildings provide the natural geometric arena for the representation theory of $p$‑adic groups and for recent constructions of $p$‑adic braid groups on these complexes.  In the existing literature the focus has been on the action of braid generators on *points* of apartments, yet the combinatorial structure of the building is fundamentally vertex‑centric: vertices encode homothety classes of lattices, edges encode elementary moves, and higher‑dimensional simplices are glued from vertices.  We argue that a vertex‑based formulation is both mathematically natural and computationally tractable.  Using the explicit combinatorics of the $\mathrm{SL}_2$ Bruhat‑Tits tree over $\mathbb{Q}_p$, we compute the exact number of vertices and edges inside a radius‑$r$ ball, and we translate these counts into a concrete bound on the size of the generating set for the $p$‑adic braid group introduced in the QNFO corpus [12].  The calculation shows that for $p=3$ and $r=2$ the braid group can be generated by at most $16$ elementary braids, a number that grows exponentially with $r$ as $p^{r}$.  We then discuss how this vertex‑centric perspective clarifies compactifications (via tropical fans [1,6]), convergence of parahoric subgroups [2], and the geometry of fixed‑point sets [4].  Limitations of the present analysis (restriction to rank‑one groups, finite radius, and omission of higher‑dimensional apartments) are identified, and a program for extending the argument to general semisimple groups is outlined.  

## 1. Introduction  
Bruhat‑Tits buildings are polysimplicial complexes attached to reductive groups over non‑Archimedean local fields.  Their vertices correspond to homothety classes of lattices in the underlying vector space, while higher‑dimensional simplices encode chains of nested lattices.  This combinatorial description makes the building a natural setting for $p$‑adic representation theory, harmonic analysis, and, more recently, for topological constructions such as $p$‑adic braid groups on buildings [12].  

The recent QNFO preprint “p‑Adic Braid Groups on Bruhat‑Tits Buildings” defines braid generators by moving a distinguished point along a path in an apartment.  However, the underlying building is a **graph** (in rank‑one) or a **simplicial complex** (in higher rank) whose elementary moves are precisely the edges between vertices.  Consequently, any continuous path can be discretised to a sequence of vertex‑to‑vertex steps without loss of topological information.  This observation raises the question posed in the title: *why should one work with arbitrary points when vertices already capture the essential combinatorics?*  

In this paper we provide a quantitative answer.  By counting vertices and edges in a finite ball of the $\mathrm{SL}_2$ Bruhat‑Tits tree we obtain an explicit upper bound on the number of elementary braid generators required to realise any braid on that ball.  The bound is expressed in elementary arithmetic, leaving no “it can be shown” gaps.  We then situate this result within the broader literature on compactifications, tropical geometry, and representation‑theoretic fixed‑point sets, showing that a vertex‑centric viewpoint aligns naturally with those developments.  

## 2. Background and Related Work  
A substantial body of work has examined the geometry of Bruhat‑Tits buildings from several complementary angles.  

1. **Tropical compactifications** – In [1] the authors relate stabilisers of points in a building to tropical linear algebra and exhibit compactifications arising from algebraic representations.  Their fan description is built from tropical Schur polynomials, which are naturally indexed by vertices of apartments.  

2. **Group‑theoretic compactifications** – The convergence theorem for sequences of parahoric subgroups proved in [2] compactifies *vertices* of the building via the Chabauty topology.  The resulting compactified space is a closure of the vertex set, not of arbitrary points.  

3. **Compression of angles** – The analogue of the compression of angles theorem for type‑$A$ buildings in [3] relies on the complex distance between *lattices*, i.e. vertices, showing that the metric geometry is already determined at the vertex level.  

4. **Fixed‑point geometry of representations** – In [4] typical representations are characterised by the geometry of their fixed‑point sets in the building.  These fixed‑point sets are unions of vertices (parahoric subgroups), reinforcing the vertex‑centric nature of representation‑theoretic phenomena.  

5. **Wonderful compactifications** – The identification of the Satake‑Berkovich compactification with the embedding into the wonderful compactification of $G$ in [5] proceeds by first embedding the *vertex* set of the building into the Berkovich analytification, again highlighting vertices as the primary carriers of geometric data.  

6. **SL‑specific tropical view** – The detailed study of $\mathrm{SL}_n$ in [6] shows that the fans used to compactify apartments are precisely the tropicalizations of Schur polynomials evaluated on vertices (lattice classes).  

7. **Locally analytic representations and sheaves** – The functor constructed in [7] sends admissible locally analytic representations to equivariant sheaves on the building.  The stalks of these sheaves are attached to vertices, and the transition maps are defined along edges.  

8. **Coefficient systems and Hecke modules** – The correspondence between pro‑$p$ Iwahori‑Hecke modules and coefficient systems on the building in [8] is mediated by vertex‑indexed modules and edge‑indexed restriction maps.  

9. **Missing branches of the Bruhat‑Tits tree** – The computation of maximal orders containing a given suborder in [9] yields a sub‑tree (a collection of vertices) called the *branch* of the order, illustrating again that algebraic data are encoded by vertex subsets.  

10. **p‑Adic braid groups** – The QNFO preprint [12] constructs discrete braid groups on Bruhat‑Tits buildings by prescribing generators that move a point along a chosen edge.  Although the language of “points” is used, the underlying combinatorial move is an edge between two vertices.  

Collectively, these works demonstrate that vertices, rather than arbitrary points, are the natural carriers of both geometric and representation‑theoretic information in Bruhat‑Tits theory.  

## 3. Methods  

### 3.1. Model building: the $\mathrm{SL}_2$ tree  
We work with the Bruhat‑Tits tree $\mathcal{T}_p$ attached to $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Q}_p)$.  Vertices correspond to homothety classes of $\mathbb{Z}_p$‑lattices in $\mathbb{Q}_p^2$.  Two vertices are joined by an edge precisely when the corresponding lattices satisfy $L\subset L'$ with index $p$.  The tree is $(p+1)$‑regular: each vertex has degree $p+1$.  

### 3.2. Finite subcomplexes  
For a fixed base vertex $v_0$ and integer radius $r\ge 0$, define the ball  
\[
B_r(v_0)=\{v\in \mathcal{T}_p\mid d(v,v_0)\le r\},
\]  
where $d$ is the graph distance.  The set $B_r(v_0)$ is finite and consists solely of vertices and the edges joining them.  

### 3.3. Counting vertices and edges  
The number of vertices $V(r)$ in $B_r(v_0)$ is a standard combinatorial quantity for regular trees.  Starting from $v_0$, each new layer adds $(p+1)p^{k-1}$ vertices at distance $k$ (for $k\ge 1$).  Hence  
\[
V(r)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{r}(p+1)p^{k-1}=1+(p+1)\frac{p^{r}-1}{p-1}.
\]  
The number of edges $E(r)$ equals $V(r)-1$, because a tree with $V$ vertices has exactly $V-1$ edges.  

### 3.4. Translating to braid generators  
In the construction of [12] each elementary braid generator corresponds to a half‑twist along an edge of the tree.  Therefore the total number of distinct generators needed to generate the braid group on $B_r(v_0)$ is bounded above by $E(r)$.  

### 3.5. Numerical instantiation  
We instantiate the formulas for the concrete parameters $p=3$ (the smallest odd prime) and $r=2$, which are small enough to be computed by hand yet large enough to exhibit non‑trivial growth.  

## 4. Analysis  

### 4.1. Input data  

| Symbol | Meaning | Source |
|--------|---------|--------|
| $p$ | Residue characteristic of the local field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ | Definition of $\mathcal{T}_p$ (standard) |
| $r$ | Radius of the ball around the base vertex | Chosen for illustration |
| $p=3$ | Specific prime for explicit computation | Chosen value |
| $r=2$ | Specific radius for explicit computation | Chosen value |
| $p+1=4$ | Degree of each vertex in $\mathcal{T}_3$ | Property of $(p+1)$‑regular tree |
| $p^{r}=3^{2}=9$ | Power of $p$ needed in the vertex count formula | Arithmetic |
| $p-1=2$ | Denominator in the geometric series | Arithmetic |

### 4.2. Vertex count $V(r)$  

The formula from §3.3 is  
\[
V(r)=1+(p+1)\frac{p^{r}-1}{p-1}.
\]  

Insert the numerical values step‑by‑step:

1. Compute $p^{r}$: $3^{2}=9$.  
2. Subtract $1$: $9-1=8$.  
3. Compute $p-1$: $3-1=2$.  
4. Form the fraction $\frac{8}{2}=4$.  
5. Multiply by $p+1$: $4\times 4=16$.  
6. Add $1$: $1+16=17$.

Thus  
\[
V(2)=17\quad\text{vertices}.
\]

### 4.3. Edge count $E(r)$  

Since a tree satisfies $E(r)=V(r)-1$, we have  

\[
E(2)=17-1=16\quad\text{edges}.
\]

### 4.4. Upper bound on braid generators  

Each edge can support at most one elementary half‑twist generator in the construction of [12].  Therefore the number $G(r)$ of generators needed satisfies  

\[
G(r)\le E(r)=16.
\]  

### 4.5. Growth with radius  

For general $r$, the vertex count simplifies to  

\[
V(r)=1+(p+1)\frac{p^{r}-1}{p-1}=1+\frac{(p+1)}{p-1}p^{r}-\frac{p+1}{p-1}.
\]  

The dominant term is proportional to $p^{r}$, so asymptotically  

\[
V(r)\sim \frac{p+1}{p-1}\,p^{r},\qquad E(r)\sim \frac{p+1}{p-1}\,p^{r}.
\]  

Consequently the generator bound grows exponentially with $r$, with base $p$.  

## 5. Results  

| Parameter | Value | Interpretation |
|-----------|-------|----------------|
| $p$ | $3$ | Prime defining the local field $\mathbb{Q}_3$ |
| $r$ | $2$ | Radius of the examined ball |
| $V(2)$ | $17$ | Total vertices in $B_2(v_0)$ |
| $E(2)$ | $16$ | Total edges in $B_2(v_0)$ |
| $G(2)$ | $\le 16$ | Upper bound on elementary braid generators needed on $B_2(v_0)$ |

The explicit arithmetic demonstrates that, for the smallest non‑trivial case, the braid group on a radius‑$2$ ball can be generated by at most sixteen elementary braids.  The exponential growth formula predicts $G(r)\approx \frac{p+1}{p-1}p^{r}$ for larger $r$, providing a concrete quantitative benchmark for any vertex‑centric implementation of $p$‑adic braid groups.  

## 6. Discussion  

### 6.1. Why vertices matter  
The calculation shows that the combinatorial complexity of a braid group on a finite subcomplex is entirely captured by the count of vertices and edges.  Since vertices encode lattice classes, any representation‑theoretic or geometric invariant that depends on a point of the building can be recovered from the vertex containing that point.  This aligns with the vertex‑focused compactifications of [1,2,5,6] and with the sheaf‑theoretic constructions of [7,8].  

### 6.2. Limitations  

1. **Rank‑one restriction** – The analysis is confined to the $\mathrm{SL}_2$ tree.  Higher‑rank buildings have apartments that are Euclidean (or affine) spaces tiled by simplices; counting vertices in a ball then involves higher‑dimensional combinatorics.  Extending the explicit arithmetic to those cases would require new formulas.  

2. **Finite radius** – The bound $G(r)\le E(r)$ is tight only when each edge yields an independent generator.  In practice relations among generators (e.g., braid relations) may reduce the minimal generating set.  Our bound is therefore an *upper* bound, not an exact count of independent generators.  

3. **Neglect of higher‑dimensional simplices** – In rank $\ge 2$, braids may act non‑trivially on 2‑simplices (triangles) and higher cells.  A vertex‑only description would need to incorporate higher‑dimensional incidence data, which is absent from the present tree model.  

4. **Choice of prime** – The explicit numbers depend on $p$.  While the asymptotic growth is universal, the constant $\frac{p+1}{p-1}$ varies, and for $p=2$ the tree is $3$‑regular, yielding a different bound.  

### 6.3. Potential falsification  

If a concrete implementation of the $p$‑adic braid group on $\mathcal{T}_p$ were to require more than $E(r)$ generators for a ball of radius $r$, that would falsify the claim that vertices alone suffice.  Conversely, discovering a relation that reduces the generating set below $E(r)$ would not invalidate the vertex‑centric viewpoint but would refine the quantitative bound.  

### 6.4. Open questions  

* **Higher rank generalisation** – Derive closed formulas for $V(r)$ and $E(r)$ in apartments of $\mathrm{SL}_n$ ($n\ge 3$) and determine the corresponding generator bounds.  
* **Relation to tropical fans** – Investigate how the vertex count interacts with the tropical fan compactifications of [1,6]; in particular, whether the number of maximal cones equals $V(r)$ for a suitable truncation.  
* **Cohomological implications** – Explore whether the vertex‑centric braid action induces non‑trivial cohomology classes in the equivariant sheaves of [7,8].  
* **Algorithmic generation** – Design efficient algorithms that, given $p$ and $r$, output an explicit generating set of braid generators based solely on the edge list of $B_r(v_0)$.  

## 7. Conclusion  

We have provided a concrete, arithmetic‑driven argument that vertices, not arbitrary points, are the natural carriers of braid‑group actions on Bruhat‑Tits buildings.  By counting vertices and edges in a finite ball of the $\mathrm{SL}_2$ tree, we derived an explicit upper bound of sixteen elementary braid generators for the case $p=3$, $r=2$, and exhibited the exponential growth law $G(r)\sim \frac{p+1}{p-1}p^{r}$.  This quantitative result dovetails with a broad spectrum of existing work that already privileges vertices in compactifications, representation theory, and tropical geometry.  While the present analysis is limited to rank‑one and finite radius, it establishes a clear methodological template for extending vertex‑centric reasoning to higher rank groups and deeper geometric structures.
