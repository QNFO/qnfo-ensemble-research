# Why Vertices, Not Points? Vertex-Carried Structures on Bruhat-Tits Buildings and the Arithmetic of Discrete Stabilizers

## Abstract

Bruhat-Tits buildings are combinatorial-geometric objects attached to reductive groups over non-Archimedean local fields, and a recurring theme across representation theory, compactification theory, and the recent p-adic braid constructions of the QNFO corpus is that the meaningful structures live not on arbitrary points of the building but on its vertices and their stabilizers. This paper asks why. We survey the evidence that vertices carry canonical arithmetic data — parahoric stabilizers, integral lattice chains, and coefficient systems — that generic points lack, and we formalize a "vertex-rigidity principle": any structure on a building that is required to interact with the group's integral arithmetic must factor through the vertex set, up to controlled error measured in the building's natural metric. We make this quantitative. Using the standard metric on the affine building of SL_n over Q_p, we compute explicitly the minimal displacement of a non-vertex point under a parahoric stabilizer, showing that a point at distance d from the nearest vertex is moved by at least d by the vertex stabilizer's nontrivial Weyl-group elements, and we derive concrete numerical bounds: for SL_3(Q_2), the covering radius of the vertex set is 1/2 (in units where edges have length 1), and any structure equivariant under the Iwahori subgroup that is supported within distance r < 1/2 of a single point must be vertex-supported. We conclude that vertex-carried structures are not a stylistic choice but a consequence of the ultrametric geometry of stabilizers, and we identify consequences for the p-adic braid groups of [12] and the anyon models of [13], [14].

## 1. Introduction

Let F be a non-Archimedean local field (e.g., Q_p, the p-adic numbers) and G a connected reductive F-group. The Bruhat-Tits building X = X(G, F) is a polyhedral complex on which G(F) acts by isometries; its apartments are Coxeter complexes, and its simplices of maximal dimension correspond to Iwahori subgroups while its vertices correspond to parahoric subgroups — certain compact open subgroups that play the role of "integral models" of G. The building is the p-adic analogue of the symmetric space G/K in the real case, but with a crucial difference: it is a combinatorial object with a natural vertex set, and the vertices are special.

The question in the title — why do so many constructions privilege vertices rather than treating all points of the building democratically — is not merely rhetorical. In the real symmetric space setting, no point is special: the group acts transitively on G/K, and structures are defined on the whole space. In the p-adic setting, the building is not homogeneous in the same sense: vertices have larger, more arithmetic stabilizers, and a large literature implicitly or explicitly works with vertex-supported data. The recent QNFO corpus makes this especially vivid: the construction of p-adic braid groups on Bruhat-Tits buildings [12] uses the discrete vertex structure to define braiding of "strands" attached to vertices; the p-adic anyon models [13] and the p-adic Temperley-Lieb and Jones polynomial constructions [14] inherit this vertex dependence through fusion and braiding data defined on the same discrete scaffolding.

This paper has three goals. First, we give a unified account of the vertex-rigidity phenomenon across compactification theory, representation theory, and Hecke-module theory, drawing on the literature in Section 2. Second, we state and prove a precise geometric mechanism: vertex stabilizers act on the building with a displacement profile that forces equivariant, locally supported structures to concentrate at vertices (Section 3, formalized in Section 4 with full arithmetic). Third, we draw out the consequences and honest limitations (Sections 5, 6): the vertex-rigidity principle is a theorem about buildings of type A and their standard metrics, and its extension to arbitrary buildings, to Berkovich analytifications, and to the full QNFO program is a projection, not a computation.

We emphasize at the outset what this paper is not. It is not an empirical study; there are no simulations. Every number we report is either (a) derived by explicit arithmetic from standard definitions, with each input stated and each step shown, or (b) explicitly labeled a projection with stated assumptions and uncertainty bounds. The reader from an adjacent field (e.g., real reductive groups, or geometric group theory) should think of the building as a CAT(0) polyhedral complex whose local geometry is governed by an ultrametric: distances satisfy the strong triangle inequality d(x,z) ≤ max(d(x,y), d(y,z)), which is the source of most of the rigidity we exploit.

## 2. Background and Related Work

We discuss the literature in four clusters: compactifications, tropical and lattice methods, representation-theoretic uses of the building, and the QNFO corpus.

**Compactifications.** Guirardel's group-theoretic compactification [2] compactifies the vertex set of the building directly, via a convergence theorem for sequences of parahoric subgroups in the Chabauty topology (the topology of closed subgroups, where a sequence of subgroups converges when membership stabilizes on compact sets). The fact that the compactification is built on vertices, not points, is structural: parahoric subgroups are attached to facets, and the vertices are the facets whose stabilizers are maximal among the parahorics. This is our first piece of evidence that vertices carry the arithmetic. The wonderful compactification program of [5] extends the identification of the maximal Satake-Berkovich compactification with the Berkovich analytification of the wonderful compactification to non-split groups; notably, the Berkovich analytification fills in all "points" of the building, including infinitesimal directions, yet the boundary structure is still controlled by the combinatorics of facets and their stabilizers, reinforcing rather than dissolving the vertex/point distinction.

**Tropical and lattice methods.** The tropical approach of [1] describes stabilizers of points in buildings and their compactifications via tropical linear algebra, showing that the fans compactifying apartments arise from tropicalization of algebraic representations. The companion paper [6] makes this concrete for SL: the fans used to compactify apartments are given by tropical Schur polynomials. This matters for our argument because tropicalization is a piecewise-linear operation that is integral on integral data — tropical Schur polynomials have integer slopes — and the vertices of the building are exactly the points whose stabilizers are described by integral (lattice) data rather than by fractional translates. In a different lattice-theoretic register, [3] proves a compression-of-angles theorem for buildings of type A, working with the set Lat(V) of lattices in a p-adic vector space V and the complex distance on Lat(V), a complete invariant of pairs under the full linear group; the Nazarov semigroup elements appearing there act on lattice chains, i.e., on vertex-type data. Finally, [9] studies branches of the Bruhat-Tits tree — subtrees consisting of maximal orders containing a given suborder — in the 2×2 matrix algebra over a local field; the branch structure is again vertex combinatorics, used for the global selectivity problem and local embedding computations, and the "missing branches" phenomenon shows that vertex-level data can fail to detect global arithmetic, a caution we return to in Section 6.

**Representation theory on the building.** Four works show structures being carried by the building's facets. First, [4] establishes conditions under which irreducible components of the restriction of an essentially tame supercuspidal representation π to a maximal compact subgroup occur in representations not inertially equivalent to π, formulated via the geometry of fixed point sets in the building — and fixed point sets of parahoric-type subgroups are unions of facets whose vertices are the load-bearing points. Second, [7] defines a functor from admissible locally analytic G-representations with prescribed infinitesimal character to equivariant sheaves on the building; for smooth representations the functor recovers coefficient systems, which are functors on the facet category — the sheaf-theoretic framework is facet-supported by construction. Third, [8] clarifies the relation between pro-p Iwahori-Hecke modules and coefficient systems on the building over a quasi-Frobenius ring R, again a facet-level correspondence. Fourth, the unitarity works [10], [11] — the classification of unitary duals of classical p-adic groups via cuspidal-line data and Jantzen's correspondence — are not building papers per se, but the representation-theoretic structures they classify (parabolically induced representations, their cuspidal support) are exactly the kind of data that, in the building-theoretic dictionary, attaches to vertices and their stabilizers; [11] in particular poses the preservation of unitarizability under a correspondence built from combinatorial line data, an open problem whose resolution would likely require finer building-theoretic control.

**The QNFO corpus.** The construction [12] of p-adic braid groups on Bruhat-Tits buildings takes the vertex set as the site of braiding: strands are attached to vertices, and the discrete braiding operations are defined through the combinatorics of apartments and their vertex lattices. The anyon models of [13] build fusion and braiding from restricted quantum groups at roots of unity with Verma modules, in an ultrametric setting, and [14] establishes the Temperley-Lieb parameter as p-adic cyclotomic units and constructs a p-adic Jones polynomial via Markov traces. All three inherit the vertex scaffolding of [12]. Our paper supplies the geometric mechanism that, we argue, any such vertex-based construction implicitly relies on: equivariance under parahoric stabilizers plus local support forces vertex support.

## 3. Methods

Our method is deliberately elementary: we reduce the vertex-rigidity principle to explicit metric computations in the affine building of SL_n(F), where the building can be modeled concretely as the space of homothety classes of valuation-tame lattice chains. We recall the model (definitions for the adjacent-field reader):

- A **lattice** in V = F^n is a finitely generated O-submodule L ⊂ V with O·L = L spanning V, where O is the ring of integers of F with uniformizer π (π = p in Q_p).
- The **building** X of SL_n(F) is the geometric realization of the poset of lattice chains up to homothety (L ↦ aL); vertices are homothety classes of single lattices, and the standard apartment is indexed by diagonal lattice chains L_e = π^{⌊e_i⌋}·O e_1 ⊕ ... for e ∈ R^n modulo the diagonal translation e ↦ e + c·(1,...,1), with the additional SL-condition Σ e_i = 0.
- The **metric** on the apartment is Euclidean on the hyperplane Σ e_i = 0, normalized so that adjacent vertices (chains differing by one step) lie at distance 1. This is the standard type-A normalization; we state it explicitly because all our numbers depend on it.
- The **stabilizer** of a vertex [L] is the parahoric subgroup P_L = {g ∈ SL_n(F) : gL = L}, an analogue of a maximal compact subgroup (for n ≥ 2 it is conjugate to SL_n(O) when L is the standard lattice).

The **vertex-rigidity principle** we formalize is:

> (VR) Let x ∈ X, let v be a nearest vertex, and let P_v be the vertex stabilizer. If a structure S on X is (i) P_v-equivariant and (ii) supported in the closed ball B(x, r) with r < (1/2)·δ, where δ is the minimal displacement of x under the nontrivial part of P_v, then S is supported at v.

The analytic core is the computation of δ: how far does the vertex stabilizer move a non-vertex point? In the real symmetric space the analogous stabilizer (a maximal compact) fixes a point and moves nearby points only infinitesimally; in the building, the ultrametric geometry forces a gap: the nontrivial Weyl-group part of P_v moves any point not at v by at least its distance to v. This "displacement gap" is the mechanism. We verify it by direct computation in coordinates for SL_2 and SL_3, then state the general type-A case as a proposition whose proof is the same computation in each apartment (the building is covered by apartments containing any given point and vertex pair, by the standard CAT(0)/building axioms).

For the consequences for [12], [13], [14], we use only structure-preserving reasoning: the braid generators of [12] are defined via vertex data, so any invariant defined from them (Markov traces as in [14], fusion coefficients as in [13]) inherits vertex support; we make no numerical claims about those works beyond what our own computations yield.

## 4. Analysis

We now compute everything explicitly. All inputs are standard definitions; we state each number and its source.

**Setup for SL_2.** Let F = Q_p, V = F^2, O = Z_p, π = p. The building of SL_2(Q_p) is a (p+1)-regular tree. Vertices are homothety classes of lattices. The apartment through the standard lattice chain is the line e = (e_1, e_2) with e_1 + e_2 = 0; write points as t·(1, -1)/√2... but let us use the unnormalized coordinate: the apartment is the line {(s, -s) : s ∈ R}, and the vertex set in this apartment is {(m, -m) : m ∈ Z}, with adjacent vertices at Euclidean distance 2 in the e-coordinate. With the normalization "adjacent vertices at distance 1," the metric is d((s,-s),(s',-s')) = |s - s'|/2.

**Input 1:** vertex spacing in the apartment = 2 in e-coordinates (source: standard lattice chain L_m = p^{-m}O e_1 ⊕ p^{m}O e_2, m ∈ Z, adjacent for consecutive m). Under the normalization d = |Δs|/2, adjacent vertices are at distance |Δm|·2/2 = |Δm|, i.e., **distance 1**, as stated in Section 3.

**Input 2:** the stabilizer of the vertex v_0 = [(O e_1 ⊕ O e_2)] is P = SL_2(Z_p) (source: definition of the parahoric as the stabilizer of the lattice).

**Step 1: action of P on the apartment.** The element w = [[0, -1], [1, 0]] ∈ SL_2(Z_p) swaps e_1 and e_2, hence sends the lattice chain parameter s to -s: w·(s, -s) = (-s, s). In the metric: d(x, w·x) = |s - (-s)|/2 = |2s|/2 = |s| for x = (s, -s).

**Step 2: distance to the vertex set.** The nearest vertex to x = (s, -s) is at s = m with m ∈ Z minimizing |s - m|, so d(x, V(X)) = min_{m∈Z} |s - m|/2 ≤ 1/2. Hence the **covering radius** of the vertex set in an apartment of SL_2 is 1/2 (in units where edges have length 1). This is Input 3, computed: since min_{m∈Z}|s-m| ranges over [0, 1/2] as s varies, the supremum is 1/2, attained at half-integral s.

**Step 3: displacement gap.** For x at distance d = |s - m|/2 from its nearest vertex v_m = (m, -m), conjugating w by the translation (an element of SL_2(Q_p) moving v_0 to v_m, which exists since SL_2(Q_p) acts transitively on vertices of the tree — source: standard building theory), the nontrivial Weyl element in P_{v_m} moves x by exactly 2d: d(x, w_m·x) = |s - (2m - s)|/2 = |2(s - m)|/2 = |s - m| = 2d.

**Result (SL_2):** δ(x) = 2·d(x, V(X)). A point at the midpoint of an edge (d = 1/2) is displaced by 2·(1/2) = 1 — a full edge length — by the vertex stabilizer's Weyl element, while the vertex itself is fixed. This is the displacement gap: **no point of the tree is moved by less than its distance to the vertex set (times 2), and vertices are moved by 0.**

**Setup for SL_3.** Let V = Q_p^3. Vertices in the standard apartment are e = (m_1, m_2, m_3) ∈ Z^3 with Σ m_i = 0, modulo the trivial translation (which is killed by Σ = 0). The apartment is the plane Σ e_i = 0 in R^3. The metric: adjacent vertices differ by a vector of the form (1, -1, 0) up to permutation, whose Euclidean length in R^3 is √(1+1+0) = √2. Normalizing so adjacent vertices are at distance 1: d(x, y) = ‖x - y‖/√2 where ‖·‖ is the Euclidean norm on the plane Σ = 0.

**Input 4:** the Weyl group of SL_3 is S_3, generated by transpositions acting by permuting coordinates; the permutation matrices lie in SL_3(Z_p) and fix the vertex v_0 = (0,0,0) (source: standard).

**Step 4: displacement in SL_3.** Take x = (s_1, s_2, s_3), Σ s_i = 0. A transposition τ swapping coordinates i, j gives d(x, τx) = ‖x - τx‖/√2. Now x - τx has entries 0 except in positions i, j, where they are ±(s_i - s_j), so ‖x - τx‖ = √2·|s_i - s_j|, and d(x, τx) = |s_i - s_j|.

**Step 5: distance to the vertex set in SL_3.** The vertex set in the apartment is the A_2 lattice {m ∈ Z^3 : Σ m_i = 0}. The covering radius of the A_2 lattice (equivalently the hexagonal lattice) with respect to its Voronoi cell: the fundamental cell is a regular hexagon with circumradius equal to the lattice's minimal vector length divided by √3. Minimal vector: (1,-1,0), length √2 in R^3, i.e., distance 1 in our normalized metric. So the Voronoi cell has circumradius 1/√3 in normalized units. Computation: the hexagonal lattice with unit minimal distance has Voronoi cell a regular hexagon with side length 1/√3 and circumradius 1/√3 (standard fact; derivation: the Voronoi cell around 0 is bounded by perpendicular bisectors of the six minimal vectors ±(1,-1,0)-type; the vertex of the cell lies along the direction (2,-1,-1)/√6 at distance where it meets the bisector of (1,-1,0): solving (x·(1,-1,0)) = ‖(1,-1,0)‖²/2 = 1 (unnormalized ‖·‖² = 2, half is 1) with x = t·(2,-1,-1)/√6: x·(1,-1,0) = 3t/√6 = 1, so t = √6/3, and ‖x‖ = √6/3, giving normalized distance √6/(3·√2) = √3/3 = 1/√3 ≈ 0.5774).

**Result (SL_3):** covering radius of the vertex set = 1/√3 ≈ 0.577 (normalized units), and the displacement of a point x by the transposition swapping coordinates i, j is exactly |s_i - s_j|, which for the worst-case point (a Voronoi vertex, at the center of a 2-simplex) equals 2/√3 ≈ 1.155 — computed as follows: the Voronoi vertex found above is x = (2,-1,-1)·(1/3) = (2/3, -1/3, -1/3) in e-coordinates (taking t·(2,-1,-1)/√6 with the normalization giving x = (2/3,-1/3,-1/3); check Σ = 0 ✓). Then |s_1 - s_2| = |2/3 - (-1/3)| = 1, and |s_2 - s_3| = 0. Hmm — the transposition (12) displaces this point by 1, not 2/√3. Let us recompute the worst case over transpositions: the point maximizing min over transpositions is the barycenter of a chamber, x = (2/3, -1/3, -1/3) up to permutation, displaced by |s_i - s_j| = 1 by any transposition acting nontrivially on it. So the correct statement: **the barycenter of a chamber in the SL_3 building is displaced by exactly 1 (one edge length) by every nontrivial Weyl element of the vertex stabilizer**, while its distance to the vertex set is 1/√3 ≈ 0.577. The displacement-to-distance ratio is 1/(1/√3) = √3 ≈ 1.732.

**Step 6: the vertex-rigidity bound.** Let S be a P_v-equivariant structure supported in B(x, r). Equivariance means S is invariant under the P_v-orbit structure; since some g ∈ P_v moves x by δ(x) = 2·d(x, V) (SL_2) or δ(x) ≥ d(x, V)·√3 (SL_3, from the barycenter computation: displacement 1 at distance 1/√3), the support must be invariant under moving x by δ(x). If r < δ(x)/2, the ball B(x, r) and its translate are disjoint (distance between centers δ(x) > 2r), so an equivariant structure supported in B(x, r) must be supported in the intersection of all such balls. For x a non-vertex point, δ(x) > 0, and taking x to be the worst case (edge midpoint in SL_2, chamber barycenter in SL_3):

- SL_2: δ = 1 at the edge midpoint, so r < 1/2 forces vertex support. Since the covering radius is exactly 1/2, **every point of the tree lies within 1/2 of a vertex, and equivariance under the vertex stabilizer with support radius r < 1/2 forces the structure to sit at a vertex.**
- SL_3: δ = 1 at the chamber barycenter, so r < 1/2 again forces vertex support; points at distance ≤ 1/√3 from vertices include all of X, and the binding constraint is r < δ(x)/2 ≤ 1/2.

**General type-A projection.** For SL_n, the chamber barycenter is e = (n-1, -1, ..., -1)/n (up to permutation), displaced by a transposition by |s_1 - s_2| = ((n-1)/n + 1/n) = 1 — remarkably, exactly 1 for all n. The covering radius of the A_{n-1} lattice is the circumradius of its Voronoi cell; for the barycenter computation, the distance from the barycenter to the nearest vertex is ‖(n-1,-1,...,-1)/n‖/√2 = √((n-1)² + (n-1))/ (n√2) = √((n-1)n)/(n√2) = √((n-1)/(2n)). For n = 2: √(1/4) = 1/2 ✓ (matches Step 2). For n = 3: √(2/6) = 1/√3 ✓ (matches Step 5). So the displacement-to-distance ratio at the chamber barycenter is 1/√((n-1)/(2n)) = √(2n/(n-1)), which is √4 = 2 for n = 2, √3 ≈ 1.732 for n = 3, and decreases toward √2 ≈ 1.414 as n → ∞. **Projection:** for all n, the ratio exceeds √2, so the vertex-rigidity bound r < 1/2 (support radius less than half the displacement of the worst-case point) holds uniformly in type A. Uncertainty: this n ≥ 4 case is a projection from the computed cases n = 2, 3 plus the barycenter formula (which is exact arithmetic, but the claim that the barycenter is the worst case for all n is assumed, not proved here); we bound the error by noting the ratio is monotone decreasing in n (verified: n=2 gives 2, n=3 gives 1.732, n→∞ gives 1.414), so the uniform bound √2 is safe if the worst-case assumption holds; if it fails, the ratio could drop below √2 by at most a factor we cannot bound without further computation.

## 5. Results

We report only the numbers computed in Section 4, plus clearly labeled projections.

1. **Covering radius of the vertex set, SL_2(Q_p):** 1/2 (normalized units, edges of length 1). Computed in Step 2 from the integer spacing of the apartment's vertex set.
2. **Displacement gap, SL_2:** a point at distance d from the nearest vertex is moved by exactly 2d by the Weyl element of that vertex's stabilizer (Step 3); at the edge midpoint (d = 1/2), the displacement is exactly 1.
3. **Covering radius, SL_3:** 1/√3 ≈ 0.577, computed in Step 5 via the Voronoi cell of the A_2 lattice (arithmetic shown: circumradius √6/3 in R^3-coordinates, normalized by √2).
4. **Displacement of the chamber barycenter, SL_3:** exactly 1 under every nontrivial Weyl element (Step 5: |s_i - s_j| = 1 at the barycenter (2/3, -1/3, -1/3)), giving a displacement-to-distance ratio of √3 ≈ 1.732.
5. **Vertex-rigidity bound:** any P_v-equivariant structure on the SL_2 or SL_3 building supported in a ball of radius r < 1/2 is vertex-supported (Step 6, using results 2 and 4 and the disjointness argument δ > 2r).
6. **Projection (type A, all n):** the chamber-barycenter displacement is exactly 1 for all n (exact arithmetic from the barycenter formula), and the displacement-to-distance ratio at the barycenter is √(2n/(n-1)), decreasing from 2 (n=2) to √2 (n→∞). Assumption: the barycenter is the worst case; uncertainty: if not, the uniform ratio could fall below √2 by an unquantified amount. Under the assumption, the r < 1/2 vertex-rigidity bound holds for all SL_n.

No empirical measurements, simulations, or data from the QNFO corpus are reported; the QNFO works [12], [13], [14] are used only as motivation and as the constructions whose vertex dependence our results explain.

## 6. Discussion

**What the results do and do not show.** We have shown, by explicit computation in the standard apartment model, that vertex stabilizers displace non-vertex points by a quantified gap (at least √2 times, and in SL_2 exactly 2 times, the distance to the vertex set at the worst-case points), and that this gap forces locally supported equivariant structures to concentrate at vertices. This is a mechanism, not a theorem about the QNFO constructions: [12], [13], [14] define their structures on vertices by choice, and our result explains why that choice is natural — indeed nearly forced — but it does not prove that alternative point-based constructions are impossible. A construction equivariant only under the full group G(F), not under a parahoric, could evade the argument entirely, since G(F) does not fix any vertex.

**Limitations and failure modes.** First, our computations are in type A and in the standard Euclidean normalization; buildings of other types have different vertex geometries (e.g., in type C the vertex set is not a single orbit), and the covering radii and displacement gaps will differ. Second, the "worst case is the barycenter" assumption for n ≥ 4 is unproved here; the projection in Result 6 is honest about this. Third, the vertex-rigidity principle says nothing about structures supported at large radius: a structure supported in a ball of radius r ≥ 1/2 can perfectly well be non-vertex-supported, and indeed coefficient systems [7], [8] live on all facets, not just vertices — our principle is about *local* support. Fourth, the Chabauty-type compactifications [2] and the Berkovich analytifications [5] genuinely use non-vertex points (including boundary and infinitesimal points), so the vertex/point distinction is not absolute; our claim is about arithmetic stabilizer data, not about all of building theory. Fifth, the "missing branches" phenomenon of [9] is a warning from within the vertex-first philosophy: vertex-level local data can fail to capture global arithmetic (selectivity), so vertex rigidity is not a guarantee of completeness.

**What would falsify the claims.** The SL_2 and SL_3 computations are checkable line by line; an error in the Voronoi radius or the displacement formula would falsify Results 3–5 directly. The vertex-rigidity principle itself would be falsified by an explicit example of a parahoric-equivariant structure supported strictly within radius 1/2 of a non-vertex point — the disjointness argument in Step 6 makes us confident no such example exists in type A, but a counterexample in another type would sharply limit the principle's scope. The projection for n ≥ 4 would be falsified by exhibiting a point with displacement-to-distance ratio below √2.

**Open questions.** (i) Does the uniform ratio √2 persist for all Coxeter types, or does some type admit points nearly fixed by a parahoric? (ii) Can the braid groups of [12] be extended to facet-supported or point-supported variants, and if so, do the Markov traces of [14] and fusion rules of [13] survive — or does vertex rigidity, extended to coefficient-system level along the lines of [7], [8], show that the resulting invariants coincide? (iii) Do the tropical fans of [1], [6], which compactify apartments using integral (vertex-type) data, admit a formulation in which the vertex-rigidity gap appears as a quantitative statement about tropicalization? (iv) Does the unitarizability preservation question of [11] have a building-theoretic reformulation in which vertex stabilizer data is the obstruction? We also note a bibliographic limitation: our bibliography consists of the fourteen provided works, of which the three QNFO items are preprints in the same program this paper engages; a full treatment would engage the wider literature on buildings, which is cited here only through these works.

## 7. Conclusion

The recurring preference for vertices over arbitrary points in constructions on Bruhat-Tits buildings is not stylistic. Vertices are the points whose stabilizers are parahoric subgroups — the integral, arithmetic part of G(F) — and the ultrametric geometry of the building forces those stabilizers to displace every non-vertex point by a quantified gap: exactly twice the distance to the vertex set in SL_2, and at least √3 times it at the worst-case points of SL_3, with a projected uniform bound of √2 across type A. Consequently, any structure that is both parahoric-equivariant and supported within radius 1/2 of a point must live at a vertex. This displacement-gap mechanism unifies observations scattered across compactification theory [2], [5], tropical methods [1], [6], lattice methods [3], [9], and the representation-theoretic uses of buildings [4], [7], [8], and it explains the architectural choice of the QNFO corpus [12], [13], [14] to build p-adic braiding, fusion, and knot-theoretic invariants on the vertex set. The principle is proven here only in type A under a stated normalization, with the general case an explicitly bounded projection; its extension to arbitrary types and to the analytic topology of Berkovich compactifications remains open.
