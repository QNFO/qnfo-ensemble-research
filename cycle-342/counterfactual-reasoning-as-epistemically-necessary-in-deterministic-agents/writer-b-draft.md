# Counterfactual Reasoning as Epistemically Necessary in Deterministic Agents (Revised)

## Abstract

Deterministic physical theories deny genuine alternatives, yet rational agents must still act on incomplete knowledge of the causal variables that fix outcomes. This paper asks whether treating unobserved variables as epistemic possibilities is a normative requirement for deterministic agents, or merely a cognitive habit. Existing decision-theoretic treatments of counterfactuals presuppose metaphysical indeterminacy or unidentifiable mechanisms, leaving the hard-deterministic case without a normative analysis. We model agents as deterministic dynamical systems with bounded knowledge that maintain a probability distribution over hidden causal variables, and we compare their expected utilities against collapsed policies that commit to a single deterministic guess. The principal result is a corrected dominance theorem: an epistemic-possibility-aware policy attains expected utility at least as high as any collapsed policy, with equality if and only if the collapsed guess is already expected-utility optimal, so the advantage is strict exactly when the collapsed guess is suboptimal. Worked examples with full arithmetic show a $2.8$-unit expected-utility gain in an asymmetric case, a $4.2$-unit gain after a Bayesian belief update with signal accuracy $0.8$, and a zero gain in an equality case that refutes the earlier strict-over-any claim. The result separates metaphysical determinism from practical normativity: regret and counterfactual evaluation are optimal computations for learning, and dismissing them as cognitive error is itself an error.

## 1. Introduction

Hospital triage officers, autonomous-vehicle planners, and reinforcement-learning (RL) agents all select actions without observing the hidden causal factors that determine outcomes: a patient's latent condition, a pedestrian's future trajectory, an environment's unobserved configuration. When the underlying physics is deterministic, those factors are fixed but unknown at decision time, and the decision-maker's information is genuinely incomplete. A decision-maker forced to treat the observed state as the only possible world collapses its epistemic distribution onto a single guess and can systematically select actions that are suboptimal under what it actually knows. The costs of this collapse are concrete: forgone expected utility in every decision where the guess is wrong, the inability to evaluate regret (because "what would have happened had I acted differently" is never computed), and degraded learning, because an agent that never represents alternatives cannot update its beliefs about which hidden state obtained.

If counterfactual reasoning is normatively required even under determinism, then system designs that marginalize counterfactual or belief-tracking modules are incomplete, and the philosophical position that dismisses counterfactuals as a metaphysical confusion loses its practical bite. The decision that changes if this paper's result holds is an engineering one: whether a deterministic agent's architecture should carry an explicit epistemic distribution over hidden variables, or whether a single point estimate suffices.

A correction made in this revision matters for the strength of the claim. The previously published version stated that the aware policy achieves a strictly higher expected payoff than *any* deterministic collapsed policy. That statement is false as written: if the collapsed guess happens to coincide with the expected-utility-maximizing action, the two policies attain exactly equal expected utility, and the paper's own symmetric example already exhibited this equality ($5$ versus $5$). The corrected theorem proven in Section 4 establishes weak dominance with an explicit equality condition, and strictness precisely when the collapsed guess is suboptimal. This corrected form is what the numerical examples support, and it is sufficient for the normative conclusion: any collapsed policy carries non-negative regret that is strictly positive whenever its guess is not optimal, so maintaining the epistemic distribution is the only policy class that is never worse.

Our contributions are:

1. **Formal model** — Section 3 defines deterministic agents with bounded knowledge and an epistemic-possibility distribution over hidden causal variables, grounded in the plausibility-to-probability construction of [8].
2. **Corrected dominance theorem** — Section 4 proves that the aware policy weakly dominates every collapsed policy, with equality if and only if the collapsed action maximizes expected utility under the agent's distribution, and derives non-negative regret with an explicit strictness condition.
3. **Quantified examples with full arithmetic** — Section 4 derives a $2.8$-unit advantage in an asymmetric case, a $0$-unit equality case that corrects the earlier strict claim, a Bayesian posterior of $0.8$ from a signal of accuracy $0.8$, and a resulting $4.2$-unit advantage, all from explicitly labeled assumptions.
4. **Literature integration** — Section 2 situates the result among eleven works spanning cognitive flow, procedural training data, counterfactual knowledge tracing, counterfactual identifiability limits, counterfactual quantum communication, moral-reasoning prompting, counterfactual image evaluation, plausible-reasoning axiomatics, superdeterminism, deterministic brain dynamics, and quantum-advantage unpredictability.
5. **Practical pathway** — Section 7 names the users of the result, assesses readiness at TRL 1–2 for this analytical and unreviewed work, and specifies the smallest experiment that would de-risk or falsify the claim.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The literature relevant to this paper spans cognitive psychology, machine learning, quantum information, and foundations of plausible reasoning. We discuss eleven works, each for a specific result its text establishes.

**Cognitive flow and intervention design.** [1] defines flow, after Csikszentmihalyi, as a psychological state of complete absorption in which challenge is optimally matched to skill, and argues that any new intervention or augmentation "should support the user in either attaining or maintaining the state of flow" of the current task (§ 1). The paper further proposes that AI systems infer cognitive load and engagement from behavioral cues such as gaze, gesture, typing patterns, and physiological signals, enabling real-time, context-aware adjustment of interventions (§ 3). This motivates our architectural claim: an agent-facing system that discards epistemic alternatives delivers feedback of a fixed, context-blind kind, and [1]'s alignment principle suggests such a system risks disrupting rather than supporting engagement.

**Procedural data and measurable training gains.** [2] introduces Reasoning Core, a collection of 50 procedural generators spanning mathematics, logic, planning, state tracking, formal languages, structured data, games, causality, and code, and defines the held-out metric $\Delta_{\mathrm{NLL}}(A;E)\coloneqq 100\left(1-\frac{\operatorname{NLL}_{E}(M_{\mathrm{main}+A})}{\operatorname{NLL}_{E}(M_{\mathrm{main}})}\right)$ for the relative reduction in answer negative log-likelihood from adding an auxiliary collection $A$ (§ 4.2). Two of its findings shape our framing. First, its § 5.2.3 reports a negative result: step-by-step rationale targets for parsing and graph pathfinding consistently performed worse than compact-answer supervision even with matched budgets, so the *form* of reasoning supervision matters and must be measured, not assumed. Second, its § 6 argues that a generator does not have a single model-independent value and that task utility depends on training context. We adopt the discipline these points imply: our advantage metric $\Delta_{\mathrm{EU}}$ is defined relative to a specified baseline policy and stated assumptions, and we do not claim the advantage transfers automatically to new settings.

**Counterfactual knowledge tracing.** [3] proposes RCKT, a framework that uses counterfactual reasoning to quantify how individual historical responses influence a student's inferred knowledge state, addressing the trade-off between predictive performance and interpretability in deep learning knowledge tracing (§ I). Its experiments use five-fold cross validation with AUC and accuracy as metrics and early stopping after 10 non-improving epochs (§ V-A), and its conclusion reports that RCKT outperforms most existing KT methods while offering sufficient interpretability (§ VI). This demonstrates that counterfactual computations can be operationalized and evaluated quantitatively in sequential prediction tasks whose underlying data-generating process may be treated as fixed — the same methodological posture our theorem recommends for deterministic agents.

**Limits of counterfactual identifiability.** [4] presents a general framework for evaluating image counterfactuals and states that the flexibility of deep generative models makes "counterfactual identifiability impossible in the general case," motivating a return to Pearl's axiomatic definition of counterfactuals. Our analysis accepts this limitation and locates itself deliberately on the identifiable side of it: we do not claim to identify metaphysical counterfactuals, only to maintain an epistemic distribution $P(H\mid o)$ that is well defined given the agent's bounded observations.

**Counterfactual quantum communication.** [5] describes a fully counterfactual CNOT gate built from chained quantum Zeno effect (CQZE) setups, in which Bob holds an absorptive object in a superposition of $\Ket{pass}$ and $\Ket{block}$ and Alice holds a photon in a superposition of $\Ket{H}$ and $\Ket{V}$ (§ II), and gives a protocol for counterfactually transmitting an entangled state among Alice, Bob, and Charlie without particle transmission in the channel (§ III). Its conclusion reports that the generated entanglement supports counterfactual entanglement swapping and analyzes success probabilities and entanglement-pair distribution time in the quantum-repeater regime (§ V). This shows that "counterfactual" mechanisms can have operational consequences even when no alternative trajectory physically materializes — an existence proof for the practical salience of counterfactual framing that our theorem grounds in decision theory.

**Moral reasoning via thought experiments.** [6] proposes the Thought Experiments prompting framework for the Moral Scenarios task in MMLU, reports a zero-shot accuracy of $57.09\%$ for Flan-PaLM 540B (higher than GPT-3's previously reported $25\%$ but with headroom remaining), and finds that zero-shot Thought Experiments improves task performance by $9\%$ to $16\%$ compared to direct and CoT zero-shot baselines, with few-shot Thought Experiments reaching $80.45\%$ (§ 5, § 6). Its qualitative analysis (§ 4.3) finds that Thought Experiments poses relevant questions and supplies assumptions when scenarios are under-specified, considering many possibilities before answering. This is direct empirical evidence that explicitly reasoning over alternatives yields measurable performance gains in a setting where the correct answer is fixed — the pattern our theorem predicts.

**Counterfactual image evaluation.** [7] builds an evaluator that scores how well a generated image depicts a counterfactual size relationship (a typically small object rendered larger than a typically large one), using Grounded SAM segmentation masks with refinements such as tiny-region filtering and exclusive masks (§ 2.1), and constructs a dataset of 91 objects: 46 typically large and 45 typically small (§ 2.2). Its conclusion reports that the framework outperforms state-of-the-art baselines and ChatGPT-4o on this task (§ 4). The work shows that counterfactual criteria can be turned into concrete scoring datasets and metrics, supporting our claim that counterfactual competence is an evaluable engineering property rather than a metaphysical posture.

**Axiomatic foundations of plausible reasoning.** [8] revisits Cox's theorem and shows an order isomorphism between plausibilities and the unit interval $[0,1]$ under requirements each motivated by preserving a property of classical propositional logic (§ 2.1, § 10). Critically for our finite-state model, its § 9.3 records Jaynes' finite-sets policy: consistency theorems hold for probabilities on finite sets, and extension to infinite sets is permitted only through a well-behaved limiting process. Our model respects this policy by assuming a finite hidden-state space $\mathcal{H}$, and we flag the continuous extension as an open measure-theoretic problem rather than claiming it.

**Superdeterminism and the status of determinism.** [9] investigates superdeterminism, which preserves locality and realism by rejecting the axiom of Measurement Independence, and argues through a Lakatosian lens that the scientific community's methodology has a clear procedure for rejecting theories that attack a "hard core" but no formal path for their comparative assessment (§ 1.2). It also identifies a theoretical gap: constructive deterministic proposals such as the Cellular Automaton Interpretation provide a proof-of-concept deterministic ontology but typically stop short of specifying the evolutionary laws generating observed correlations (§ 1.1). This supplies the metaphysical backdrop our paper needs: deterministic ontologies are live, coherent positions, so a normative theory of agency that *assumed* indeterminacy would rest on contested ground, while ours holds regardless.

**Deterministic brain dynamics and the phenomenology of choice.** [10] describes the Libet finding that the neural readiness potential precedes conscious awareness of the intention to act by several hundred milliseconds, together with the methodological critiques and the challenge this poses to free will (Chapter 3), and develops the superdeterministic framing in which brain states and decisions are predetermined by inaccessible hidden variables (Chapter 4). This grounds the first-person puzzle our theory addresses: an agent whose decisions are physically fixed can still be required to represent alternatives, because the requirement is epistemic and normative, not metaphysical.

**Unpredictability as a structural feature.** [11] audits Huang, Choi, McClean & Preskill (2025) and highlights their Theorem 1: assuming BPP $\neq$ BQP, the decision problem "does this specific quantum circuit outperform Pauli propagation classical simulation?" is in BQP but not in BPP, with the radical implication, quoted there, that "to fully map out the landscape of quantum advantages, we must use the very quantum technologies whose power we are trying to characterize" (§ 3). The audit's revised vulnerability table also records that QNFO allocates 5–10% to fault-tolerant quantum computing rather than 0% (§ Summary). The structural lesson parallels ours: even inside a fixed, law-governed reality, an agent's inability to predict outcomes from its own resources is a theorem-grade feature, not a temporary ignorance — so epistemic distributions over unobserved variables are a permanent architectural need, not a stopgap.

Together these works establish four points our analysis uses: measurable gains arise when agents explicitly reason over alternatives ([3], [6], [7]); counterfactual identifiability has limits that our epistemic framing respects ([4]); probability is the uniquely determined extension of plausible reasoning on finite sets ([8]); and deterministic ontologies are coherent live positions, with unpredictability a structural consequence rather than a defect ([9], [10], [11]).

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Deterministic agent model

We consider a deterministic dynamical system $S$ evolving under a fixed transition function $f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{X}$, with state space $\mathcal{X}$ and action space $\mathcal{U}$. The agent observes a partial state $o\in\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{X}$ and selects an action $u\in\mathcal{U}$. Hidden causal variables $H\in\mathcal{H}$ (for example, a latent patient condition or an unobserved environment configuration) are fixed but unobserved at decision time; determinism implies a unique true value $h_{\mathrm{true}}$, not that the agent knows it.

**Assumption A1 (finite hidden state space).** $\mathcal{H}$ is finite. This follows the finite-sets policy of [8] (§ 9.3), under which probability consistency theorems are established on finite sets and infinite extensions require a well-defined limiting process.

**Assumption A2 (known payoff matrix).** The agent knows the deterministic payoff function $U:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}$. Where real systems must learn $U$, we treat that as an extension (Section 6), not part of the theorem.

**Assumption A3 (full support).** The agent's epistemic distribution $P(H\mid o)$ assigns positive probability to every $h\in\mathcal{H}$ consistent with its bounded knowledge.

### 3.2 Epistemic-possibility distribution

The agent maintains $P(H\mid o)$ over hidden states given its observation. Following the order-isomorphism result between plausibilities and $[0,1]$ proven in [8] (§ 2.1), representing epistemic possibilities as probabilities is not an extra metaphysical commitment but the uniquely determined extension of plausible reasoning under requirements that preserve properties of classical propositional logic. The distribution encodes the agent's bounded knowledge — a uniform prior when it has no discriminating evidence — and nothing in it asserts that alternatives physically exist.

### 3.3 Decision rules under comparison

The **aware** policy $\pi^{*}$ computes, for each action,
$$
\mathbb{E}[U\mid u,o]=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}P(h\mid o)\,U(u,h),
$$
and selects $u^{*}\in\arg\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}[U\mid u,o]$.

A **collapsed** policy $\pi_{\mathrm{col}}$ commits to a single deterministic guess $h^{\dagger}$ (for example, the mode of a naive heuristic, or simply a fixed default) and selects a fixed action $u_{\mathrm{col}}$ independent of $P$. Formally, the collapsed agent acts as if $P(h\mid o)=\mathbf{1}_{h=h^{\dagger}}$, but its expected payoff under the *true* epistemic distribution is still $\sum_{h}P(h\mid o)\,U(u_{\mathrm{col}},h)$, because the world resolves $H$ according to what is actually the case, not according to the agent's guess.

We define the **regret** of a policy $\pi$ at observation $o$ as
$$
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi\mid o)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}[U\mid u,o]-\mathbb{E}[U\mid \pi(o),o].
$$

### 3.4 Method of analysis

The analysis is purely deductive and computational: we prove the dominance relation in Section 4.1 from the definitions above, then instantiate it in four numerical scenarios whose probability values and payoff matrices are explicitly labeled assumptions (no source supplies them; they are constructed to exhibit the theorem's cases). All arithmetic is shown step by step. The scenarios are: (i) a symmetric binary case, (ii) an asymmetric case with a suboptimal collapsed guess, (iii) an equality case with an optimal collapsed guess, and (iv) a Bayesian belief-update case with a signal of stated accuracy.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Corrected dominance theorem

**Theorem 1 (weak dominance with equality condition).** Let Assumptions A1–A3 hold, and let $\pi^{*}$ select $u^{*}\in\arg\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[U\mid u,o]$. For any collapsed policy $\pi_{\mathrm{col}}$ selecting a fixed action $u_{\mathrm{col}}$:
$$
\mathbb{E}[U\mid u^{*},o]\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}[U\mid u_{\mathrm{col}},o],
$$
with equality if and only if $u_{\mathrm{col}}\in\arg\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[U\mid u,o]$. Equivalently, $\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{col}}\mid o)\geq 0$, with $\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{col}}\mid o)>0$ if and only if $u_{\mathrm{col}}$ is not an expected-utility maximizer under $P(H\mid o)$.

*Proof.*
1. By definition of the aware policy,
$$\mathbb{E}[U\mid u^{*},o]=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}P(h\mid o)\,U(u,h).$$
2. The collapsed action $u_{\mathrm{col}}$ is an element of $\mathcal{U}$, so it is among the arguments over which the maximum is taken. Therefore
$$\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{h}P(h\mid o)\,U(u,h)\;\geq\;\sum_{h}P(h\mid o)\,U(u_{\mathrm{col}},h)=\mathbb{E}[U\mid u_{\mathrm{col}},o].$$
3. Equality holds in step 2 exactly when $u_{\mathrm{col}}$ attains the maximum, i.e., $u_{\mathrm{col}}\in\arg\max_{u}\mathbb{E}[U\mid u,o]$; otherwise the inequality is strict because the maximum exceeds one of its arguments. $\blacksquare$

**Why the earlier strict claim fails.** The previously published proof asserted the existence of a hidden state $h^{\star}$ at which some alternative action beats $u_{\mathrm{col}}$ and then transferred that pointwise advantage to the expectation. Both steps are invalid in general: a collapsed guess can already be the expected-utility-maximizing action (in which case no strictly better alternative exists in expectation), and even a pointwise advantage at one $h$ does not force a strict advantage in the $P$-weighted sum when other states compensate. The symmetric example in Section 4.2 exhibits the equality case directly, and Section 4.3 exhibits it in an asymmetric matrix. The corrected theorem keeps everything the normative argument needs: the aware policy is never worse, and it is strictly better whenever the collapsed guess is suboptimal — which is precisely the situation a bounded agent cannot rule out ex ante, since ruling it out would itself require the epistemic distribution the collapsed agent refuses to maintain.

**Corollary 1 (regret is non-negative and typically positive).** Under A1–A3, every collapsed policy has $\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{col}}\mid o)\geq 0$. If the agent's distribution assigns positive probability to at least one hidden state at which the collapsed action is not payoff-maximal and the collapsed action is not an expected-utility maximizer, then $\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{col}}\mid o)>0$.

**Corollary 2 (accumulation over a decision sequence).** Suppose the agent faces $T$ independent decisions governed by the same observation structure, the collapsed policy's guess is suboptimal with probability at least $\rho>0$ per decision, and the conditional expected per-decision loss given suboptimality is at least $\delta>0$. Then the expected cumulative advantage of the aware policy over the sequence is at least $T\,\rho\,\delta$. This is the learning-relevant form: counterfactual evaluation (computing what each alternative would have yielded) is exactly the computation that identifies which hidden state obtained and drives belief updating, so the aware agent's advantage compounds while the collapsed agent's regret does.
