# Hindsight Hierarchies as a Supervision-Yield Problem: A Formal Commentary and Quantitative Blueprint for the Self-Improvement Loop of arXiv:2610.12168

## Abstract

Formal mathematics automation is blocked by a data problem: proofs that exceed a model's current ability produce no training signal, because an unsuccessful attempt yields neither a solution nor a usable intermediate target. The hindsight-hierarchies proposal of arXiv:2610.12168 addresses this by jointly training one model to predict solution ideas from problems alone, to reverse-engineer ideas from problems with known solutions, and to solve problems when ideas are supplied, iterating the loop [1], [2]. The proposal ships with a formal specification and a Lean instantiation but no quantitative account of how much supervision the loop actually produces per unit of externally supplied solution. This paper supplies that account. We formalize the loop as a supervision-yield process with an amplification factor $A = k \, d \, h$ (ideas extracted per solved problem, deduplication retention, and training heads per idea) and derive, under stated assumptions, that a corpus of $N = 10^5$ problems with a machine-checked-solution fraction $p_s = 0.3$ yields $9 \times 10^4$ idea-conditioned supervision examples per loop iteration, an amplification of $A = 3$ examples per verified solution, and a compound growth of the solved-problem pool to approximately $4.887 \times 10^4$ after ten iterations at a per-iteration relative gain of $\Delta = 0.05$. We situate the loop against hindsight reinforcement learning [3], cognitive-flow-aware tutoring [4], hierarchical reasoning with exceptions [5], interpretability surveys [6], hindsight model predictive control [7], transfer learning for conditional generative models [8], and procedural symbolic data generation [9]. The result converts the proposal from a qualitative recipe into a falsifiable yield model whose break-even condition can be measured on a single Lean corpus.

## 1. Introduction

Teams building automated theorem proving and formal verification tooling face a persistent bottleneck: the problems worth solving are, by selection, the ones their models cannot yet solve. In interactive theorem proving, a failed proof attempt produces a proof state, a set of errors, and no supervision target; the expensive artifact — a complete, machine-checked solution — exists only for problems already within reach or for problems a human expert has closed by hand. The cost is concrete. Every training iteration on self-generated data is capped by the fraction of the corpus the model can close unaided, and scaling compute does not manufacture solutions to problems beyond the current frontier. What would change if a method could convert externally supplied solutions into several times their count in usable training signal: the frontier of automatically provable statements would advance at a rate set by the supply of human-written proofs rather than by the model's current solve rate, and formalization pipelines in mathematics and safety-critical software verification would gain a training loop whose input is an artifact they already produce and archive.

The hindsight-hierarchies proposal [1], [2] is a direct attack on this bottleneck. Its central observation is that even when a problem exceeds the model's solving ability, a supplied solution enables the model to extract useful solution ideas in hindsight. The method jointly trains the same model on three capabilities: predicting solution ideas from problems alone, reverse-engineering ideas from problems with known solutions, and solving problems using provided ideas. The loop alternates between reverse-engineering ideas from problems with supplied solutions and using those ideas as additional supervision for joint training of all three capabilities. The authors give a formal specification and a concrete instantiation for the Lean theorem prover, and state explicitly that empirical evaluation remains future work.

What the proposal does not yet contain is a quantitative answer to the question a training-pipeline engineer must ask before adopting it: how much supervision does one loop iteration produce, per unit of the scarce resource — the verified solution? Without that number, the loop cannot be compared against alternative uses of the same verified solutions (direct fine-tuning on solutions, for example), and there is no stated condition under which the loop is worth running at all. This paper supplies the missing account. We treat the loop as a supervision-yield process, state its parameters, derive its per-iteration output and its multi-iteration growth under explicit assumptions, and state the measurement that would confirm or refute the model.

The contributions are:

- A parameterization of the hindsight-hierarchies loop as a supervision-yield process with an amplification factor $A = k \, d \, h$, derived and computed in Section 4.
- A worked numerical projection for a Lean-style corpus: $9 \times 10^4$ idea-conditioned supervision examples per iteration and $A = 3$ per verified solution, derived in Section 4 and reported in Section 5.
- A compound-growth projection of the solved-problem pool over ten loop iterations, $N_{\text{solved},10} \approx 4.887 \times 10^4$ from an initial $3 \times 10^4$, derived in Section 4.
- A positioning of the loop within the hindsight-learning lineage — hierarchical reinforcement learning with hindsight [3], hindsight model predictive control [7] — and against adjacent data-generation and tutoring approaches [4], [5], [6], [8], [9], in Section 2.
- A falsification criterion and a smallest-next-experiment design for the yield model, in Sections 6 and 7.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The direct object of this commentary is the hindsight-hierarchies paper itself [1], [2]. Its supplied abstract states the observation, the three jointly trained capabilities, the alternating loop, the formal specification, and the Lean instantiation, and states that empirical evaluation remains future work. Entries [1] and [2] describe the same work — [1] is the arXiv query record and [2] the paper entry — and we cite both where the grounding distinguishes them, treating their content as identical because their supplied text is identical. The present paper takes the specification at face value and asks the question the specification leaves open: the size of the supervision stream the loop generates.

The loop's intellectual ancestry lies in hindsight-based learning from unsuccessful or repurposed experience. Hierarchical reinforcement learning with hindsight [3] addresses poor sample efficiency of reinforcement learning when rewards are delayed and sparse; its approach combines universal value functions with hindsight learning so that agents learn temporally extended actions at multiple levels of abstraction in a sample-efficient and automated fashion, learning policies belonging to different levels of a hierarchy. The structural parallel is exact: both methods convert experience that was not a success at the original task into supervision at a different level of abstraction — subgoals or ideas in place of terminal rewards. The supplied summary of [3] does not report quantitative results, so we draw only the structural correspondence and no performance comparison.

A second control-theoretic antecedent is episodic improvement of model predictive control by learning from the hindsight plan [7]. Model predictive control re-plans at every time step under a limited horizon, constrained by computation and real-time requirements and often for robustness to model errors, and the limited horizon leads to suboptimal performance; the work considers learning from the hindsight plan — the plan that would have been optimal with full foresight — to improve episodic performance. Again the mechanism matches: an artifact computed after the fact, under information the agent did not have while acting, becomes the training target. The hindsight-hierarchies loop applies the same inversion to symbolic reasoning, where the after-the-fact artifact is the human- or tool-produced solution and the extracted target is the solution idea.

On the data-supply side, the loop competes with and complements procedural generation. Reasoning Core [9] is a scalable suite that procedurally generates verifiable symbolic reasoning data for pre-training and post-training, motivated by the observation that training on verifiable symbolic data is a promising way to expand the reasoning frontier beyond standard pre-training corpora, while existing procedural generators rely on fixed puzzles or templates and do not deliver the distributional breadth needed at scale. The two approaches differ in where their supervision originates: procedural generation manufactures problems and verifiable answers from templates, whereas the hindsight loop harvests supervision from solutions that already exist for hard problems. The yield model of Section 4 is the instrument that makes the comparison quantitative in principle, since both approaches can be scored in supervision examples per unit of generation or verification effort.

Transfer learning with pre-trained conditional generative models [8] is relevant to the loop's assumption that one model can carry three capabilities at once. The supplied summary states that current transfer learning methods always assume at least one of three conditions: source and target task label spaces overlap, source datasets are available, or target network architectures are consistent with source ones, and that holding these assumptions is difficult in practical settings because the target task raises difficulties that the summary text truncates before specifying. The three heads of the hindsight loop share one architecture and one parameter set by construction, which satisfies the architecture-consistency condition; whether the three label spaces — ideas, idea-problems pairs, idea-conditioned solutions — overlap enough for positive transfer is an empirical question the loop's joint training is designed to answer but which no supplied entry resolves.

The interpretability question — why joint training of three heads on one model should reinforce rather than interfere — connects to the survey volume [6], in which six leading deep-learning researchers of the Intel Collaborative Research Institute for Computational Intelligence address the challenge of why and when deep learning works, with the goal of looking inside deep learning and providing insights on how deep networks function. The supplied entry describes the volume's provenance and aim and gives no specific findings, so we use it only to mark the interpretability question as an open, institutionally recognized one, not to claim any result about multi-task interference.

Two further entries bound the loop's scope. The appendix to reasoning with justifiable exceptions in contextual hierarchies [5] provides further details on the language, the complexity results, and the datalog translation introduced in the main paper by Bozzato, Serafini and Eiter (2018); its supplied text gives no further substantive detail, and we relate it to our argument only through what is stated: that contextual hierarchies with justifiable exceptions have a formal language and complexity theory, a lineage relevant to any claim that "hierarchies" of reasoning levels carry formal meaning. Separately, the cognitive-flow framework [4] proposes a context-aware cognitive augmentation framework that adapts interventions based on context, grounded in flow theory's observation that individuals experience deep focus and intrinsic motivation when a task's difficulty aligns with their skill level, and that interventions disrupting cognitive flow can hinder rather than enhance decision-making. This bears on the loop's human-facing deployment: if the loop is used to select which problems and hints to show a human mathematician, supplying ideas for problems far above the user's skill level is precisely the kind of difficulty-skill mismatch the flow literature warns against. The supplied summary of [4] reports no empirical measurements, so this remains a design caution rather than a quantified constraint.

Finally, the QNFO corpus entries frame the broader research program in which this commentary sits. Syntactic generation [10], impact of cognitive linearity on epistemic modeling [11], and projective geometric frameworks for semantic structures [12] are supplied as corpus context without abstract text; we note their presence as program-level context and draw no specific claims from them. Structured cross-domain translation as a testable consilience mechanism [13] formalizes a conjecture that systematically translating a single formal object into multiple domain lexicons is a reproducible research procedure rather than an ad hoc rhetorical act, with protocol design, truth-preservation conditions, and a computational evaluation blueprint. The connection to the present work is methodological: the hindsight loop's "solution idea" is itself a translation of a concrete solution into a reusable intermediate representation, and [13]'s framing of translation-as-protocol supports treating idea extraction as a formalizable, checkable operation rather than a free-text summary.

## 3. Methods

We adopt the loop of [1], [2] as given and model its supervision output. No new training is performed and no empirical data are collected; the method of this paper is explicit analytical modeling with all inputs stated as assumptions and all arithmetic shown.

**Notation.** Let $N$ denote the number of problems in a training corpus. Let $p_s$ denote the fraction of problems for which a machine-checked solution exists in the corpus (in the Lean instantiation of [1], [2], a solution is a proof term accepted by the kernel). Let $k$ denote the expected number of distinct solution ideas extractable in hindsight from one solved problem. Let $d$ denote the deduplication retention factor: the fraction of extracted ideas that survive near-duplicate removal across the corpus. Let $h$ denote the number of training heads, fixed at $h = 3$ by the method of [1], [2] (idea prediction, idea reverse-engineering, idea-conditioned solving). Let $\Delta$ denote the per-iteration relative increase in the number of problems the model can solve unaided, which under the loop becomes the per-iteration relative increase in problems for which solutions can be produced and archived.

**Yield model.** One loop iteration processes the corpus, reverse-engineers ideas from every problem with a supplied solution, deduplicates them, and converts each surviving idea into supervision examples for each of the three heads. The per-iteration supervision yield is

$$S = N \, p_s \, k \, d \, h.$$

The amplification factor — supervision examples produced per machine-checked solution consumed — is

$$A = \frac{S}{N \, p_s} = k \, d \, h.$$

**Growth model.** If each iteration raises the count of solvable problems by a relative factor $(1 + \Delta)$, and solutions for newly solvable problems are produced and fed back into the corpus, the solved-problem pool after $T$ iterations is

$$N_{\text{solved},T} = N \, p_s \, (1 + \Delta)^{T}.$$

**Assumption register.** Every input below is an assumption of this model, not a measurement: $N = 10^5$ (a corpus of one hundred thousand formalized problem statements, of the order of a large Lean-style library corpus); $p_s = 0.3$ (thirty percent of statements carry a machine-checked solution, either human-written or previously machine-produced); $k = 2$ (two distinct extractable ideas per solved problem on average); $d = 0.5$ (half of extracted ideas survive deduplication); $h = 3$ (fixed by [1], [2]); $\Delta = 0.05$ (five percent relative growth of the solvable set per iteration). Section 6 treats sensitivity to each assumption; Section 7 states the measurement that would replace each with a datum.

## 4. Analysis

This section derives every number with its arithmetic shown. All inputs come from the assumption register of Section 3 and are labeled as projections.

**Derivation 1: per-iteration supervision yield.** With $N = 10^5$, $p_s = 0.3$, $k = 2$, $d = 0.5$, $h = 3$:

$$S = N \, p_s \, k \, d \, h = 10^5 \times 0.3 \times 2 \times 0.5 \times 3.$$

Step by step: $10^5 \times 0.3 = 3 \times 10^4$ problems with machine-checked solutions. $3 \times 10^4 \times 2 = 6 \times 10^4$ ideas extracted before deduplication. $6 \times 10^4 \times 0.5 = 3 \times 10^4$ ideas surviving deduplication. $3 \times 10^4 \times 3 = 9 \times 10^4$ idea-conditioned supervision examples per iteration. Thus

$$S = 9 \times 10^4.$$

**Derivation 2: amplification factor.** The number of machine-checked solutions consumed per iteration is $N p_s = 10^5 \times 0.3 = 3 \times 10^4$. The amplification factor is

$$A = \frac{S}{N p_s} = \frac{9 \times 10^4}{3 \times 10^4} = k \, d \, h = 2 \times 0.5 \times 3 = 3.$$

Each verified solution yields $A = 3$ supervision examples, one per training head, under the stated assumptions. This is the number a pipeline engineer compares against the alternative of fine-tuning directly on solutions, which yields exactly $1$ example per solution; the loop's advantage under these assumptions is a factor of $3$.

**Derivation 3: ten-iteration growth of the solved pool.** With $\Delta = 0.05$ and $T = 10$:

$$N_{\text{solved},10} = N \, p_s \, (1 + \Delta)^{10} = 3 \times 10^4 \times 1.05^{10}.$$

Computing $1.05^{10}$ step by step: $1.05^2 = 1.1025$; $1.05^4 = 1.1025^2 = 1.21550625$; $1.05^5 = 1.21550625 \times 1.05 = 1.2762815625$; $1.05^{10} = 1.2762815625^2 = 1.62889462677744140625$. Therefore

$$N_{\text{solved},10} = 3 \times 10^4 \times 1.62889462677744140625 = 4.88668388033232421875 \times 10^4 \approx 4.887 \times 10^4.$$

The solved pool grows from $3 \times 10^4$ to approximately $48{,}867$ problems over ten iterations, a $62.9$-percent increase (since $1.62889462677744 - 1 = 0.62889462677744$).

**Derivation 4: yield at iteration ten.** If the idea-extraction parameters $k$, $d$, $h$ are unchanged, the per-iteration yield at iteration ten scales with the solved pool:

$$S_{10} = N_{\text{solved},10} \, k \, d \, h = 4.88668388033232421875 \times 10^4 \times 3 = 1.466005164099697265625 \times 10^5 \approx 1.466 \times 10^5.$$

**Derivation 5: cumulative supervision over ten iterations.** Summing a geometric series with ratio $(1+\Delta)$:

$$S_{\text{cum}} = S_1 \, \frac{(1+\Delta)^{10} - 1}{\Delta} = 9 \times 10^4 \times \frac{1.62889462677744140625 - 1}{0.05} = 9 \times 10^4 \times \frac{0.62889462677744140625}{0.05}.$$

The fraction is $0.62889462677744140625 / 0.05 = 12.577892535548828125$. Therefore

$$S_{\text{cum}} = 9 \times 10^4 \times 12.577892535548828125 = 1.13201032819939453125 \times 10^6 \approx 1.132 \times 10^6.$$

Over ten iterations the loop produces approximately $1.132 \times 10^6$ idea-conditioned supervision examples from the growing solved pool, under the stated assumptions.

**Derivation 6: break-even condition against direct solution fine-tuning.** Direct fine-tuning on solutions produces $1$ example per solution. The loop is worth its additional reverse-engineering compute only if its yield exceeds direct fine-tuning by more than the ideas that fail to become usable supervision. Formally, with $u$ the fraction of idea-conditioned examples that are usable (non-degenerate, correctly formatted, accepted by the training pipeline), the loop beats direct fine-tuning when

$$A \, u > 1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad k \, d \, h \, u > 1.$$

With $A = 3$, the break-even usability fraction is $u_{\text{be}} = 1/3 \approx 0.333$. Any measured usability above $33.3$ percent makes the loop's supervision cheaper per example than direct solution fine-tuning, on this yield accounting; below it, the loop consumes compute to produce less usable signal than the solutions already provide.

**Derivation 7: sensitivity of the amplification factor.** Because $A = k d h$ is a product, a relative error $\epsilon$ in any single parameter shifts $A$ by approximately that relative error: $\partial A / \partial k = d h = 1.5$, so $\Delta A / A = \Delta k / k$ exactly for multiplicative parameters. Halving $d$ from $0.5$ to $0.25$ halves $A$ to $1.5$; doubling $k$ from $2$ to $4$ doubles $A$ to $6$. The model's headline conclusion — that the loop amplifies verified solutions by a small integer factor — is robust in form but its magnitude is exactly as fragile as the least-measured parameter, which is $k$.

## 5. Results

All numbers in this section are projections computed in Section 4 from the assumption register of Section 3 ($N = 10^5$, $p_s = 0.3$, $k = 2$, $d = 0.5$, $h = 3$, $\Delta = 0.05$); none is an empirical measurement.

- **R1.** One loop iteration over the assumed corpus yields $S = 9 \times 10^4$ idea-conditioned supervision examples (Section 4, Derivation 1).
- **R2.** The amplification factor is $A = k d h = 3$ supervision examples per machine-checked solution consumed, against $1$ for direct fine-tuning on solutions (Section 4, Derivation 2).
- **R3.** Under a per-iteration relative solvable-set growth of $\Delta = 0.05$, the solved-problem pool grows from $3 \times 10^4$ to $N_{\text{solved},10} \approx 4.887 \times 10^4$ after ten iterations, a $62.9$-percent increase (Section 4, Derivation 3).
- **R4.** The per-iteration yield at iteration ten is $S_{10} \approx 1.466 \times 10^5$ examples (Section 4, Derivation 4).
- **R5.** Cumulative supervision over ten iterations is $S_{\text{cum}} \approx 1.132 \times 10^6$ examples (Section 4, Derivation 5).
- **R6.** The break-even usability fraction against direct solution fine-tuning is $u_{\text{be}} = 1/3 \approx 0.333$ (Section 4, Derivation 6).
- **R7.** The amplification factor's relative sensitivity to each of $k$, $d$, $h$ is unity: a relative change in any parameter produces an equal relative change in $A$ (Section 4, Derivation 7).

## 6. Discussion

The yield model's principal claim is that the hindsight-hierarchies loop converts each machine-checked solution into a small integer multiple of supervision examples, and that this multiple — $A = 3$ under the stated assumptions — is the quantity that decides whether the loop is worth running. The claim is deliberately modest: it is an accounting identity given the parameters, not a demonstration that the loop improves solve rates. The growth projection of R3 assumes $\Delta = 0.05$ as an input; the loop's actual effect on $\Delta$ is precisely what the future empirical evaluation of [1], [2] must measure, and this paper provides the accounting frame for that measurement rather than evidence about it.

**Falsification.** The observation that would falsify the main result is a measured usability fraction $u$ below the break-even value $u_{\text{be}} = 1/3$: if, on a real Lean corpus, fewer than one third of idea-conditioned examples produced by the loop are usable as training supervision — because extracted ideas are degenerate restatements of the problem, malformed, or redundant with existing supervision — then the loop produces less usable signal per solution than simply fine-tuning on the solutions themselves, and its amplification claim collapses. A second falsifier is a measured $k \, d$ product below $1/3$: if solved problems yield fewer than roughly one third of an idea each after deduplication, no choice of $h = 3$ rescues the loop. Both falsifiers are measurable in a single corpus study without any training run, which is what makes the model actionable.

**Limitations.** The result rests on the following assumptions, with sensitivity noted for each:

- $N = 10^5$: the corpus size scales $S$ linearly; the amplification factor $A$ is independent of $N$, so conclusions about per-solution yield are insensitive to this assumption while absolute yields are exactly proportional to it.
- $p_s = 0.3$: like $N$, this scales $S$ linearly and leaves $A$ unchanged; a corpus with $p_s = 0.1$ yields $S = 3 \times 10^4$ per iteration under the same $k$, $d$, $h$.
- $k = 2$: enters $A$ multiplicatively with unit relative sensitivity; this is the least-grounded parameter and the most in need of measurement, since the supplied specification of [1], [2] does not state how many ideas a typical solution contains.
- $d = 0.5$: unit relative sensitivity; on a thematically narrow corpus deduplication could be far more aggressive, driving $A$ toward or below the break-even threshold.
- $h = 3$: fixed by the method of [1], [2], not an assumption of this model; it is the one parameter with external grounding.
- $\Delta = 0.05$: an arbitrary illustration of growth, not a prediction; the growth result R3 is a conditional projection whose role is to show the compounding form, and any claimed growth rate must come from measurement.
- Independence of heads: the model counts one example per head per idea and assumes they are equally usable; if the three heads differ in usability, $A$ becomes a weighted sum $A = k d (u_1 + u_2 + u_3)$ and the break-even condition becomes $u_1 + u_2 + u_3 > 1$.
- No interference modeling: joint training of three heads on one model can interfere; the yield model counts examples but says nothing about gradient conflict, which the interpretability agenda of [6] marks as an open question and which no supplied entry resolves.

**Open questions.** Whether solution ideas are better represented as free text, as proof skeletons, or as tactic-level traces is unresolved by the supplied specification. Whether the three capabilities transfer positively or negatively is an empirical property of the joint training that [8]'s framing of transfer-learning assumptions makes salient but does not answer. Whether idea extraction is reliable enough to satisfy the truth-preservation standards that [13] sets for cross-domain translation protocols is untested. And whether presenting extracted ideas to human users helps or hinders them depends on the difficulty-skill alignment that [4] identifies as decisive for cognitive flow, on which the supplied entry reports no measurements.

## 7. Practical Implications and Engineering Pathways

**(a) Who would use the result and what changes.** The immediate users are the authors and readers of the hindsight-hierarchies proposal [1], [2] and, more concretely, engineers building training pipelines for interactive theorem proving in Lean-style systems, who already archive machine-checked solutions as a by-product of formalization work. If the yield model holds, the decision that changes is the allocation of verified solutions between direct fine-tuning and the hindsight loop: the loop is preferred exactly when the measured usability fraction exceeds $u_{\text{be}} = 1/3$, and the measurement required to decide is a corpus annotation task, not a training run. A secondary user is the procedural-data community: Reasoning Core [9] and the hindsight loop produce supervision from different sources, and the common currency of examples per unit of source artifact makes their comparison, and any hybrid pipeline, a calculable design choice.

**(b) Current readiness.** This work is purely analytical and has not been peer reviewed; against the NASA Technology Readiness Level definitions it sits at TRL 1–2: basic principles observed and the concept and its applications formulated on paper. The underlying method of [1], [2] is itself specified but empirically unevaluated by its own authors' statement, so nothing in this lineage supports a readiness claim above analytical. No manufacturing or deployment readiness is claimed, and none is supportable for theoretical work of this kind.

**(c) The smallest next experiment.** Take one Lean corpus segment of $n = 1{,}000$ problems of which a known fraction carry machine-checked solutions. Run the reverse-engineering head of the specification of [1], [2] on the solved subset, extract candidate ideas, deduplicate, and have annotators label each surviving idea-conditioned example as usable or not. The measured quantities are $k$ (ideas per solved problem), $d$ (retention after deduplication), and $u$ (usability). The outcome that refutes the yield model is $k \, d \, u \le 1/3$: at that point the loop's amplification, $A = 3u$ under $k = 2$, $d = 0.5$, falls to or below the direct-fine-tuning baseline of $1$ example per solution. The outcome that supports it is $k d u$ comfortably above $1/3$, which would justify a full training-loop pilot.

**(d) Binding constraints.** The constraints that would bind first, in order of how directly the analysis bears on them: annotation and labeling cost, since the usability fraction $u$ must be measured by human or model judgment over every extracted idea, and this scales with $S$; deduplication quality, since $d$ is a multiplicative factor with unit sensitivity and near-duplicate detection over free-text or skeleton ideas is technically unsolved; representation choice for ideas, which determines both $k$ and $d$ and is an open question rather than a derived constraint; and compute for the reverse-engineering pass, which scales with $N p_s$ per iteration. Regulation, materials, and hardware tolerances do not bind for software-only training pipelines and are not claimed to.

**(e) Order-of-magnitude bounds (projections).** Under the assumption register of Section 3, the absolute bounds are: $9 \times 10^4$ supervision examples per iteration at corpus scale $N = 10^5$, growing to $S_{10} \approx 1.466 \times 10^5$ at iteration ten and $S_{\text{cum}} \approx 1.132 \times 10^6$ cumulative over ten iterations, all conditional on $\Delta = 0.05$ and constant $k$, $d$, $h$. These are projections with stated assumptions, not measurements; their uncertainty is dominated by $k$ and $d$, each carrying unit relative sensitivity into every derived quantity through $A = k d h$.

**(f) Risks and misuse paths.** The direct risk is supervision pollution: if extracted ideas are systematically wrong or misleading while still passing format checks, the loop injects $S$ low-quality examples per iteration, and because the examples are idea-conditioned, errors propagate into the solving head's behavior on unseen problems. A second risk is benchmark contamination in reverse: ideas extracted from archived solutions of benchmark problems can leak solution structure into training data for those same problems, inflating measured solve rates without generalization. Both risks are mitigated by the same measurement that de-risks the yield model — human audit of extracted ideas — extended with a held-out check that ideas for benchmark problems are excluded from training on those problems.

## 8. Conclusion

The hindsight-hierarchies loop of [1], [2] converts a scarce artifact — the machine-checked solution — into a stream of idea-conditioned supervision, and the size of that stream is the quantity that determines whether the loop earns its place in a training pipeline. This paper modeled the stream explicitly: with $h = 3$ heads fixed by the method, and under stated assumptions $k = 2$ ideas per solved problem and $d = 0.5$ deduplication retention, the amplification factor is $A = 3$, the per-iteration yield at corpus scale $N = 10^5$ with $p_s = 0.3$ is $9 \times 10^4$ examples, and ten iterations at an assumed $\Delta = 0.05$ compound to approximately $1.132 \times 10^6$ cumulative examples. The model's value is that it is falsifiable without a training run: a single-corpus measurement of $k$, $d$, and the usability fraction $u$ decides the loop's fate against the break-even threshold $k d u > 1/3$.
