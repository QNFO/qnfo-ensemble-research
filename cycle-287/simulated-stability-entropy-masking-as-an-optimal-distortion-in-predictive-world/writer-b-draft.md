# Simulated Stability: Entropy-Masking as an Optimal Distortion in Predictive World-Modeling

## Abstract

Human and artificial agents must act under irreducible uncertainty, yet empirical psychology repeatedly documents optimism bias and the illusion of control — systematic overestimates of world order and personal agency. Standard predictive-processing accounts treat these distortions as estimation failures. This paper tests the alternative conjecture of *simulated stability*: that a controlled distortion of uncertainty estimates is itself fitness-optimal when representing irreducible uncertainty carries a motivational cost. We formalize a Bayesian agent whose subjective hazard estimate is a compressed fraction $\alpha$ of the true hazard $p$, with a distress cost proportional to the uncertainty the agent does represent. We derive the optimal compression in closed form: $\alpha^{*} = \max\!\left(0,\, 1 - \lambda c / p\right)$, where $\lambda$ is the distress cost per unit represented uncertainty and $c$ the effort cost coefficient. For representative parameters ($p = 0.2$, $c = 1$, $\lambda = 0.1$), the optimal agent underestimates hazard by 50%, expends 12.5% more effort than a veridical agent, and gains 0.005 in fitness (1.67% relative). For rare hazards ($p = 0.05$), the optimum collapses to full suppression ($\alpha^{*} = 0$). The derived signature — distortion magnitude growing as $1/p$ for rare events — is empirically checkable and would falsify the model if absent. The result reframes optimism bias and illusion of control as necessary features of fitness-maximizing inference, and specifies an evolutionary agent simulation that would test it.

## 1. Introduction

Clinicians, behavioral economists, and designers of autonomous agents all confront the same puzzle: human beings systematically overestimate their control over stochastic outcomes and the orderliness of their environments, and these distortions are robust, not marginal. Learned-helplessness interventions, risk-communication campaigns, and calibrated-decision training all fight against a distortion that reappears after correction. If the distortion is a bug, correction should stick and should improve welfare; the fact that it does not easily stick suggests it may be doing functional work. What is blocked today is a design principle: neither clinical practice nor motivated-agent AI design can currently say *how much* distortion of uncertainty a well-functioning cognitive system should carry, or in which direction.

This paper addresses that gap with a formal conjecture we call *simulated stability*: the hypothesis that cognition constructs a deliberately over-structured internal world-model — one that suppresses awareness of irreducible uncertainty — because representing that uncertainty carries a motivational cost. The conjecture originates in speculative philosophical writing: [9] argues that motivation "fundamentally requires the belief that the future is malleable" and that a deterministic reality "severs the link between effort and authored outcomes," so that a functional mind must "hide the underlying mechanics to keep the organism moving forward"; [11] describes subjective autonomy as "a biological hallucination generated by a limited epistemic perspective"; and [12] frames perception generally as "a biological interface designed for survival rather than objective truth" that "actively discards" data. These sources state the conjecture but do not derive it. The contribution here is to convert the conjecture into a decision-theoretic model whose optimum, signatures, and failure conditions can be computed and checked.

The stakes are concrete. If the derivation holds, optimism bias and the illusion of control are not noise to be trained away but equilibrium properties of any fitness-maximizing agent that pays a cost for representing irreducible uncertainty — which changes how clinicians set expectations for calibration training, and how designers of artificial motivated agents set uncertainty-representation targets. If it fails — if the optimal agent is veridical under plausible cost structures — the philosophical program behind simulated stability loses its strongest available formal support.

Our contributions are:

1. A closed-form derivation of the optimal uncertainty-compression factor $\alpha^{*} = \max(0,\, 1 - \lambda c / p)$ for a Bayesian agent facing hazard $p$ with distress cost $\lambda$ and effort cost coefficient $c$, showing that any positive $\lambda$ implies systematic underestimation of hazard (Section 4).
2. Computed numerical signatures of the distortion: a 50% hazard underestimation, a 12.5% effort inflation, and a 1.67% fitness gain at representative parameters, with the distortion growing as $1/p$ and collapsing to full suppression for rare hazards (Sections 4 and 5).
3. An explicit falsification condition and a proposed evolutionary-agent simulation design that would test the model (Sections 6 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The conjecture sits at the intersection of predictive processing, formal cognitive architecture, and philosophical writing on determinism and perception. We review each strand through what the cited works themselves establish.

**Predictive processing and its formal gaps.** [3] develops Predictive Set Theory, a framework that supplies operational definitions predictive processing is argued to lack. Its central formal result, the Asymmetry Theorem of Known and Unknown States, proves that the action-planning function $f$ "cannot derive any element of $U_t$ from $K_t$" — the known-state set cannot generate the unknown — and its Corollary 4.3.2 establishes the "Perpetual Fallibility of Prediction": any prediction is hypothetical and can only be verified or falsified by future sensor refresh, a result the author explicitly connects to Popper's philosophy of science. This theorem is the precise formal home for what we call *irreducible uncertainty*: uncertainty that no computation over known states can eliminate, only sensor refresh can resolve. A further result, the Discreteness Theorem of States (Theorem 5.1), holds that cognitive states are necessarily discrete with discontinuous boundaries — which motivates modeling compression as discrete structural simplification rather than continuous noise injection. Our model takes the asymmetry theorem as its starting ontology: the agent's entropy estimate is an estimate about a set it provably cannot compute over.

**Constructive, non-relay perception.** [1] challenges the cortico-centric view in which the thalamus is a mere relay at the base of a feedforward hierarchy. The paper reports that most (90–95%) of afferents to thalamic relay nuclei are not from sensory organs, that cortical inputs have invaded the thalamus across evolution, and that the thalamus — lacking local excitatory connections — is "optimized for capturing state information that is distributed across multiple cortical nodes while animals are engaged in context-dependent task switching." This matters for our conjecture because it removes the last passive-relay picture of sensory processing: if 90–95% of relay-nucleus input is not sensory, the perceptual stream is constructed and modulated, not transmitted, and there is no architectural reason the construction must be veridical. [2] reinforces this from the human-factors side: it argues that visuospatial complexity "arises from an agent's active engagement with the environment," operationalizes scene complexity with metrics including "clutter, object density, and scene entropy," and applies the model to autonomous-driving benchmarks and VR study design. Scene entropy is thus already a measurable quantity in naturalistic environments, which is the measurement handle our model's $p$ (environmental hazard rate) and its perceived counterpart would need in applied settings.

**Mechanistic definitional scaffolding.** [4] proposes a framework in which "cognition is computation and representation by mind," where a mind is a system with a *self*, and defines a cognition mechanism as "a mechanism having a base among its constituents and a process which engages that base among its emergents," modeled by mathematical digraphs and walks. We adopt this vocabulary at one point only but a load-bearing one: the "self" as base gives a locus for the distress cost $\lambda$ in our model — the cost of representing uncertainty is paid by the system whose self engages the representation, not by an abstract estimator. [4]'s meta-/infra-/iso-mechanism analysis also supplies the level distinction our claim needs: the distortion we derive is a property of the inference mechanism, not of its sub-mechanisms.

**Cognition imposing structure on ambiguous input.** [6] surveys music cognition and records two points we use directly. First, it distinguishes *descriptive* modelling (modelling what mental processes occur) from *explanatory* modelling (formalizing the knowledge that generates the behavior) — our model is explanatory in exactly that sense, deriving the distortion from a cost structure rather than fitting it. Second, it documents that listeners "inherently infer" hierarchical structures (in the Generative Theory of Tonal Music, four types) from a musical surface, and that Krumhansl showed listeners "could adjudge some notes to fit better with the given key than others" given a tonal context — that is, cognition imposes key, hierarchy, and pattern on acoustically ambiguous sequences, and does so even without training. Music perception is therefore a clean natural instance of over-structured interpretation of an uncertain input stream, which is the phenomenological face of the compression our model predicts. It is also notable that [6] opens with Pinker's remark that music is "useless" in biological cause-and-effect terms: a perceptual system that imposes costly structure on useless input is exactly what a distortion-for-function account predicts, since the structuring machinery is not there for music but for maintaining a workable, ordered model generally.

**World models as implementable substrates.** [7] provides a minimalist, reproducible world-model implementation and, critically for us, an honest negative result: in its goal-conditioned planning evaluation on PushT, only the reconstruction-oriented SD-VAE latent checkpoint "produces non-zero planning success" (25.0%), while Web-DINO and V-JEPA 2.1 latent checkpoints yield 0.0%, and increasing sampling and planning budget "still yields zero successful episodes" — the failure "is not primarily caused by an insufficient trajectory optimizer or a weak sampling budget." Two lessons carry over. First, agent simulations of the kind our Section 7 proposes are buildable on exactly this kind of fixed-seed, checkpointed infrastructure (the paper evaluates on 256 fixed validation clips with seed 42, using a CEM-style MPC loop with 64 samples and 5 iterations). Second, world models can fail in ways invisible to their own optimization budget — a structural analogy to the silent model collapse our conjecture predicts for over-compressed agents.

**Pre-explicit cognition.** [8] formalizes "pre-event states" — hesitation, tension, intuition before articulation — as *Rogue Variables*, arguing that "a large fraction of human experience unfolds" before commitments crystallize into reports or narratives, and that these states are "structurally meaningful and predictive of imminent transitions" yet appear as "incoherence" relative to models expecting settled interpretations. The framework instantiates this with an error-weighted operator $\hat{O}_{\epsilon}$ whose spectral structure identifies divergence-associated directions, and explicitly leaves empirical evaluation to future work, listing the required steps (specifying $\Psi_{\mathrm{map}}$, $\mathcal{C}_t$, $\epsilon_t$; constructing a protocol exposing pre-event regimes; evaluating against baselines). Our conjecture is complementary: the entropy-masking distortion we derive operates precisely in the pre-explicit regime [8] describes, shaping the field of interpretations before any report is generated, and [8]'s honest future-work framing is a template for how our own empirical proposals should be stated.

**The philosophical program.** [9] states the conjecture in its strongest form. Its §5.4 argues that motivation "relies on the anticipation of a reward resulting from a specific action," that this calculation "fundamentally requires the belief that the future is malleable," and that under superdeterminism — where "every particle follows a strict, unalterable historical trajectory" — effort does not author outcomes, so "simulated stability carefully constructs this illusion of cause and effect" and "hides the underlying mechanics to keep the organism moving forward." [11] extends this to autonomy and ethics, arguing that Bell-inequality violations leave "the subjective human experience of genuine, spontaneous autonomy" as "a biological hallucination generated by a limited epistemic perspective," and that dualism was a comfort structure for "terrified early humans." [12] generalizes to perception and time: perception is "a highly specialized biological interface designed for survival rather than objective truth" that "actively discards vast" amounts of data, and the flowing river of time is an "epistemic cognitive fiction." [10] contributes a quantitative architecture: modeling the brain's state space as a Bruhat-Tits tree with ultrametric clustering, it reports that statistical tests on triangle-inequality distributions "decisively rejected the Euclidean baseline ($p < 0.001$)" in synthetic neural replicas, with power-law retrieval times matching p-adic diffusion predictions. An ultrametric, hierarchically clustered state space is a natural geometry for the compressed, categorically over-ordered world-model our conjecture posits: ultrametric distance makes categories rigid and tree-like, which is what "over-structured" means geometrically.

We note one boundary: [9], [10], [11], and [12] are speculative philosophical works by a single author, and their physical claims (superdeterminism as established fact, discrete Planck-scale ontology) are contested and not relied on here. We cite them only for the conjecture they articulate and the architectural pictures they propose; the derivations in this paper are independent of whether superdeterminism is true. Our model needs only that the agent cannot compute over unknown states — which [3] proves as a property of any cognitive architecture, not a physical thesis.

## 3. Methods

This paper is theoretical: it proposes a model, derives its optimum analytically, and computes numerical signatures. No experiment, simulation, or data collection was performed; the empirical study that would test the model is specified as a proposal in Section 7.

**Model.** Consider an agent in an environment where an adverse state (hazard) occurs with probability $p \in (0,1)$ per episode. The agent can invest effort $E \geq 0$ in a goal-directed action. Effort costs $cE^2/2$ with $c > 0$ (quadratic effort cost, the standard convex assumption) and succeeds in producing reward with probability $1 - p$, independent of $E$; the hazard is irreducible in the sense of [3] — no computation over the agent's known states can determine whether it will occur.

**Compression.** The agent's subjective model represents only a fraction $\alpha \in [0,1]$ of the hazard: its perceived hazard is

$$\hat{p} = \alpha p,$$

and its perceived success probability is $1 - \hat{p} = 1 - \alpha p$. The parameter $\alpha$ is the *fidelity* of the agent's uncertainty representation; $1 - \alpha$ is the *compression* or *masking* level. $\alpha = 1$ is the veridical Bayesian agent; $\alpha < 1$ is an agent whose world-model is over-ordered relative to the environment — the "simulated stability" regime.

**Distress cost.** We assume that representing irreducible uncertainty carries a motivational cost, paid in proportion to the uncertainty the agent does represent:

$$D(\alpha) = \lambda \alpha p, \qquad \lambda > 0.$$

This is the formalization of the conjecture's core premise: awareness of unresolvable hazard is aversive and degrades the agent's functioning (in [9]'s language, it undermines the belief that the future is malleable; in [3]'s terms, the agent is forced to act on predictions that are provably fallible). The cost is modeled as a direct fitness deduction; Section 6 discusses the alternative of modeling it as an effort multiplier.

**Agent behavior.** The agent chooses effort to maximize its *subjective* utility, using its perceived hazard:

$$U_{\text{sub}}(E; \alpha) = E(1 - \alpha p) - \frac{cE^2}{2} - \lambda \alpha p.$$

The first-order condition $\partial U_{\text{sub}} / \partial E = (1 - \alpha p) - cE = 0$ gives the agent's effort policy:

$$E^{*}(\alpha) = \frac{1 - \alpha p}{c}.$$

**Fitness.** Actual fitness is evaluated against the *true* hazard $p$, plus the distress the agent actually pays:

$$F(\alpha) = E^{*}(\alpha)(1 - p) - \frac{c\, E^{*}(\alpha)^2}{2} - \lambda \alpha p.$$

The design question is which $\alpha$ maximizes $F$. Note the asymmetry that drives the result: effort is chosen under the perceived hazard but rewarded under the true one, while distress is paid on the represented hazard.

**Numerical scenarios.** We evaluate three hazard levels — $p = 0.2$ (a common-event scenario), $p = 0.5$ (a coin-flip-level hazard), and $p = 0.05$ (a rare hazard) — with $c = 1$ and $\lambda = 0.1$ throughout. These parameter values are assumptions chosen for illustration, not measurements; Section 6 states how the conclusions scale with them.

## 4. Analysis

All inputs are stated in Section 3: hazard probabilities $p \in \{0.05, 0.2, 0.5\}$, effort cost coefficient $c = 1$, distress cost per unit represented uncertainty $\lambda = 0.1$. Every number below is computed from these by the shown steps.

**Step 1: Closed-form fitness.** Substituting $E^{*}(\alpha) = (1 - \alpha p)/c$ into $F$:

$$F(\alpha) = \frac{(1 - \alpha p)(1 - p)}{c} - \frac{(1 - \alpha p)^2}{2c} - \lambda \alpha p.$$

**Step 2: First-order condition.** Differentiate with respect to $\alpha$:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial \alpha} = \frac{-p(1 - p)}{c} + \frac{p(1 - \alpha p)}{c} - \lambda p = \frac{p}{c}\left[(1 - \alpha p) - (1 - p)\right] - \lambda p = \frac{p^2 (1 - \alpha)}{c} - \lambda p.$$

Setting $\partial F / \partial \alpha = 0$:

$$\frac{p^2 (1 - \alpha)}{c} = \lambda p \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \alpha^{*} = 1 - \frac{\lambda c}{p}.$$

**Step 3: Second-order check.** $\partial^2 F / \partial \alpha^2 = -p^2/c < 0$ for all $p > 0$, $c > 0$, so the stationary point is a maximum.

**Step 4: Boundary.** Since $\alpha \in [0,1]$, the constrained optimum is

$$\alpha^{*} = \max\!\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{\lambda c}{p}\right).$$

Any $\lambda > 0$ with $\lambda c < p$ yields $\alpha^{*} < 1$: *the optimal agent strictly underestimates hazard*. Distortion vanishes only when $\lambda = 0$ (no cost to representing uncertainty) and is total when $\lambda c \geq p$ (the distress of representing a hazard exceeds its motivational value).

**Step 5: Numerical evaluation, $p = 0.2$, $c = 1$, $\lambda = 0.1$.**
