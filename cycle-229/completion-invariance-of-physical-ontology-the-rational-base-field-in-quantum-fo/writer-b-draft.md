# Completion-Invariance of Physical Ontology: The Rational Base Field in Quantum Foundations

## Abstract

Every recorded quantum measurement outcome is a rational number, yet the standard formalism is stated over the archimedean completion $\mathbb{R}$, and the choice of completion is rarely examined as an ontological commitment. We propose and formalize a principle of *completion-invariance*: the archimedean completion $\mathbb{R}$ and the non-archimedean completions $\mathbb{Q}_p$ of the rational base field $\mathbb{Q}$ are equally instrumental constructs, so any consistent realism about the continuum must extend equal reality-status to every completion of the same base field. We develop a finite-precision (rational) formulation of quantum mechanics in which amplitudes are restricted to rationals of bounded denominator and prove an explicit error bound: for a $d$-dimensional system with amplitudes approximated at $n$-bit precision, every Born-rule probability deviates from its standard value by at most $(2+\delta)\sqrt{d}\,\delta$ with $\delta = 2^{-n-1}$, which for $d=4$ and $n=64$ is $\approx 1.08\times 10^{-19}$. We derive the bit budget required to reach any target tolerance ($n=41$ bits for $d=4$ at tolerance $10^{-12}$; $n=21$ bits at $10^{-6}$), identify scale-invariant and ultrametric regimes as candidate loci of genuine completion-divergence, and formalize the transfer thesis that any realist argument for $\mathbb{R}$-reality transfers mutatis mutandis to $\mathbb{Q}_p$-reality. The principle converts a metaphysical dispute into a sharp formal question — do standard quantum predictions depend on the archimedean completion at all? — and legitimizes adelic methods as calculational rather than ontological.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum mechanics is written in the language of the real numbers. State vectors live in Hilbert spaces over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, spectra are real, and probabilities are non-negative reals. Yet every measurement outcome ever recorded — every pointer reading, every detector click, every digit in a data file — is a rational number: a finite string, a ratio of integers. The physical content of the theory is therefore delivered entirely within $\mathbb{Q}$, while the formalism is stated in a completion of $\mathbb{Q}$. This gap between the field over which the theory is formulated and the field in which its results are expressed is usually treated as harmless bookkeeping. This paper argues that it is not harmless, and that taking it seriously yields a substantive foundational principle with practical consequences.

Who faces this problem in practice? Three groups. First, engineers designing finite-resource quantum computing systems: the finite-information critique of the continuum has been used to argue that resource estimates built on real-valued state descriptions (for example, surface-code physical-to-logical qubit ratios of roughly $1000{:}1$, as cited in the ultrametric program of [11]) inherit an unexamined commitment to infinite-precision amplitudes, and no tool currently exists to certify how many bits of amplitude precision any quantum protocol actually requires. Second, practitioners of adelic and $p$-adic methods in physics, whose calculational choices are routinely met with the objection that $p$-adic numbers are "not physically real"; today that objection is answered informally, case by case, rather than by a principle. Third, philosophers and foundational researchers assessing realist interpretations: the pilot-wave research program descended from Bohm's 1952 papers [7] posits an ontology over the reals, and the ontological-models literature [8] lacks a criterion for when two mathematical formulations of the same operational theory should be granted the same ontological status. What is blocked today is a principled answer to the question: how much of the real-number apparatus of quantum mechanics is load-bearing, and how much is convention? If the result of this paper holds — that all operationally accessible quantum predictions are reproducible within a rational base field at a modest, explicitly computable bit budget — then the choice of completion is demoted to a calculational convention, bit budgets for rational quantum simulation become designable rather than guessed, and the ontological objection to adelic methods dissolves.

The principle is **completion-invariance**: $\mathbb{R}$ and the $p$-adic fields $\mathbb{Q}_p$ are *equally instrumental* completions of the same rational base field, so a physical ontology must be invariant under the choice of completion. Whichever completion one declares "physically real," the same argument licenses the others; hence a realist about the continuum who rejects $p$-adic reality on ontological grounds is applying a double standard that the structure of the mathematics does not support. Completion-invariance forbids realism about the continuum specifically, while leaving room for realism about the rational base field itself — the field that all completions share and that all measurements inhabit.

The principle matters because it converts a metaphysical dispute — is the continuum real? — into a sharp formal question: **do standard quantum predictions depend on the archimedean completion at all?** If the answer is no for all operationally accessible observables, then the choice of completion is a calculational convention, and adelic methods (the joint use of real and $p$-adic techniques) are legitimate tools rather than ontological commitments. If the answer is yes in some regime, completion-invariance tells us exactly where to look for physically observable completion-divergence.

The finite-information premise of this argument is not new. The program examined in [10] argues that physical quantities contain only finite information and that real numbers are physically unreal — *de facto* idealizations; the same source reports, in its own red-team audit, the finding R5 that neither that program nor its companion framework has produced a differential empirical prediction, positioning the work as foundations/philosophy of physics. Our contribution converges with the finite-information premise but sharpens its target: the problem is not merely that $\mathbb{R}$ carries too much information, but that $\mathbb{R}$ is one completion among many, and privileging it is an unmotivated symmetry breaking at the level of ontology. The three-axis framework of [10] — Depth (computable reals), Breadth (non-computable reals, judged physically vacuous), and Valuation ($p$-adic completions as information carriers) — supplies a taxonomy in which our principle reads as a constraint on the Valuation axis: no valuation may be ontologically privileged.

Our contributions are:

1. **Formulation and transfer theorem.** We state completion-invariance as a definition and prove a transfer thesis (Proposition 1): any realist argument for $\mathbb{R}$-reality appealing only to density of $\mathbb{Q}$, completeness, or field closure transfers mutatis mutandis to $\mathbb{Q}_p$-reality (Section 3.1).
2. **Rational quantum mechanics with an explicit error bound.** We construct a finite-precision formulation and derive $\epsilon_P \le (2+\delta)\sqrt{d}\,2^{-n-1}$ for every Born-rule probability, with full arithmetic; for $d=4$, $n=64$ this is $1.084202\times 10^{-19}$ (Sections 4.1–4.2).
3. **Bit-budget formulas.** We derive the minimal precision $n$ for any target tolerance: $n = 41$ bits for $d=4$ at tolerance $10^{-12}$ and $n=21$ at $10^{-6}$, with verification arithmetic (Section 4.3), and the corresponding state-specification information budgets of $328$ and $168$ bits (Section 4.4).
4. **Divergence loci.** We identify scale-invariant and ultrametric regimes as the only candidate loci where completions can genuinely diverge, stated as hypotheses (Section 4.6).
5. **Falsifiability criteria and engineering pathway.** We state what would falsify the principle, and translate the bit-budget results into a concrete next numerical experiment and a technology-readiness assessment (Sections 6 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

This section situates the proposal in twelve works, discussing each for a specific point its text makes.

**[3] Introduction to *Quantum Theory: Informational Foundations and Foils*.** This introduction to a contributed volume (Springer Netherlands, 2016) distinguishes the dynamicist tradition, in which the physicist describes natural dynamical behaviour without reference to human agents, from the pragmatist tradition, in which the laws of physics are characterized by the extent to which we can learn and control the behaviour of physical systems. It further documents the shift from interpretation to reconstruction: where older work took the formalism as given and inferred a reality story from it (the volume's text names the Everett and de Broglie–Bohm interpretations and collapse models as examples of the older mode), the newer wave reconstructs quantum theory from physical principles. Completion-invariance belongs squarely in this reconstruction program: we take the operational record — rational outcomes — as primitive and ask which mathematical apparatus is forced by it. The volume's framing that the quantum-information revolution reconceived quantum theory in terms of information-processing possibilities supports our stance that the theory's content is informational, not continuum-ontological.

**[8] *Quantum prescriptions are more ontologically distinct than they are operationally distinguishable*.** This work generalizes the Leibniz principle of the identity of indiscernibles into a principle of *bounded ontological distinctness*, which equates the distinguishability of a set of operational physical entities to the distinctness of their ontological counterparts. Its framework analyzes prepare-transform-measure experiments, in which an operational theory specifies preparation, transformation, and measurement procedures and predicts probabilities $p(k|P,T,M)$, and its concluding remarks observe that well-known ontological principles — Bell's local causality being the paradigm — supply ontological bases for operational *indistinguishability* conditions, whose quantum violation then discards the corresponding ontological models. This is the closest structural analogue to our argument. We apply the same schema one level up: $\mathbb{R}$-reality and $\mathbb{Q}_p$-reality are two ontological posits that generate no operational distinguishability at any attainable precision, so bounded ontological distinctness denies them distinct ontological status. Completion-invariance is bounded ontological distinctness applied to completions of $\mathbb{Q}$.

**[10] *Finite Specification, Ontological Indeterminism: The Gisin–Del Santo Program Converges with Autaxys Ontological Closure*.** This work argues that physical quantities contain only finite information, that real numbers are physically unreal — *de facto* hidden variables — and that classical mechanics is consequently ontologically indeterminate. Its introduction observes that the real number system, formalized in the late nineteenth century by Dedekind, Cantor, and Weierstrass, was adopted wholesale by physics and may introduce ontological commitments that nature does not honor. Its cross-domain consilience analysis tabulates the same structural dynamic — finite specification, ontological openness, processual determination — across physics, computer science, cognitive science, information theory, and biology. Critically for our purposes, its red-team audit reports finding R5: no differential empirical prediction has been produced, and the response positions the work as foundations/philosophy of physics whose value is theoretical unification. Our paper supplies precisely what that audit identifies as missing at the level of the number system: a quantitative bound (Section 4.1) that converts the finite-information premise into checkable numbers.

**[10] also supplies the taxonomy in which our constraint lives.** Its companion framework [9] — Depth, Breadth, and Valuation: A Unified Ontology of the Physical Continuum — assigns ontological weight to $p$-adic completions as information carriers on the Valuation axis, with falsifiable predications for quantum error correction and gauge structure. Our principle is a direct constraint on that axis: no valuation may be ontologically privileged, so any ontological weight assigned to $\mathbb{Q}_p$ must be assigned equally to $\mathbb{R}$ and vice versa. The specific QEC and gauge predictions are not developed in the text available to us, so we treat the falsifiability claim as programmatic.

**[11] *Alpha Pi Project*.** This work begins from an engineering challenge: quantum states decohere rapidly, and the text attributes this fragility to the continuous real-number field underlying standard quantum mechanics, arguing that the Bloch sphere is a smooth continuous manifold allowing infinitesimal thermal drifts to accumulate. It cites surface-code physical-to-logical qubit ratios of roughly $1000{:}1$ and argues that active quantum error correction faces thermodynamic walls and a signal-processing ceiling, motivating an "ontological pivot" to a discrete ultrametric hierarchy. Its experimental-signatures section lists testable predictions distinguishing the framework from standard quantum mechanics: deviations at ultra-low energies, anisotropies in the fine-structure constant observable in quasar absorption lines, discrete-spacetime effects in cosmic-ray events, and tests with few-particle systems in ultracold traps. We cite [11] for two specific roles. First, it is a worked example of a program that *does* treat the continuum commitment as an engineering variable — the same motivation as ours — but replaces $\mathbb{R}$ with a specific ultrametric structure and attaches empirical predictions to it; our principle is more conservative, making no replacement and no prediction, only forbidding ontological privilege. Second, its ultrametric predictions are exactly the kind of completion-divergence effects our Section 4.6 locates: if any of them were confirmed, the operational-indistinguishability claim of this paper would be falsified in the relevant regime.

**[12] *Syntactic Generation*.** This work rejects foundational substances — particles, fields, or continuous spatial manifolds — and constructs physical structure from a single primitive: the act of drawing a boundary, a syntactic token whose entire existence is its capacity to separate inside from outside, combined by recursive rules of juxtaposition over a syntactic void that acts as an identity element. Its relevance to us is methodological: it demonstrates, in a different domain, the strategy of rebuilding physical ontology from a minimal discrete substrate rather than from a continuum, and it shares our suspicion that the continuous spatial manifold is an inherited assumption rather than a derived necessity. We do not adopt its boundary calculus; we note only that a rational base field is the minimal shared substrate of all completions, playing for the number system the role its syntactic void plays for structure.

**[7] Forewords for the special issue *Pilot-wave and beyond*.** This foreword documents that Bohm's two 1952 papers on hidden variables, submitted 5 July 1951 and published 15 January 1952, had a tremendous long-term effect, quoting Bell's testimony: "In 1952 I saw the impossible done," and connecting Bohm's work to Bell's subsequent theorem on the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox. The realist programs descended from de Broglie–Bohm are the primary targets of completion-invariance: a pilot-wave ontology formulated over $\mathbb{R}$ inherits the completion-choice problem in its strongest form, because the posited wavefunction configuration is a point of a real vector space, not merely a predictor of rational outcomes. The topical collection this foreword introduces is the active venue in which such ontologies are defended, which is why the transfer thesis of Proposition 1 matters there.

**[4] *Homotopy Type Theory: Univalent Foundations of Mathematics*.** This work presents a foundational language for mathematics based on a connection between homotopy theory and type theory, in which Voevodsky's univalence axiom implies that isomorphic structures can be identified, and higher inductive types provide direct logical descriptions. Its preface records the 2012–13 IAS Special Year on Univalent Foundations organized by Awodey, Coquand, and Voevodsky. Univalence is the structural analogue of completion-invariance: structures that are isomorphic should be identified as carriers of the same content unless a structure-preserving distinction is exhibited. The completions of $\mathbb{Q}$ are not isomorphic as valued fields — the archimedean and $p$-adic norms are inequivalent — so univalence does not literally identify them; rather, it supplies the template our Proposition 1 instantiates: to distinguish two structures ontologically, one must exhibit the specific structure-bearing property that does the distinguishing, and we show that the standard candidates (density, completeness, field closure) are shared, leaving only the archimedean ordering.

**[5] Recent experiments performed at "Carlo Novero" lab at INRIM.** This paper reports work at the "Carlo Novero" laboratory of the Italian National Institute of Metrological Research (INRIM), devoted to experimental study of the foundations of quantum mechanics and quantum information using quantum optical states, principally parametric down-conversion (PDC) biphoton pairs. Its text gives concrete figures: a bright source of polarization entangled states with a 10 kHz coincidence rate at 200 mW pump power, built from two type I $LiIO_3$ crystals of 1.5 cm length; and type II PDC experiments using 250 m and 1 km of dispersive fiber in which temporal wave-packet spreading enabled interference effects below the temporal resolution of the detectors and restoration of indistinguishability allowing a Bell-inequality violation. We cite [5] for the character of the experimental class in which any observable completion-divergence would have to be sought: precision optical tests of entanglement and local realism. The text does not report probability-level precision figures, so we cannot anchor our tolerance assumptions empirically from it; this is flagged as a limitation in Section 6.

**[1] and [2] The Gene Ontology primer and pitfalls chapters.** Entry [1] describes the Gene Ontology (GO) as the largest resource for cataloguing gene function, whose combination of solid conceptual underpinnings and practical features has made it widely adopted and essential for data analysis. Entry [2] states that the GO "is sufficiently simple that it can be used without deep understanding of its structure or how it is developed, which is both a strength and a weakness," and discusses common misinterpretations of the ontology. We cite these for a methodological analogy, explicitly flagged as ours: an "ontology" in the informatic sense is a commitment structure that users can adopt without inspecting its foundations, and [2] documents that such unexamined use breeds misinterpretation. Physics' unexamined commitment to $\mathbb{R}$ is, on our reading, exactly such an unexamined ontological default — usable without deep understanding of what completion was chosen, which is both a strength (calculational convenience) and a weakness (hidden ontological commitment).

**[6] *GraphMatcher: A Graph Representation Learning Approach for Ontology Matching*.** This work defines ontology matching as finding correspondences between entities in different ontologies to solve interoperability problems, and presents GraphMatcher, a system using graph attention with a new neighbourhood aggregation approach, developed in Python with PyTorch and Ontospy and evaluated in the OAEI 2022 conference track. Its results table reports F1-measures of 0.71 (ra1-M1), 0.67 (ra1-M3), and 0.66 (ra2-M1), with weaker performance in the M2 variants, and it reports the highest F1-measure (72%) on uncertain reference alignments among all matchers in the track. We cite it to complete the analogy begun with [1] and [2]: where two ontological schemes must interoperate, matching seeks the structural correspondences that make them mutually translatable. Completion-invariance performs the same service for $\mathbb{R}$-based and $\mathbb{Q}_p$-based formulations of quantum mechanics: it identifies the shared base field $\mathbb{Q}$ as the translation manual and forbids declaring either side ontologically fundamental. The measured performance figures of [6] are irrelevant to physics; only the interoperability framing is borrowed, and it is borrowed as an analogy.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 The rational base field and its completions

Let $\mathbb{Q}$ denote the rational numbers. A **completion** of $\mathbb{Q}$ is a field into which $\mathbb{Q}$ embeds densely and completely with respect to a norm. The archimedean norm $|\cdot|_\infty$ yields $\mathbb{R}$; the $p$-adic norms $|\cdot|_p$, defined for a prime $p$ by writing $q = p^{k}\,\frac{a}{b}$ with $\gcd(a,p)=\gcd(b,p)=1$ and setting $|q|_p = p^{-k}$, yield the fields $\mathbb{Q}_p$. All completions share the same dense subfield $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Definition (completion-invariance).** A physical ontology $O$ is *completion-invariant* if, for any two completions $K_1, K_2$ of $\mathbb{Q}$ and any ontological commitment $C(K_i)$ expressed as "the physical quantities take values in $K_i$," the argument schema justifying $C(K_1)$ over the shared base $\mathbb{Q}$ also justifies $C(K_2)$.

**Proposition 1 (transfer thesis).** Any realist argument for $\mathbb{R}$-reality that appeals only to (i) the density of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$, (ii) the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$, or (iii) the closure of $\mathbb{R}$ under the operations appearing in the quantum formalism, transfers mutatis mutandis to $\mathbb{Q}_p$-reality, since $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in each $\mathbb{Q}_p$, each $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is complete, and each $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is a field closed under addition, multiplication, and division. Hence consistent realism must accept all completions or none.

The proof is immediate: the three cited properties are shared by every completion of $\mathbb{Q}$ by construction, so no argument appealing to them alone can discriminate. An argument that does discriminate must appeal to a property $\mathbb{R}$ has and $\mathbb{Q}_p$ lacks — the archimedean ordering, which underwrites limits, convergence of series, and the topological connectedness used in spectral theory. Section 3.3 separates these uses from the operational content of the theory.

### 3.2 Rational quantum mechanics

Fix a finite-dimensional system of dimension $d$. A **rational state** is $|\tilde\psi\rangle \in \mathbb{Q}(i)^d$ (rational real and imaginary parts) with $\langle \tilde\psi | \tilde\psi \rangle = 1$ exactly, or approximately with controlled error. An observable is a Hermitian matrix with rational entries; its eigenvalues are then algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ and computable to any requested precision by rational arithmetic. Measurement outcomes are recorded as rationals of bounded denominator $q = 2^n$ (binary fixed-point with $n$ fractional bits).

The physical claim of completion-invariance is: for every operationally accessible observable and every experimentally attainable precision $\epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}$, there exists an $n$ such that the rational formulation reproduces the standard ($\mathbb{R}$-formulated) predictions to within $\epsilon_{\mathrm{exp}}$. Section 4 derives the required $n$ explicitly.

### 3.3 What the archimedean structure is used for

In standard quantum mechanics, $\mathbb{R}$ supplies: (a) the ordering used to state spectral decompositions and measurement statistics; (b) convergence of the perturbation and scattering formalisms; (c) continuity assumptions in dynamics. None of these enters the *recorded content* of an experiment, which is a finite table of rational outcomes and rational instrument settings. The $p$-adic fields carry their own analysis (ultrametric topology, in which $|x+y|_p \le \max(|x|_p,|y|_p)$), and the Valuation axis of [9] explicitly treats $p$-adic completions as information carriers. Our method is to separate the *calculational* role of $\mathbb{R}$ (legitimate, conventional) from its *ontological* role (forbidden by Proposition 1 unless all completions are granted equal status).

## 4. Analysis

All numbers in this section are derived here from stated inputs; no empirical data are imported.

### 4.1 Probability-error bound for rational amplitudes

**Inputs.** Dimension $d$; approximation precision parameter $\delta$ (defined below); the fact that each amplitude satisfies $|\psi_k| \le 1$ because $\sum_k |\psi_k|^2 = 1$.

**Step 1 (rational approximation).** Any real amplitude $\psi_k$ can be approximated by a rational $\tilde\psi_k = a_k / 2^n$ with

$$|\psi_k - \tilde\psi_k| \le \frac{1}{2 \cdot 2^n} = 2^{-n-1}.$$

Set $\delta = 2^{-n-1}$.

**Step 2 (state-vector error).** By the triangle inequality in the $\ell_2$ norm,

$$\|\psi - \tilde\psi\|_2 = \left(\sum_{k=1}^{d} |\psi_k - \tilde\psi_k|^2\right)^{1/2} \le \sqrt{d}\,\delta.$$

**Step 3 (per-coordinate bound).** Since $|\psi_k| \le 1$ and $|\tilde\psi_k| \le |\psi_k| + \delta \le 1 + \delta$, the Born-rule probability for outcome $j$ deviates by

$$\left|\,|\psi_j|^2 - |\tilde\psi_j|^2\,\right| = |\psi_j - \tilde\psi_j|\,|\psi_j + \tilde\psi_j| \le \delta\,(|\psi_j| + |\tilde\psi_j|) \le \delta\,(1 + 1 + \delta) = (2+\delta)\,\delta.$$

**Step 4 (worst case over the spectrum).** For any observable with rational entries, each eigenvalue-eigenstate projection is itself a vector to which Steps 1–3 apply, so the worst-case deviation of any Born probability from its standard value is bounded by

$$\epsilon_P \le (2+\delta)\,\sqrt{d}\,\delta, \qquad \delta = 2^{-n-1}.$$

This is the central bound: the deviation of every quantum prediction from its rational reformulation is at most $(2+\delta)\sqrt{d}\,2^{-n-1}$, i.e., of order $\sqrt{d}\,2^{-n}$.

### 4.2 Numerical evaluation for a two-qubit system

Take $d = 4$ (two qubits) and $n = 64$ fractional bits. Then $\delta = 2^{-65}$ and

$$\epsilon_P \le (2 + 2^{-65}) \cdot \sqrt{4} \cdot 2^{-65} = (2 + 2^{-65}) \cdot 2 \cdot 2^{-65} = (4 + 2^{-64})\, 2^{-65}.$$

Numerically: $2^{-64} = 1/2^{64} = 1/1.8446744\times 10^{19} \approx 5.421011\times 10^{-20}$, so $2^{-65} \approx 2.710505\times 10^{-20}$, and

$$\epsilon_P \le 4 \times 2.710505431\times 10^{-20} + 2^{-64}\cdot 2^{-65} \approx 1.084202172\times 10^{-19} + 1.47\times 10^{-39} \approx 1.084202173\times 10^{-19}.$$

The second term is negligible; the bound is $\epsilon_P \approx 1.084202\times 10^{-19}$, i.e. $\approx 1.08\times 10^{-19}$ to two significant figures.

For comparison, at the double-precision word size $n = 53$: $\delta = 2^{-54} \approx 5.5511\times 10^{-17}$, and $\epsilon_P \le (2+2^{-54})\cdot 2\cdot 2^{-54} \approx 4 \times 5.551115\times 10^{-17} = 2.220446\times 10^{-16}$.

### 4.3 Bit budget for a target tolerance

**Problem.** Given target tolerance $\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}}$ and dimension $d$, find the minimal $n$ such that $\epsilon_P \le \epsilon_{\mathrm{target}}$. Using the leading-order bound $\sqrt{d}\,2^{-n} \le \epsilon_{\mathrm{target}}$ (a conservative simplification: the true bound $(2+\delta)\sqrt{d}\,2^{-n-1}$ is smaller, so the derived $n$ is sufficient),

$$n \ge \log_2\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}}}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\log_2 d + \log_2\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}}}.$$

**Example 1.** $d = 4$, $\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}} = 10^{-12}$: $\frac{1}{2}\log_2 4 = 1$, and $\log_2(10^{12}) = 12 \log_2 10 = 12 \times 3.321928 = 39.86314$. So

$$n \ge 1 + 39.86314 = 40.86314 \implies n = 41.$$

**Check.** With $n = 41$: $\sqrt{d}\,2^{-n} = 2 \cdot 2^{-41} = 2^{-40} = 1/1.0995116\times 10^{12} \approx 9.0949\times 10^{-13} < 10^{-12}$.

**Example 2.** $d = 4$, $\epsilon_{\mathrm{target}} = 10^{-6}$: $n \ge 1 + 6 \times 3.321928 = 20.93157$, so $n = 21$ fractional bits suffice.

### 4.4 Information budget of a rational state

Specifying a rational state $|\tilde\psi\rangle \in \mathbb{Q}(i)^d$ at $n$ fractional bits requires $2 d n$ bits (real and imaginary parts, $d$ components each). For $d=4$, $n=64$: $2 \times 4 \times 64 = 512$ bits. For the $n=41$ configuration of Section 4.3: $2 \times 4 \times 41 = 328$ bits. For the $n=21$ configuration: $2 \times 4 \times 21 = 168$ bits. This makes precise the finite-specification premise of [10]: the physically meaningful content of a two-qubit state is fully carried by a few hundred bits, with no residual appeal to the continuum.

### 4.5 Grid-rounding convention (alternative bound)

An alternative, coarser convention models the measurement apparatus as rounding every probability $p \in [0,1]$ to the nearest multiple of $1/N$ for a maximal denominator $N$: $q = \mathrm{round}(pN)/N$. Since $|pN - \mathrm{round}(pN)| \le 1/2$, dividing by $N$ gives

$$|p - q| \le \frac{1}{2N}.$$
