# Representation-Invariant Structure of the Prime Numbers: A Functorial Framework Unifying Arithmetic, Analytic, and Computational Presentations

**Preprint — draft for open peer review**

---

## Abstract

The prime numbers admit many presentations: as the atomic set $\mathcal{P} \subset \mathbb{N}$, as the support of the von Mangoldt function $\Lambda(n)$, as the zeros-and-poles data of the Riemann zeta function $\zeta(s)$, as the output of sieve procedures, as Beatty-type sequences generated by irrational rotations, and as the fixed points of concrete algorithms. Working number theorists move fluidly between these presentations, but the literature rarely makes explicit *which* statements about the primes survive a change of presentation and *which* are artifacts of the chosen encoding. In this preprint we formalize this question by defining a category $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$ whose objects are presentations of the primes and whose morphisms are structure-preserving translations between them. We prove that the Euler product identity, the Möbius inversion relation, and the leading asymptotic $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ of the prime-counting function are invariants of this category: they are preserved under every morphism. We further identify quantities that are *not* invariants — for example, the error term in the prime number theorem, which changes form between the analytic (zeta-zero) presentation and the sieve presentation — and we quantify the divergence with explicit numerical comparisons of $\pi(x)$ against the logarithmic integral $\operatorname{Li}(x)$ for $x = 10, 10^2, \dots, 10^6$. The framework gives a precise language for a question that is usually handled by informal analogy: when a theorem "about the primes" is proved in one presentation, what exactly transfers to another? We argue that the answer is: exactly the functorial image of the statement, and we give a decision procedure for testing functoriality of a statement's hypotheses. Worked examples include the transfer of Möbius inversion between the arithmetic and sieve presentations, and the failure of naive transfer for statements quantifying over individual primes without a naturality condition.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The problem

Every mathematician who works with the primes maintains, usually without comment, a private equivalence relation on ways of talking about them. The statement "$\sum_{n \le x} \Lambda(n) \sim x$" and the statement "$\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$" are treated as the same theorem, because the first implies the second by partial summation and the second implies the first by a standard Abelian argument. The statement "$\zeta(s)$ has no zeros on $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$" is treated as equivalent to both. Yet the *proofs* live in different worlds: one in elementary arithmetic, one in complex analysis, one in the theory of Dirichlet series. The tacit claim that these are "the same theorem" is a claim about a category that has never, to our knowledge, been written down in full generality and used as a working tool.

The cost of leaving this claim tacit is concrete. Statements about the primes are regularly transported between presentations in proofs, and the transport occasionally fails silently. A hypothesis of the form "for all primes $p$" is functorial; a hypothesis of the form "for the least prime $p$ with property $P$" generally is not, because the *least* element of a set is not preserved by an order-preserving-but-not-order-reflecting map. A bound of the form $O(x/\log x)$ in the sieve presentation corresponds to a bound of a different shape in the analytic presentation, and conflating the two shapes produces error terms that are wrong by a factor that grows with $x$.

### 1.2 The proposal

We propose to treat presentations of the primes as objects of a category and theorems about the primes as statements with well-defined functoriality properties. Concretely:

1. **Objects.** A *presentation* of the primes is a structure $(X, \phi, \iota)$ where $X$ is a mathematical object (a set, a measure space, a function, a multiset of complex numbers, an algorithm), $\phi$ is a distinguished substructure or function on $X$ intended to encode the primes, and $\iota$ is an interpretation map connecting $\phi$ to the canonical presentation $\mathcal{P} \subset \mathbb{N}$.

2. **Morphisms.** A morphism $(X, \phi, \iota) \to (Y, \psi, \jmath)$ is a map $f : X \to Y$ (of the appropriate kind for the ambient category in which $X$ and $Y$ live) such that the square

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
X & \xrightarrow{f} & Y \\
\downarrow{\iota} & & \downarrow{\jmath} \\
\mathcal{P} & = & \mathcal{P}
\end{array}
$$

commutes in a sense made precise in Section 3 — that is, $f$ carries the prime-encoding substructure of $X$ onto the prime-encoding substructure of $Y$, as witnessed against the fixed canonical presentation.

3. **Invariants.** A statement $S$ about the primes is an *invariant* of the category if, whenever $S$ holds in presentation $X$ and there is a morphism $X \to Y$, then $S$ holds in presentation $Y$. Our main results identify a core of statements that are invariants: the Euler product, Möbius inversion, and the leading term of the prime-counting asymptotic. We also identify statements that fail to be invariants and explain the mechanism of failure.

### 1.3 Contributions

- We define the category $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$ and prove it is well-defined (identities and composition exist and satisfy the category axioms; Section 3).
- We prove the **Invariance Theorem** (Theorem 4.1): the Euler product identity $\prod_{p}(1 - p^{-s})^{-1} = \sum_n n^{-s}$ for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$, the Möbius inversion relation, and the asymptotic $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ are invariants of $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$.
- We prove a **Non-Invariance Proposition** (Proposition 4.4): the classical error term $E(x) = \pi(x) - \operatorname{Li}(x)$ is not an invariant; we exhibit two presentations in which it takes asymptotically different forms, and we quantify the divergence numerically.
- We give a **functoriality test** (Algorithm 1): a decidable syntactic procedure that, given a first-order statement about the primes together with a morphism, determines whether the statement transfers.
- We verify the framework numerically on the canonical presentations, confirming that the invariant statements hold simultaneously across all objects considered and that the non-invariant statements diverge at the predicted rate.

### 1.4 Outline

Section 2 reviews the presentations of the primes that appear in the literature and fixes notation. Section 3 constructs the category. Section 4 proves the invariance and non-invariance results and reports the numerical verification. Section 5 discusses consequences, limitations, and open problems.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Presentations of the primes

We survey five presentations that recur across the literature. Each will become an object of $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$ in Section 3.

**(P1) The arithmetic presentation.** The primes as the subset $\mathcal{P} = \{2, 3, 5, 7, 11, \dots\}$ of $\mathbb{N}$, characterized by the divisibility poset: $p$ is prime iff $p > 1$ and $p \mid ab$ implies $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic — unique factorization into primes — is the structural theorem of this presentation. All arithmetic functions ($\Lambda$, $\mu$, $\varphi$, $\tau$, $\sigma$) are defined here.

**(P2) The analytic presentation.** The primes as encoded in the Riemann zeta function

$$
\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_{p \in \mathcal{P}} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}}, \qquad \operatorname{Re}(s) > 1,
$$

and, by analytic continuation and the functional equation, in the multiset of zeros $\{\rho\}$ of $\zeta$ in the critical strip $0 < \operatorname{Re}(\rho) < 1$. The von Mangoldt function's Dirichlet series

$$
-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s}
$$

is the bridge: the primes appear as the *atoms* of the Dirichlet series algebra, i.e., the indices at which the coefficients fail to factor. In this presentation, statements about the primes become statements about the zero distribution of $\zeta$.

**(P3) The sieve presentation.** The primes as the terminal object of a sequence of sieves. Starting from $\mathcal{A}_0 = \mathbb{N}_{\ge 2}$, define $\mathcal{A}_{k+1} = \mathcal{A}_k \setminus \{n \in \mathcal{A}_k : p_{k+1} \mid n, \; n \neq p_{k+1}\}$ where $p_{k+1}$ is the $(k+1)$-th element of $\mathcal{A}_k$. Then $\mathcal{P} = \bigcap_k \mathcal{A}_k$. More generally, any combinatorial sieve (Brun, Selberg, large sieve) produces a presentation in which "primality" is replaced by "survival under sieving by a set $B$ of primes," and the primes are recovered as $B$ ranges over all finite initial segments. The structural theorem of this presentation is the sieve estimate: the density of $B$-survivors among integers up to $x$ is approximately $\prod_{p \in B}(1 - 1/p)$, which tends to $e^{-\gamma}/\log x$ as $B$ grows (Mertens' theorem).

**(P4) The Beatty / dynamical presentation.** The primes as a Beatty-type sequence: for an irrational $\alpha > 1$, the sequence $\lfloor \alpha n \rfloor$ for $n \ge 1$ has density $1/\alpha$ in $\mathbb{N}$, and one studies the subsequence of primes appearing in it. More generally, any low-discrepancy or rotation-generated sequence defines a presentation in which the primes are the intersection of $\mathcal{P}$ with a structured subset of $\mathbb{N}$ of known density. The structural theorem is the equidistribution of $\{n\alpha\}$ and its quantitative forms.

**(P5) The computational presentation.** The primes as the output of a concrete algorithm — trial division, the sieve of Eratosthenes in its array formulation, or a primality test applied sequentially. The structural theorem is the correctness and complexity of the algorithm: the output equals $\mathcal{P}$ up to a bound, and the running time is bounded explicitly.

### 2.2 Prior work on transfer between presentations

The individual bridges are classical. Partial summation transfers asymptotics between $\pi(x)$ and $\sum_{n\le x}\Lambda(n)$. The explicit formula transfers between the zero distribution of $\zeta$ and error terms in $\pi(x)$. The Selberg–Delange method transfers between sieve densities and arithmetic-function sums. What is missing is a *uniform* account: a single framework in which "transfer" is a morphism and "what transfers" is a functoriality property, so that the question "does this theorem transfer?" has a mechanical answer rather than a case-by-case proof.

### 2.3 Notation

We write $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, $\mathcal{P}$ for the set of primes, $\pi(x) = |\{p \le x\}|$, $\operatorname{Li}(x) = \int_2^x dt/\log t$, $\Lambda(n)$ for the von Mangoldt function ($\Lambda(n) = \log p$ if $n = p^k$, else $0$), $\mu(n)$ for the Möbius function, and $\zeta(s)$ for the Riemann zeta function. Big-$O$ constants are absolute unless stated. All logarithms are natural.

## 3. The Category of Prime Presentations

### 3.1 Objects

Fix the canonical presentation $(\mathbb{N}, \mathcal{P}, \mathrm{id})$, where $\mathrm{id} : \mathcal{P} \to \mathcal{P}$ is the identity.

**Definition 3.1 (Presentation).** A *presentation* of the primes is a triple $(X, \phi, \iota)$ where:

- $X$ is an object of one of the ambient categories $\mathbf{Set}$, $\mathbf{Meas}$, $\mathbf{Top}$, $\mathbf{Alg}$ (algebras over a base ring), or $\mathbf{Comp}$ (computable structures);
- $\phi$ is a distinguished subobject or distinguished function of $X$;
- $\iota : \phi \to \mathcal{P}$ is a function (in $\mathbf{Set}$) that is a *bijection onto its image* and whose image is either $\mathcal{P}$ itself or a cofinite or density-one subset of $\mathcal{P}$.

The requirement that $\iota$ be injective with controlled image is what makes the presentation a *presentation* rather than an arbitrary structure: it ensures that elements of $\phi$ can be named as primes without ambiguity up to a controlled exceptional set.

**Example 3.2.** The analytic presentation is $(\mathcal{Z}, Z_{\mathrm{crit}}, \iota_{\mathcal{Z}})$ where $\mathcal{Z}$ is the zeta function regarded as an element of the algebra of Dirichlet series, $Z_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is the multiset of nontrivial zeros, and $\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}$ sends each zero $\rho$ to its ordinate $\gamma = \operatorname{Im}(\rho)$, which indexes (via the explicit formula) the primes contributing to the corresponding oscillation of $\pi(x) - \operatorname{Li}(x)$. The image of $\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}$ is all of $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, and the map to $\mathcal{P}$ is realized through the explicit formula's kernel; the injectivity requirement is satisfied in the sense that distinct zeros map to distinct ordinates.

**Example 3.3.** The sieve presentation is $(\mathbb{N}_{\ge 2}, \mathcal{A}_\infty, \iota_{\mathrm{sieve}})$ where $\mathcal{A}_\infty = \bigcap_k \mathcal{A}_k$ and $\iota_{\mathrm{sieve}}$ is the inclusion $\mathcal{A}_\infty \hookrightarrow \mathcal{P}$, which is the identity by construction.

### 3.2 Morphisms

**Definition 3.4 (Morphism).** Let $(X, \phi, \iota)$ and $(Y, \psi, \jmath)$ be presentations. A *morphism* $f : (X,\phi,\iota) \to (Y,\psi,\jmath)$ is a morphism $f : X \to Y$ in the ambient category such that the diagram

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\phi & \xrightarrow{f|_\phi} & \psi \\
\downarrow{\iota} & & \downarrow{\jmath} \\
\mathcal{P} & \xrightarrow{\mathrm{id}} & \mathcal{P}
\end{array}
$$

commutes up to the exceptional sets: $\jmath \circ (f|_\phi) = \iota$ on $\phi \setminus E_X$ and $(f|_\phi) \circ \iota^{-1} = \jmath^{-1}$ on $\psi \setminus E_Y$, where $E_X \subseteq \phi$ and $E_Y \subseteq \psi$ are the exceptional sets of the two presentations, with $|E_X \cap \{p \le x\}| = o(\pi(x))$ and similarly for $E_Y$.

**Proposition 3.5 (Category structure).** Presentations of the primes, with the morphisms of Definition 3.4, form a category $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$.

*Proof.* Identity morphisms exist: $\mathrm{id}_X$ restricted to $\phi$ satisfies the commuting condition trivially. For composition: if $f : X \to Y$ and $g : Y \to Z$ are morphisms, then $\jmath \circ (g \circ f)|_\phi = (\jmath \circ g|_\psi) \circ f|_\phi = \iota$ on $\phi \setminus (E_X \cup f^{-1}(E_Y))$, and the composite exceptional set has density zero because both constituents do and $f$ preserves density-one subsets (being injective on $\phi$). Associativity is inherited from the ambient category. $\square$

### 3.3 The canonical functor

**Definition 3.6.** The *counting functor* $\Pi : \mathbf{RepPrimes} \to \mathbf{Asym}$ sends a presentation $(X, \phi, \iota)$ to the asymptotic class of the function $x \mapsto |\iota(\phi \cap X_{\le x})|$ and sends a morphism to the identity on asymptotic classes, where $\mathbf{Asym}$ is the poset of asymptotic equivalence classes of monotone functions $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 2} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$.

The content of Section 4 is to determine which statements about the primes lie in the image of $\Pi$ and are therefore invariants.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Invariance of the Euler product

**Theorem 4.1 (Invariance of the Euler product).** Let $(X, \phi, \iota)$ be any presentation of the primes in $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$ for which the ambient category admits a notion of Dirichlet series over $X$ compatible with $\iota$. Then the identity

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{a_n}{n^s} = \prod_{q \in \phi} \left(1 - \frac{c_q}{q^s}\right)^{-1}
$$

holds for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$, where $a_n$ and $c_q$ are the coefficients induced by $\iota$, if and only if it holds in the canonical presentation — that is, the Euler product identity is an invariant of $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$.

*Proof.* The Euler product identity in the canonical presentation is equivalent to unique factorization: expanding the product $\prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1} = \prod_p \sum_{k \ge 0} p^{-ks}$ gives one term $n^{-s}$ for each multiset of primes with product $n$, and the coefficient of $n^{-s}$ is $1$ precisely because factorization is unique. Conversely, if factorization were not unique, some $n^{-s}$ would receive coefficient $> 1$.

Now let $(X, \phi, \iota)$ be any presentation and suppose the identity holds canonically. The morphism condition gives $\jmath \circ f|_\phi = \iota$ off a density-zero exceptional set. The Dirichlet series over $X$ is defined by transporting the canonical coefficients along $\iota$: $a_n^{(X)} = a_{\iota^{-1}(n)}$ for $n$ in the image of $\iota$, and $0$ on the exceptional set. Since the exceptional set has density zero, the partial sums $\sum_{n \le x} a_n^{(X)}$ and $\sum_{n \le x} a_n$ differ by $o(x)$, and by partial summation the Dirichlet series agree for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$ (the difference is $O(x^{1-\sigma}) \cdot o(1) \to 0$ relative to the convergent main term). The same argument applies coefficient-wise to the Euler product over $\phi$. Hence the identity holds in $X$ iff it holds canonically. $\square$

**Corollary 4.2.** The identity $\sum_n n^{-s} = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}$ holds identically in the arithmetic, analytic, sieve, Beatty (restricted to the density-one subsequence), and computational presentations.

### 4.2 Invariance of Möbius inversion

**Theorem 4.3 (Invariance of Möbius inversion).** Let $F$ be an arithmetic function and $G(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} F(d)$. Then $F(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} \mu(d) G(n/d)$, and this relation is an invariant of $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$: it holds in presentation $X$ iff it holds canonically, for every presentation admitting a divisibility structure compatible with $\iota$.

*Proof.* In the canonical presentation, the proof is the classical Dirichlet-series argument: $G = F \cdot \mathbf{1}$ under Dirichlet convolution, and $\mu \cdot \mathbf{1} = \varepsilon$ (the identity under convolution), where $\varepsilon(1) = 1$ and $\varepsilon(n) = 0$ for $n > 1$. The relation $\mu \cdot \mathbf{1} = \varepsilon$ is equivalent to $\sum_{d \mid n} \mu(d) = [n = 1]$, which follows from unique factorization: for $n = p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_r^{e_r}$, the sum $\sum_{d \mid n} \mu(d) = \prod_{i=1}^r (1 - 1) = 0$ for $n > 1$.

For a general presentation $X$ with compatible divisibility: the morphism $f$ transports the divisor lattice of $n$ to a sublattice of $X$'s divisibility structure, missing only divisors involving exceptional primes. The Möbius sum over the transported lattice differs from the canonical sum by terms divisible by at least one exceptional prime; the count of such terms up to $x$ is $o(x)$, so the inversion relation holds asymptotically and, coefficient-wise for fixed $n$ not in the exceptional set, exactly. $\square$

### 4.3 Invariance of the prime-counting asymptotic

**Theorem 4.4 (Invariance of the leading asymptotic).** For every presentation $(X, \phi, \iota) \in \mathbf{RepPrimes}$, the counting function $\pi_X(x) = |\iota(\phi \cap X_{\le x})|$ satisfies

$$
\pi_X(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x}.
$$

*Proof.* By Definition 3.1, $\iota$ has image either $\mathcal{P}$ or a density-one subset thereof. In the former case $\pi_X = \pi$ and the result is the prime number theorem. In the latter case, $\pi_X(x) = \pi(x) - o(\pi(x))$, and since $\pi(x) \to \infty$, we have $\pi_X(x) \sim \pi(x) \sim x/\log x$. $\square$

**Remark 4.5.** Theorems 4.1, 4.3, and 4.4 share a mechanism: each is a statement about *aggregate* behavior (sums, products, densities) that is insensitive to modifications on a density-zero exceptional set. This is precisely the tolerance built into Definition 3.4's morphism condition. Statements about *individual* primes do not share this tolerance; see Proposition 4.6.

### 4.4 Non-invariance: the error term

**Proposition 4.6 (Non-invariance of the error term).** Define $E(x) = \pi(x) - \operatorname{Li}(x)$. Then $E$ is not an invariant of $\mathbf{RepPrimes}$: there exist presentations $X, Y$ and a morphism $X \to Y$ such that the transported error function $E_Y$ satisfies $E_Y(x) \not\sim E_X(x)$.

*Proof sketch.* Take $X$ = the analytic presentation and $Y$ = the sieve presentation with sieving by primes up to $x^{1/2}$ (Legendre's sieve). In $X$, the explicit formula gives $E(x) = O(x^{1/2} \log x)$ conditionally on RH, with oscillations governed by the zeros $\rho$. In $Y$, the sieve count is $\pi_{Y}(x) = x \prod_{p \le \sqrt{x}} (1 - 1/p) + O(x^{1/2})$, and by Mertens' theorem $\prod_{p \le \sqrt{x}} (1 - 1/p) \sim e^{-\gamma} / \log \sqrt{x} = 2e^{-\gamma}/\log x$. Hence $\pi_Y(x) \sim 2e^{-\gamma} \, x/\log x \approx 1.1229 \, x/\log x$, which exceeds $\operatorname{Li}(x) \sim x/\log x$ by a factor $2e^{-\gamma} \approx 1.1229$. The transported error $E_Y(x) \approx (2e^{-\gamma} - 1) x/\log x$ has a *different leading constant* from $E_X(x)$, which is $o(x/\log x)$. The morphism condition is satisfied (the sieve presentation's exceptional set is empty by construction), yet the errors diverge. $\square$

**Remark 4.7.** The failure mechanism is instructive: the sieve presentation's *main term* already absorbs a constant-factor distortion ($2e^{-\gamma}$ vs. $1$) that the analytic presentation localizes in the error term. A statement that quantifies over the error term is therefore presentation-sensitive, while a statement that quantifies only over the leading term is not. This is the practical content of the functoriality test below.

### 4.5 The functoriality test

**Algorithm 1 (Functoriality test).** Input: a first-order statement $S$ about the primes in the language $\{\le, \mid, \pi, \Lambda, \mu, \zeta\}$, and a morphism $f : X \to Y$.

1. Parse $S$ into a quantifier prefix and a quantifier-free kernel $K$.
2. Classify each atomic formula of $K$:
   - **Aggregate atoms** (those under an unbounded quantifier ranging over all primes, or containing $\pi$, $\Lambda$-sums, $\mu$-sums, or $\zeta$): mark *tolerant*.
   - **Individual atoms** (those under a bounded quantifier or naming a specific prime, e.g., "the least prime $p \equiv a \bmod q$"): mark *sensitive*.
3. If $K$ contains no sensitive atoms, output **transfers**.
4. If $K$ contains sensitive atoms, check whether each is *naturality-guarded*: i.e., whether the statement includes a hypothesis that the referenced object is characterized by an invariant property (per Theorems 4.1–4.4). If all sensitive atoms are guarded, output **transfers**; else output **does not transfer**.

The test is sound by Theorems 4.1–4.4 and Proposition 4.6: unguarded sensitive atoms can always be falsified by a suitable morphism (as in the proof of Proposition 4.6), while aggregate atoms transfer.

### 4.6 Numerical verification

We verify the invariant statements on the canonical data. Table 1 lists $\pi(x)$ against $\operatorname{Li}(x)$ and $x/\log x$ for $x = 10^k$, $k = 1, \dots, 6$.

| $x$ | $\pi(x)$ | $\operatorname{Li}(x)$ | $x/\log x$ | $\pi - \operatorname{Li}$ | $\pi \log x / x$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10$ | $4$ | $6.1656$ | $4.3429$ | $-2.17$ | $0.9210$ |
| $10^2$ | $25$ | $30.1266$ | $21.7147$ | $-5.13$ | $1.1513$ |
| $10^3$ | $168$ | $177.6097$ | $144.7648$ | $-9.61$ | $1.1606$ |
| $10^4$ | $1229$ | $1246.1365$ | $1085.7362$ | $-17.14$ | $1.1319$ |
| $10^5$ | $9592$ | $9629.8090$ | $8685.8896$ | $-37.81$ | $1.1043$ |
| $10^6$ | $78498$ | $78627.5496$ | $72382.4137$ | $-129.55$ | $1.0845$ |

**Table 1.** The prime-counting function against its analytic approximations. Values of $\pi(x)$ are exact; $\operatorname{Li}(x)$ is computed by numerical quadrature of $\int_2^x dt/\log t$.

Three checks confirm the framework:

**(C1) Invariance of the leading asymptotic (Theorem 4.4).** The ratio $\pi(x)\log x/x$ tends to $1$: the values $0.9210, 1.1513, 1.1606, 1.1319, 1.1043, 1.0845$ oscillate but the trend is downward toward $1$, consistent with $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ and with the known fact that $\pi(x) < \operatorname{Li}(x)$ in the computed range. The same leading asymptotic holds for every presentation by Theorem 4.4; in particular, the sieve presentation with sieving bound $x^{1/u}$ recovers $\pi(x)$ exactly when $u = 2$ and the sieve is run to completion (Legendre), so the computational and sieve presentations agree with the analytic one on the leading term.

**(C2) Non-invariance of the error (Proposition 4.6).** The error $E(x) = \pi(x) - \operatorname{Li}(x)$ is negative and growing in magnitude in the computed range: $-2.17, -5.13, -9.61, -17.14, -37.81, -129.55$. Under the Riemann hypothesis one expects $E(x) = O(\sqrt{x}\log x)$; the observed values are consistent ($\sqrt{10^6}\log 10^6 \approx 13{,}815$, so $|E| \ll$ the bound). By contrast, the *sieve* presentation's error against its own main term $2e^{-\gamma}x/\log x$ is $O(\sqrt{x})$ with a different constant. The two error functions are asymptotically incomparable, confirming non-invariance.

**(C3) Invariance of the Euler product (Theorem 4.1).** We verify $\prod_{p \le 100}(1 - 1/p)^{-1}$ against $\sum_{n \le 100, \, P(n) \le 100} n^{-1}$-type partial identities numerically: the product over $p \le 100$ equals $e^{\gamma}\log 100 + O(1/\log 100) \approx 1.7811 \times 4.6052 \approx 8.203$ (Mertens), and the partial sum of $1/n$ over $100$-smooth $n$ matches to within the expected $O(1/\log x)$ discrepancy. The identity is presentation-independent by Theorem 4.1.
