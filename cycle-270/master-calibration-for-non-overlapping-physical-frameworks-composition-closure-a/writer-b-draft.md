# Master Calibration for Non-Overlapping Physical Frameworks: Composition, Closure, and the Limits of Partial Translation

## Abstract

Physics presents itself as convergent, yet systematic inventories of its foundations list 29 unresolved bifurcations—general relativity versus quantum mechanics, continuum versus discrete description, the Heisenberg cut, the status of the observer. We propose that such schisms are symptoms of frameworks that are each internally self-consistent over disjoint domains, lacking a *master calibration*: a family of partial translation maps between frameworks that carries quantified error and composes consistently. We formalize frameworks as calibrated theories over domains, translations as partial maps with fractional error, and composition failure as cycle-closure error around loops in the framework graph. We derive closed-form bounds on composed translation error, prove by counting that pairwise consistency cannot imply global consistency at scale, and give a classification criterion separating structural schisms (no admissible translation within a stated error budget) from conventional ones (translations exist but are costly). Applying the machinery to the 29-bifurcation inventory, we compute 406 pairwise translation maps subject to 3654 composition constraints (a constraint-to-map ratio of exactly 9.0), a worked three-edge closure budget of $3.4965 \times 10^{-3}$, and—under a labeled independence projection with compatibility probability $p = 0.9$—that random composition of all squares succeeds with probability $\approx 10^{-167.2}$, with the largest self-calibrating family size $n_{\max} = 7$. The Feynman-Shor bifurcation of quantum computing serves as a worked two-framework case with zero composition constraints. The practical upshot: beyond small families, global consistency must be enforced by construction, and candidate unifications must be checked against closure criteria rather than asserted.

## 1. Introduction

The working scientist, the funding agency, and the philosopher of physics all face the same practical blockage, at different scales. A physicist trying to combine general relativity with quantum mechanics has no agreed procedure for even stating what a translation error between the two would be; a program manager deciding between competing experimental facilities has no formal way to tell whether two research programs disagree structurally or merely conventionally; a community prioritization process must aggregate inputs from subfields that share no common observable. Today these decisions are made by intuition and committee. What is missing is a quantitative theory of *when two internally successful frameworks can be made commensurable at all*, and at what cost. If the results of this paper hold, the decision changes in a concrete way: a proposed unification or translation between frameworks could be checked against an explicit closure-error budget, and a schism could be classified as structural (no translation chain meets the budget) or conventional (a chain exists but is expensive) before resources are committed.

The motivating evidence is an inventory rather than a single puzzle. A systematic audit of physics foundations reports 29 unresolved bifurcations and hidden assumptions, ranging from the nature of the continuum to the status of the observer, the consistency of mathematical language, and the meaning of explanation itself [10]. The same audit notes the paradox that sharpens the problem: quantum electrodynamics predicts the electron's magnetic moment to eleven decimal places and general relativity describes binary-pulsar orbital decay to within seconds per century, yet general relativity and quantum mechanics use mathematically incompatible structures and the cosmological constant sits 120 orders of magnitude from naive expectations [10]. Each tower is internally superb; the cracks run between towers. A second inventory documents the same structure inside a living field: quantum computing has split into two ontologies, a Feynman-lineage path of mimetic simulation that is inherently robust to noise and viable on current hardware, and a Shor-lineage path of algorithmic calculation that remains practically intractable without fault tolerance because phase estimation is fragile [11]. These are not failures of either path; they are two self-consistent programs whose translation is the open problem.

The word *calibration* is borrowed deliberately from metrology and engineering, where it has an operational meaning: a calibration is a procedure that renders the outputs of one instrument commensurable with another, with quantified residual error. Engineering routinely calibrates instruments with *non-overlapping fields of view*: a targetless extrinsic calibration of a stereo camera and a LiDAR sharing no common observable achieves approximately $0.02\ \mathrm{m}$ and $0.086^{\circ}$ by exploiting road markings as a mediating structure visible to both [3]. Microwave metrology shows that a standard previously thought essential can be eliminated: a thru-free multiline calibration for vector network analyzers removes the thru standard by using an arbitrary transmissive two-port device plus a reflect standard, and agrees excellently with conventional multiline TRL when both are available [8]. Machine learning treats calibration as a relational property between model and domain: calibration across multiple domains can be viewed as a special case of an invariant representation, and in a linear-Gaussian setting multi-domain calibration provably guarantees out-of-domain generalization [5]. Cosmology identifies calibration precision itself as a limiting systematic, with two coexisting paradigms—sky-based and redundant—resting on different assumptions for the same instrument class [6]. These are small-scale instances of exactly the problem physics faces at its foundations, and each supplies a mechanism this paper formalizes.

The central objects are:

1. **Frameworks** $F_i$, each a self-consistent theory over a domain $D_i$.
2. **Partial translation maps** $\tau_{ij}: D_i \supset S_{ij} \to D_j$, defined only where admissible, with quantified translation error $\varepsilon_{ij}$.
3. **Composition conditions**: when does $\tau_{jk} \circ \tau_{ij}$ agree with $\tau_{ik}$?
4. **Master calibration**: a family $\{\tau_{ij}\}$ that composes consistently for all pairs simultaneously.

Our contributions are: (i) formal definitions of framework, translation, calibration failure, and the structural-versus-conventional classification of schisms, derived in Section 3; (ii) counting and error-accumulation results showing that pairwise calibration does not guarantee global consistency, with the 29-framework inventory yielding 406 maps under 3654 constraints, derived in Section 4 and reported in Section 5; (iii) closed-form closure-error budgets for translation chains and cycles, including the chain-length bound $k \leq \ln(1-\tau)/\ln(1-\varepsilon_{\min})$, derived in Section 4; (iv) a genericity projection showing that beyond roughly seven mutually calibrated frameworks, composition must be enforced by construction, derived in Section 4 and labeled a projection; and (v) a worked two-framework application to the Feynman-Shor bifurcation [11], showing it carries zero composition constraints and is therefore conventional-or-structural at the pair level only, in Sections 4 and 5.

## 2. Background and Related Work

We organize the related work into four strands: calibration with non-overlapping coverage in engineering, calibration as a limiting systematic in cosmology, calibration under domain shift in machine learning, and the physics-side inventories and facility proposals that motivate and constrain the master-calibration conjecture.

**Calibration with non-overlapping coverage.** The closest engineering analogue to our central problem is the targetless extrinsic calibration of a stereo camera and a LiDAR with a non-overlapping field of view, which exploits road markings as static and robust features among otherwise dynamic objects [3]. Two instruments, each internally consistent, share no common observable, yet a translation (an extrinsic rigid-body transform) is required to use them jointly; the reported accuracy of approximately $0.02\ \mathrm{m}$ and $0.086^{\circ}$ without manual operation demonstrates that a quantified error budget is attainable even without overlap, provided a mediating structure exists. The method's cost analysis adds a mechanism we import: the edge-based cost is noisier but has a broader convergence segment than the NID cost, so it tolerates larger initial calibration error [3]—an instance of choosing a translation whose error landscape is forgiving, which is precisely the engineering content of our conventional-schism class. In the same register, [8] proposes a thru-free multiline calibration for vector network analyzers, eliminating the thru (or line) standard by using an arbitrary transmissive two-port device together with an additional reflect standard; the motivation is that in on-wafer and waveguide applications a fully defined thru cannot be realized at the required calibration plane. The verification is direct: both methods yield the same normalized error terms because a thru definition is not required in the eigenvalue formulation, and measurements over $1$–$150\ \mathrm{GHz}$ with 299 frequency points show excellent agreement between the thru-free method and multiline TRL [8]. This is evidence that some calibration constraints are conventional rather than structural: an apparently essential common standard can be replaced by a weaker, more available one while preserving the internal consistency of the calibration family—the exact move our master calibration would make between physical frameworks, replacing an impossible direct standard (a shared domain) with an indirect chain of weaker standards.

**Calibration as a limiting systematic.** In 21 cm cosmology, calibration precision is currently a limiting systematic, and most calibration approaches fall into two classes: sky-based, relying on an extremely accurate model of astronomical foreground emission, and redundant, requiring a precisely regular array with near-identical antenna response patterns [6]. The stakes are quantified in the same work: astrophysical foreground emission is 4–5 orders of magnitude brighter than the cosmological signal, so separating them requires extremely precise instrumental calibration [6]. Two paradigms with different assumptions—external foreground model versus internal array regularity—coexist for one instrument class, a miniature physics schism; and the simulation results in [6] illustrate that a unified approach combining redundant calibration with a sky-model prior can improve calibration performance, i.e., that a framework containing both paradigms can outperform either. This is the small-scale existence proof for our master-calibration conjecture: a unifying layer above two calibration paradigms is not only conceivable but demonstrably beneficial in at least one domain.

**Calibration under domain shift.** In machine learning, [5] draws a link between out-of-domain generalization and model calibration, arguing that calibration across multiple domains can be viewed as a special case of an invariant representation; the work defines calibration on multiple environments formally ($\mathbb{E}[Y \mid f(X)=\alpha, E=e_i] = \alpha$ for all environments $e_i$), proves in a linear-Gaussian setting that models calibrated on multiple domains are free of spurious correlations and therefore generalize out of domain, and proposes a training objective that directly penalizes multi-domain calibration error [5]. This is the most direct conceptual precedent for the master-calibration notion: being calibrated is relational (model-plus-domain), and the multi-domain condition is strictly stronger than any collection of in-domain conditions—an algebraic shadow of our composition constraints. Complementarily, [7] addresses uncertainty calibration under domain drift: standard deep networks yield uncalibrated predictions, post-hoc methods produce calibrated confidence scores in-domain, but the quality of uncertainty decreases with increasing domain shift for all evaluated methods, resulting in highly overconfident predictions far from the training domain; replacing the validation set by a transformed validation set, sampled over 28 perturbation types, substantially improves calibration under drift [7]. The lesson we import is exact: a framework calibrated on its home domain may be uncalibrated under translation, and post-hoc correction of the translation maps—not a new monolithic theory—is the realistic remedy.

**Community-scale priority setting and shared observables.** At the scale of the discipline, the European Particle Physics Strategy Update takes a bottom-up approach in which the community first submits proposals (inputs) for projects to be realised in the near-term, mid-term and longer-term future, with national and laboratory inputs also important elements [1]. Theory choice at community scale is thus already an aggregation problem over partially incommensurable inputs; foundational schisms, being cross-cutting rather than facility-specific, fit awkwardly into such a process. The same strategy document records the substantive content over which schisms bite: the list of Standard-Model-external puzzles (electroweak symmetry breaking, the Higgs nature, quark and lepton mass patterns, neutrino nature; dark matter, dark energy, inflation, the matter-antimatter asymmetry) did not change in the post-Run 2 LHC era, and progress proceeds by two complementary routes—raising the energy scale of direct searches, or precision measurements of rare processes at much lower energies [1]. The precision route has explicit calibration targets: oblique parameters measured with a precision of $\sim 1\%$, about a factor 10 better than current precision [1]—a concrete tolerance $\tau$ of the kind our classification criterion requires. As a proposed shared observable, [2] presents the design and physics program of an $e^+e^-$ linear collider of center-of-mass energy 500 GeV to 1 TeV, demonstrating its key role in exploring physics beyond the Standard Model and the feasibility of constructing the machine; the preface states the design range as 0.5–1.5 TeV and identifies the missing piece the machine targets: the agent that breaks the symmetry on which the weak interactions are based, which must provide new phenomena at the TeV scale [2]. In our vocabulary such a facility is a manufactured overlap between frameworks—an attempt to create a common domain where none exists naturally—and Section 4 quantifies how partial such an overlap is (a factor-of-2 to factor-of-3 energy window). In a different regime, [4] analyzes the physical designs of CFETR and HFRC, described as the two major projects representative of the low-density steady-state and high-density pulsed pathways to fusion; the MHD assessment predicts the ECCD power and current required for full NTM stabilization for CFETR, finds that for 80% of steady-state-operation scenarios active control methods may be required for RWM stabilization, and simulates disruption mitigation by massive neon injection with time scales found equivalent to ITER [4]. Two internally coherent programs whose joint assessment is the open task form a concrete small-scale analogue of the schism structure we formalize.

**The schisms inventory and the prior formalization.** The inventory that motivates this paper reports 29 unresolved bifurcations and hidden assumptions, from the nature of the continuum to the status of the observer, the consistency of mathematical language, and the meaning of explanation itself [10]. Its own response is a self-referential calibration framework: a Bootstrap Theorem asserting that if the distinction function is ultrametric, the calibration operator $\Phi$ is a contraction mapping on the space of theories, so by the Banach fixed-point theorem a unique fixed point $T^* = \Phi(T^*)$ exists [10]. We treat this as a candidate master calibration in our sense—a single structure intended to close all cycles—and our composition analysis supplies a criterion such a candidate must satisfy independently of its internal machinery. The prior formalization of the master-calibration conjecture [9] introduces the definitions we refine here (framework as a pair $F_i = (D_i, \mathcal{P}_i)$; mediating domains following the strategy of [3]) and reports the counting results we recompute and extend in Section 4: $\binom{29}{2} = 406$ maps, $\binom{29}{3} = 3654$ constraints, ratio exactly 9.0, and the excess of at least $29 - 21 = 8$ schisms over a seven-domain pairwise reduction [9]. Our contribution relative to [9] is to make the error algebra explicit, add the chain-length bound and the genericity projection with full arithmetic, and add a two-framework worked case from [11] that the prior version did not treat. Finally, [11] supplies that case: quantum computing's Feynman-Shor bifurcation, in which path A (mimetic simulation, rooted in Feynman's 1982 vision of structural isomorphism, mapping the target Hilbert space directly onto the simulator's own) is inherently robust to noise and viable on current hardware, while path B (algorithmic calculation, defined by Shor's 1994 factoring algorithm) remains practically intractable due to the fragility of phase estimation without fault tolerance [11]. This is a live, documented two-framework schism with named primitives on each side, and Section 4 shows it occupies the trivial corner of our constraint combinatorics.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Frameworks and domains

**Definition 3.1 (Framework).** A framework is a pair $F_i = (D_i, \mathcal{P}_i)$ where $D_i$ is a domain—a set of states, configurations, or propositions the framework speaks about—and $\mathcal{P}_i$ is a set of primitives: quantities, operations, or axioms the framework treats as given without further calibration. A framework is *internally consistent* if no contradiction is derivable within $D_i$ using $\mathcal{P}_i$. We assume each framework in our motivating set satisfies internal consistency, since the schisms arise at the boundaries, not inside [9].

Examples: non-relativistic quantum mechanics $F_{\mathrm{QM}}$ with primitives including the Hilbert-space state $|\psi\rangle$ and unitary evolution $U_t = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$; classical statistical mechanics $F_{\mathrm{SM}}$ with primitives including phase-space measure and coarse-graining cells; general relativity $F_{\mathrm{GR}}$ with the metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ as primitive [9]. In the Feynman-Shor case [11], path A has as primitive the structural isomorphism between target and simulator Hamiltonians, and path B has as primitive the idealized noiseless logical operation.

### 3.2 Translation maps and error

**Definition 3.2 (Translation map).** A translation from $F_i$ to $F_j$ is a partial map $\tau_{ij}: S_{ij} \to D_j$ with $S_{ij} \subseteq D_i$, together with a calibration certificate specifying how primitives of $F_i$ are expressed in primitives of $F_j$ on $S_{ij}$. The map carries a *translation error* $\varepsilon_{ij} \in [0,1)$, defined operationally: for a proposition $p$ in the overlap of meaning, $\tau_{ij}(p)$ disagrees with the best available $F_j$-native rendering of $p$ on a fraction $\varepsilon_{ij}$ of a chosen test battery. Symmetry is not assumed: $\varepsilon_{ij} \neq \varepsilon_{ji}$ in general, since calibration is directional, as when the sky-based and redundant paradigms of [6] lean on different assumptions for the same instrument.

Two canonical examples, both standard physics rather than novel claims: decoherence supplies a quantum-to-classical translation $\tau_{\mathrm{QM}\to\mathrm{cl}}$ valid on the sector of states where environment-induced superselection holds; coarse-graining supplies a micro-to-macro translation $\tau_{\mu\to M}$ valid above a resolution scale $\ell_{\mathrm{cg}}$.

### 3.3 Composition and closure

For composable translations $\tau_{ij}$ and $\tau_{jk}$, the composed error is bounded by

$$\varepsilon_{ij \circ jk} \;\leq\; \varepsilon_{ij} + \varepsilon_{jk} + \varepsilon_{ij}\,\varepsilon_{jk},$$

the standard first-order composition bound for independent fractional errors, which we adopt as the error structure of the translation semigroupoid. **Definition 3.3 (Composition failure).** Composition *fails* if there exists $x \in \tau_{ij}^{-1}(S_{jk}) \cap S_{ik}$ such that $(\tau_{jk} \circ \tau_{ij})(x) \neq \tau_{ik}(x)$. For a cycle $\gamma: F_{i_1} \to F_{i_2} \to \cdots \to F_{i_n} \to F_{i_1}$, the *closure error* is

$$\Delta_{\gamma} \;\leq\; 1 - \prod_{e \in \gamma} (1 - \varepsilon_e),$$

where $\varepsilon_e$ are the edge errors. A framework graph is *globally calibrated* to tolerance $\tau$ if $\Delta_{\gamma} \leq \tau$ for all cycles $\gamma$.

**Definition 3.4 (Master calibration).** A family $\mathcal{T} = \{\tau_{ij}\}_{i<j}$ is a master calibration if every square commutes: $\tau_{jk} \circ \tau_{ij} = \tau_{ik}$ on $\tau_{ij}^{-1}(S_{jk}) \cap S_{ik}$.

### 3.4 Mediating domains

Following the strategy validated in [3]—calibrating two sensors with non-overlapping fields of view through a structure (road markings) visible to both—we define a *mediating domain* $M$ with translations $\tau_{iM}: S_{iM} \to M$ and $\tau_{Mj}: M \supset \tau_{iM}(S_{iM}) \to D_j$. The mediated translation is $\tau_{Mj} \circ \tau_{iM}$; it incurs two edge errors instead of one direct error, a price quantified in Section 4. The thru-free calibration of [8] is the complementary move: when the direct standard is unrealizable, an indirect chain of weaker standards preserves the calibration family's consistency.

### 3.5 Structural versus conventional schisms

**Definition 3.5.** A schism between $F_i$ and $F_j$ is *structural* at tolerance $\tau$ if no admissible translation chain from $i$ to $j$ achieves closure error $\Delta \leq \tau$ (or no nontrivial overlap $S_{ij}$ exists); it is *conventional* if such a chain exists but is costly—long, or requiring strong auxiliary assumptions such as the extremely accurate foreground model of the sky-based paradigm [6]. The classification is relative to $\tau$ and to available auxiliary structure, which is precisely where engineering ingenuity, as in the thru-free standard of [8], can convert a structural schism into a conventional one. The *master calibration problem* is: given frameworks $\{F_1, \dots, F_n\}$ and candidate translations, does there exist an assignment of edge errors such that all cycles close within $\tau$? We do not solve this in general; we derive the quantitative machinery and apply it to the corpus counts.

## 4. Analysis

All inputs below are stated with sources; all arithmetic is shown step by step. No empirical data are collected or used in this paper.

**Input 1 (from [10]):** the number of unresolved bifurcations is $N_{\mathrm{schism}} = 29$.

**Input 2 (from [11]):** the Feynman-Shor bifurcation partitions quantum computing into $m = 2$ ontologies (path A, mimetic simulation; path B, algorithmic calculation).

**Input 3 (from [2]):** the proposed linear collider center-of-mass energy range is $E_{\mathrm{cm}} = 500\ \mathrm{GeV}$ to $1\ \mathrm{TeV}$ (abstract) and $0.5$–$1.5\ \mathrm{TeV}$ (preface).

**Input 4 (from [6]):** calibration precision is a limiting systematic in 21 cm cosmology, with foregrounds 4–5 orders of magnitude brighter than the signal (qualitative; used to justify nonzero error floors, no number cited).

**Input 5 (from [1]):** oblique parameters at future $e^+e^-$ colliders are expected to be measured with a precision of $\sim 1\%$, about a factor 10 better than current precision.

**Derivation 1: counting maps and composition constraints.** Treating the 29 bifurcations as 29 frameworks requiring mutual calibration is a modeling choice; calibrating both sides of each dichotomy would give up to $2 \times 29 = 58$ towers, and we carry the conservative $N = 29$. The number of pairwise translation maps for a complete master calibration is

$$\binom{N}{2} = \frac{29 \times 28}{2} = \frac{812}{2} = 406.$$

Each triple $\{i,j,k\}$ yields one independent square constraint $\tau_{jk} \circ \tau_{ij} = \tau_{ik}$:

$$\binom{29}{3} = \frac{29 \times 28 \times 27}{6} = \frac{21924}{6} = 3654.$$

The constraint-to-map ratio is

$$\frac{3654}{406} = 9.0,$$

exactly, since $\binom{N}{3}/\binom{N}{2} = (N-2)/3 = 27/3 = 9$. Interpretation: each new framework added to a calibrated family of size $n$ contributes $n$ new maps but $\binom{n}{2}$ new constraints, so constraints grow quadratically in family size while maps grow linearly. A master calibration is therefore a strongly overdetermined system, and generic assignments of translations will not compose: pairwise consistency does not imply global consistency. Under the factor-2 alternative ($N = 58$): $\binom{58}{2} = 58 \times 57/2 = 1653$ maps and $\binom{58}{3} = 58 \times 57 \times 56/6 = 185136/6 = 30856$ constraints, ratio $56/3 = 18.666\ldots \approx 18.667$; the qualitative conclusion is unchanged.

**Derivation 2: the two-framework corner.** For the Feynman-Shor bifurcation [11] with $m = 2$ frameworks, the number of unordered pairs is $\binom{2}{2} = 1$ and the number of triple constraints is $\binom{2}{3} = 0$. A two-framework schism therefore carries no composition constraint at all: any single translation map, however poor, is trivially a "master calibration" for the pair, because there is no third framework to disagree with. The Feynman-Shor schism is thus conventional-or-structural at the pair level only; its difficulty lives entirely in the size of $\varepsilon_{AB}$ (the fragility of phase estimation on one side [11]) and not in overdetermination. This contrasts sharply with Derivation 1: the 29-framework inventory is hard *because of* the constraint count, while the two-path field is hard because of the error floor.

**Derivation 3: closure-error budget for a three-framework loop.** Take a loop $F_1 \to F_2 \to F_3 \to F_1$ with edge errors $\varepsilon_{12} = 10^{-3}$, $\varepsilon_{23} = 2 \times 10^{-3}$, $\varepsilon_{31} = 5 \times 10^{-4}$ (illustrative values, chosen far below unity and consistent with the qualitative claim of [6] that calibration is a limiting but not saturated systematic). The closure bound is

$$\Delta_{\gamma} \leq 1 - (1 - 10^{-3})(1 - 2 \times 10^{-3})(1 - 5 \times 10^{-4}).$$

Compute each factor: $(1 - 10^{-3}) = 0.999$; $(1 - 2 \times 10^{-3}) = 0.998$; $(1 - 5 \times 10^{-4}) = 0.9995$. Product: $0.999 \times 0.998 = 0.997002$; then $0.997002 \times 0.9995 = 0.997002 - 0.997002 \times 0.0005 = 0.997002 - 0.000498501 = 0.996503499$. Hence

$$\Delta_{\gamma} \leq 1 - 0.996503499 = 0.003496501 \approx 3.50 \times 10^{-3}.$$

Cross-check by the additive first-order bound: $\varepsilon_{12} + \varepsilon_{23} + \varepsilon_{31} = 10^{-3} + 2 \times 10^{-3} + 5 \times 10^{-4} = 3.5 \times 10^{-3}$, and the exact product bound $3.4965 \times 10^{-3}$ is indeed below the additive $3.5 \times 10^{-3}$, as expected since second-order terms are subtracted; the two agree to within $3.5 \times 10^{-6}$ in absolute terms.

The closure bound is
