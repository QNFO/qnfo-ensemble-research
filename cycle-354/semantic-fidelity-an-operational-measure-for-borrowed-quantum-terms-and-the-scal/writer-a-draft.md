# Semantic Fidelity: An Operational Measure for Borrowed Quantum Terms and Their Scale of Failure

## Abstract

Quantum‑computing projects routinely import terminology from quantum optics, condensed‑matter physics, and particle physics. When the ontological commitments of a borrowed term diverge from the circuit‑model formalism, engineers may make design decisions that are sub‑optimal or outright infeasible, contributing to multi‑billion‑dollar delays in hardware roll‑out. We introduce **operational semantic fidelity** $F_S^{(n)}(T,C)=1-D_{TV}^{(n)}(T,C)$, where $D_{TV}^{(n)}$ is the total‑variation distance between the outcome distributions generated by a term’s native ontology $O_T$ and the target circuit context $C$ on the decision‑relevant observable set $\mathcal O_C$. For the prototypical term “photon”, we compute a per‑element divergence of $1/2$ at a balanced two‑boson beamsplitter, derive the compounding law $D_{TV}^{(k)}=1-2^{-k}$ for $k$ independent interference elements, and show that a decision‑relevance threshold $\varepsilon=0.9$ is crossed at $k^{*}=4$ (≈ 8 qubits of photonic hardware). Using the Tianyan‑287 superconducting platform’s published fidelities [1] we obtain error‑free‑gate budgets of $10^{3}$ single‑qubit, $2.3\times10^{2}$ two‑qubit, and $7.7\times10^{1}$ readout operations. The measure converts ontological drift into a quantitative engineering specification, enabling systematic validation and falsification. Limitations, required assumptions, and a concrete next‑step experiment are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Large‑scale quantum‑computing programmes (e.g., national cloud‑access platforms) allocate billions of dollars to hardware that must interoperate across disciplinary boundaries. Engineers who design photonic interferometers, superconducting processors, or trapped‑ion chains regularly encounter terms such as “photon”, “quasiparticle”, or “measurement” that originated in quantum‑optical, condensed‑matter, or high‑energy contexts. When the native ontology of such a term implies indistinguishability, copyability, or locality that the circuit‑model formalism does not guarantee, design teams can pursue architectures that are fundamentally incompatible with the underlying hardware. The cost of this semantic drift appears as stalled collaborations, repeated redesign cycles, and missed performance targets, all of which inflate project budgets and delay commercial delivery.

If a quantitative criterion could tell a hardware architect whether a borrowed term remains trustworthy at a given system size, the architect could either retain the term (saving documentation effort) or replace it with a vocabulary‑neutral description before costly redesigns occur. Our work provides exactly such a criterion.

## 2. Background and Related Work

Quantitative device characterizations such as the Tianyan‑287 cloud‑access prototype report 105 qubits with single‑qubit fidelity $99.90\%$, two‑qubit fidelity $99.56\%$, and readout fidelity $98.7\%$ [1]. These numbers constitute the operational baseline against which any borrowed‑term divergence must be compared.

Vocabulary‑neutral formulations have been proposed in quantum‑measure theory, which introduces a “quantum measure” $|S|$ for sets of histories and a sum rule that generalizes classical probability [2]. The follow‑up analysis shows that the quantum measure $\mu$ can exceed unity because of interference effects, a property that particle‑based vocabularies cannot accommodate [3]. This illustrates how a term rooted in particle ontology can misrepresent the underlying formalism.

The “impossible measurements” study demonstrates that naïve combinations of relativity and quantum measurement lead to signaling between spacelike separated regions, exposing a clash between the classical‑probe intuition of “measurement” and the non‑signaling requirement of quantum theory [4]. The categorical analysis of quantum measurement without sums proves that quantum mechanics can be expressed solely with the tensor product, without direct sums, and that classical spaces are precisely those that permit copying [7]. The inability of particle‑based vocabularies to express copyability underlies many of the semantic divergences we quantify.

A methodological analogue outside physics is the white‑box analysis of the ColBERT retrieval model, which dissects how a system actually uses its terms rather than how the terms suggest it should behave [5]. We adopt a similar “white‑box” stance by comparing the outcome distributions generated by the native ontology of a term with those generated by the circuit model.

Error‑corrected codes provide a regime where borrowed vocabulary is heavily stressed. The stabilizer formalism for operator‑algebra quantum error correction generates hybrid classical‑quantum stabilizer codes and makes explicit the layering of classical syndrome information on top of quantum logical operations [6]. Although we do not compute a code‑distance threshold here, the formalism supplies the context for future extensions of our measure.

Finally, the holographic entanglement‑entropy simulation on a quantum simulator reports the migration of the term “holographic entropy” from high‑energy physics into a quantum‑simulation platform, exemplifying cross‑domain vocabulary transfer that can introduce ontological mismatches [8].

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Formal definitions

Let $C$ denote a computational context characterised by a family of circuits parameterised by a size $n$, a set of decision‑relevant observables $\mathcal O_C$, and a deterministic decision rule that maps measurement outcomes to engineering actions. Let $T$ be a borrowed term with a native ontology $O_T$ that specifies a set of commitments (e.g., indistinguishability, copyability, number conservation). Both $O_T$ and the circuit formalism induce probability distributions over the outcome space $\mathcal X^{(n)}$ of $\mathcal O_C$:

\[
P_T^{(n)}(x),\qquad P_C^{(n)}(x),\qquad x\in\mathcal X^{(n)} .
\]

The **operational divergence** is the total‑variation distance

\[
D_{TV}^{(n)}(T,C)=\frac12\sum_{x\in\mathcal X^{(n)}}\bigl|P_T^{(n)}(x)-P_C^{(n)}(x)\bigr| .
\]

The **semantic fidelity** is defined as

\[
F_S^{(n)}(T,C)=1-D_{TV}^{(n)}(T,C) .
\]

Total‑variation distance upper‑bounds the advantage any decision rule can obtain by using one description instead of the other; thus $F_S$ directly quantifies the decision‑relevance of the ontological mismatch.

### 3.2 Decision threshold and failure scale

A design team selects a decision‑relevance threshold $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ that reflects the maximum tolerable probability of making a wrong engineering choice due to semantic drift. The term $T$ is **semantically faithful** at size $n$ if $D_{TV}^{(n)}\le\varepsilon$ and becomes **provably misleading** at the smallest $n^{*}$ satisfying

\[
D_{TV}^{(n^{*})}>\varepsilon .
\]

### 3.3 Compounding across independent elements

If the context decomposes into $k$ statistically independent loci where $T$ is invoked, each with per‑element divergence $\delta_i$, and the joint outcome distribution factorises, then

\[
D_{TV}^{(k)}=1-\prod_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(1-\delta_i\bigr) .
\]

For identical elements $\delta_i=\delta$ this reduces to

\[
D_{TV}^{(k)}=1-(1-\delta)^{k} .
\]

The crossing index $k^{*}$ follows from $(1-\delta)^{k^{*}}\le 1-\varepsilon$.

### 3.4 Validation plan (future work)

1. Analytic computation of $F_S$ for canonical borrowed terms (completed in Sections 4–5).  
2. Numerical simulation of growing entangled systems to test the independence assumption and the compounding law.  
3. Survey of engineer mental models to assess whether divergences $\ge\varepsilon$ correlate with divergent design choices.

Steps 2 and 3 are proposed experiments; the present paper delivers step 1.

## 4. Analysis

All input numbers are cited with their source, and every arithmetic step is shown.

### 4.1 Per‑element divergence for “photon”

**Setup.** Consider a balanced $50/50$ beamsplitter with one particle in each input port. The decision‑relevant observable is whether the two output particles exit the same port (“bunch”) or different ports (“split”).

**Native‑ontology prediction.** In the bosonic ontology of a photon, two indistinguishable bosons interfere destructively in the split amplitudes, yielding

\[
P_T(\text{same})=1,\qquad P_T(\text{split})=0 .
\]

**Circuit‑model prediction.** Treating the two particles as distinguishable qubits routed independently through the beamsplitter gives

\[
P_C(\text{same})=\tfrac12,\qquad P_C(\text{split})=\tfrac12 .
\]

**Divergence.** With outcome space $\mathcal X=\{\text{same},\text{split}\}$:

\[
\begin{aligned}
D_{TV}^{(1)} &=\frac12\Bigl(\bigl|1-\tfrac12\bigr|+\bigl|0-\tfrac12\bigr|\Bigr) \\
&=\frac12\Bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\Bigr)=\frac12 .
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus

\[
F_S^{(1)}=1-\frac12=\frac12 .
\]

### 4.2 Compounding law and threshold crossing

Assume $k$ independent beamsplitter elements, each with per‑element divergence $\delta=\tfrac12$ (derived above). Applying the identical‑element compounding law:

\[
D_{TV}^{(k)}=1-\bigl(1-\tfrac12\bigr)^{k}=1-2^{-k}.
\]

We tabulate the first six values:

| $k$ | $D_{TV}^{(k)}$ | $F_S^{(k)}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $0.5$ | $0.5$ |
| 2 | $0.75$ | $0.25$ |
| 3 | $0.875$ | $0.125$ |
| 4 | $0.9375$ | $0.0625$ |
| 5 | $0.96875$ | $0.03125$ |
| 6 | $0.984375$ | $0.015625$ |

We adopt a decision‑relevance threshold $\varepsilon=0.9$ (a design‑team stipulation). The smallest $k^{*}$ with $D_{TV}^{(k)}>\varepsilon$ satisfies

\[
1-2^{-k^{*}} > 0.9 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2^{-k^{*}} < 0.1 .
\]

Taking natural logarithms:

\[
\begin{aligned}
- k^{*}\ln 2 &< \ln 0.1 \\
k^{*} &> \frac{\ln 0.1}{-\ln 2}
      = \frac{-2.302585\ldots}{-0.693147\ldots}
      \approx 3.3219 .
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence $k^{*}=4$. Since each element involves two particles, the crossing occurs at $2\times4=8$ photonic particles (or equivalently eight qubits of a two‑mode interferometric hardware).

### 4.3 Gate‑budget calculations for the native vocabulary

The Tianyan‑287 report supplies the following fidelities [1]:

- Single‑qubit gate fidelity $F_1=99.90\% = 0.9990$.  
- Two‑qubit gate fidelity $F_2=99.56\% = 0.9956$.  
- Readout fidelity $F_r=98.7\% = 0.987$.

The error probability per operation is $p=1-F$.

**Single‑qubit gates.**  
\[
p_1 = 1-0.9990 = 0.0010 = 1.0\times10^{-3}.
\]
The expected number of error‑free gates before the first error (geometric mean) is
\[
N_1 = \frac{1}{p_1}= \frac{1}{1.0\times10^{-3}} = 1000 .
\]

**Two‑qubit gates.**  
\[
p_2 = 1-0.9956 = 0.0044 = 4.4\times10^{-3},
\qquad
N_2 = \frac{1}{p_2}= \frac{1}{4.4\times10^{-3}} \approx 227 .
\]

**Readout.**  
\[
p_r = 1-0.987 = 0.013 = 1.3\times10^{-2},
\qquad
N_r = \frac{1}{p_r}= \frac{1}{1.3\times10^{-2}} \approx 77 .
\]

These numbers represent the **semantic reach** of the native vocabulary “qubit”: after roughly $N_2$ two‑qubit gates or $N_r$ readouts, the ideal circuit description is expected to have diverged from the implemented hardware with probability approaching unity.

### 4.4 Extension to error‑corrected cycles (qualitative)

In stabilizer‑based error‑corrected codes the circuit context contains $k_{\text{cycle}}$ loci where borrowed terms such as “syndrome” or “anyon” appear. If each locus exhibits a per‑locus divergence $\delta_{\text{ec}}$, the cycle‑level divergence follows the same compounding law $1-(1-\delta_{\text{ec}})^{k_{\text{cycle}}}$. Determining $\delta_{\text{ec}}$ requires a noise model beyond the supplied literature and is therefore left to future simulation work (validation step 2).

## 5. Results

- **R1.** Per‑element divergence for “photon” at a balanced beamsplitter: $D_{TV}^{(1)}=1/2$, $F_S^{(1)}=1/2$ (Section 4.1).  
- **R2.** Compounded divergence for $k$ independent elements: $D_{TV}^{(k)}=1-2^{-k}$ (Section 4.2).  
- **R3.** Decision‑relevance crossing at $k^{*}=4$ elements (≈ 8 photonic particles) for $\varepsilon=0.9$ (Section 4.2).  
- **R4.** Projection: under the assumption of perfect independence, “photon” becomes misleading at $n^{*}=8$ qubits of photonic‑interference hardware (Section 4.2).  
- **R5.** Error‑free‑gate budgets derived from Tianyan‑287 fidelities [1]: $N_1\approx1000$ single‑qubit gates, $N_2\approx227$ two‑qubit gates, $N_r\approx77$ readouts (Section 4.3).  
- **R6.** Qualitative observation that quantum‑measure theory permits measures exceeding unity, exposing a mismatch with particle‑based vocabularies [3]; “impossible measurements” analysis shows signaling contradictions for classical‑probe measurement notions [4]; tensor‑product‑only formulation denies copyability assumed by particle vocabularies [7].

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Limitations

1. **Idealised ontologies.** The divergence calculations compare two perfect theoretical models (bosonic photons vs. distinguishable qubits) rather than noisy physical devices. Real beamsplitters exhibit partial indistinguishability and loss, which would reduce the observed $D_{TV}$.  
2. **Independence assumption.** The compounding law presumes statistical independence and factorisation of the $k$ elements. In practice, shared sources, mode‑matching, and control electronics introduce correlations that can either attenuate or amplify the effective divergence.  
3. **Threshold choice.** The decision‑relevance threshold $\varepsilon=0.9$ is a design‑team stipulation; different engineering contexts may adopt stricter or looser values, shifting $k^{*}$ according to $\ln(1-\varepsilon)/\ln(1-\delta)$.  
4. **Error‑budget independence.** The gate‑budget estimates assume uncorrelated errors; correlated leakage or crosstalk would lower the effective $N_1$, $N_2$, $N_r$.  
5. **Scope of terms.** We have demonstrated the method only for “photon”. Extending to other borrowed terms (e.g., “quasiparticle”, “measurement”) requires explicit native‑ontology predictions, which are not supplied in the current literature excerpts.

### 6.2 Falsification condition

If a high‑fidelity simulation of $k=4$ independent photonic interferometers (including realistic mode overlap and loss) yields a total‑variation distance $D_{TV}^{(4)}\le 0.5$, i.e. far below the predicted $0.9375$, the compounding law would be empirically falsified for this context. Conversely, observing $D_{TV}^{(4)}\ge 0.95$ would support the analytic prediction.

### 6.3 Open questions

- How does partial indistinguishability (parameterised by a visibility $V$) modify the per‑element divergence $\delta$?  
- What is the appropriate $\varepsilon$ for safety‑critical quantum‑control software versus exploratory research?  
- Can the measure be integrated into automated design‑space exploration tools to flag semantic drift early?  
- How does the measure behave in fault‑tolerant error‑corrected cycles where logical operations are compiled from many physical gates?

## 7. Practical Implications and Engineering Pathways

**(a) Intended users and impact.** Quantum‑hardware architects, compiler developers, and cross‑disciplinary project managers can employ $F_S$ to audit terminology in design specifications. If $F_S$ falls below a chosen $\varepsilon$ for a given system size, the team should replace the borrowed term with a vocabulary‑neutral description (e.g., “mode‑pair” instead of “photon”) to avoid mis‑aligned intuition.

**(b) Technology readiness.** The present work is a purely analytical derivation that has not undergone peer review; according to NASA’s scale it resides at TRL 2 (formulation of concept and analytical proof‑of‑concept).

**(c) Minimal next experiment.** Build a photonic testbed comprising four nominally independent $50/50$ beamsplitters fed by identical photon‑pair sources. Measure the empirical outcome distribution over the “same vs. split” observable and compute the empirical $D_{TV}^{(4)}$. An observed $D_{TV}^{(4)}\le 0.5$ would falsify the current compounding prediction; $D_{TV}^{(4)}\ge 0.9$ would confirm it.

**(d) Engineering constraints.**  
- **Independence:** Requires spatially separated sources and independent phase‑stabilisation loops to approximate factorisation.  
- **Detection efficiency:** High‑efficiency photon‑number‑resolving detectors are needed to avoid loss‑induced bias.  
- **Mode matching:** Imperfect mode overlap directly reduces the native‑ontology bunching probability, altering $\delta$.  
These constraints are derived from the assumptions listed in Section 4.2.

**(e) Projected cost/performance bounds.** Using the gate‑budget numbers (Section 4.3) as a projection, a superconducting processor that executes fewer than $N_2\approx227$ two‑qubit gates can be expected to retain semantic fidelity $F_S>0.9$ for the native “qubit” term under the independence‑of‑errors assumption. No additional cost estimate is provided because all inputs are derived analytically.

**(f) Risks and misuse.** Over‑reliance on $F_S$ without considering hardware‑specific error correlations could lead to premature dismissal of useful terminology. Moreover, if a design team treats $F_S$ as a hard safety threshold rather than a guideline, it may unnecessarily constrain innovation. Proper contextualisation of $\varepsilon$ and of the underlying assumptions mitigates these risks.

## 8. Conclusion

We have introduced **operational semantic fidelity** $F_S^{(n)}=1-D_{TV}^{(n)}$ as a quantitative, vocabulary‑neutral diagnostic for assessing when a borrowed quantum term becomes decision‑relevantly misleading. Applying the framework to the term “photon” yields a per‑element divergence of $1/2$, a compounding law $D_{TV}^{(k)}=1-2^{-k}$, and a crossing scale of $k^{*}=4$ (≈ 8 photonic qubits) for a design‑team threshold $\varepsilon=0.9$. Gate‑budget calculations from the Tianyan‑287 superconducting platform demonstrate that even the native “qubit” vocabulary possesses a finite semantic reach. The measure converts ontological concerns into actionable engineering specifications, while its limitations—idealised models, independence assumptions, and threshold arbitrariness—are explicitly stated. A concrete four‑interferometer experiment is proposed to validate or falsify the compounding prediction, establishing a clear pathway from theory to practice.
