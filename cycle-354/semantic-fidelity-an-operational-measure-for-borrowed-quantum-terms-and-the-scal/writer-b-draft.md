# Semantic Fidelity: An Operational Measure for Borrowed Quantum Terms and the Scale at Which They Fail

## Abstract

Quantum computing borrows its vocabulary from quantum optics, condensed matter, and particle physics, and this borrowed ontology can quietly conflict with the operational content of the circuit model it is meant to describe. The specific gap addressed here is that no existing quantity converts this ontological mismatch into a number with a scale attached: practitioners can argue about "ontological baggage" but cannot state at what system size a borrowed term becomes decision-relevantly misleading. We formalize semantic fidelity $F_S$ as one minus the total variation distance between the decision-relevant outcome distributions implied by a borrowed term's native ontology and those implied by the target circuit model. We illustrate the measure on the term "photon": for two indistinguishable bosons at a balanced beamsplitter, the native ontology predicts bunching with probability $1$, while a distinguishable-particle reading predicts a split outcome with probability $1/2$, giving $F_S = 1/2$ per interference element. Because semantic divergence compounds across independent elements, the total variation distance over $k$ such elements is $1 - 2^{-k}$, crossing a decision-relevance threshold of $0.9$ at $k = 4$ elements, i.e., roughly eight qubits of photonic-interference hardware. Illustrative gate-budget computations under explicitly assumed device fidelities show that even the circuit model's native vocabulary has finite semantic reach. The measure supplies a vocabulary-neutral diagnostic that turns ontological worries into testable engineering specifications.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum computing hardware teams, cross-disciplinary collaborations between physicists and engineers, and the funding programs that support them all face a problem that no current tool quantifies: the field's working vocabulary was inherited from other subfields, and the inherited words carry commitments the circuit model does not make. Words such as "photon," "quasiparticle," "exchange," and "particle number" arrive from quantum optics, condensed matter physics, and particle physics, each carrying an ontology — a commitment about what exists and what can be distinguished, copied, and counted. The circuit model of quantum computation, by contrast, commits only to finite-dimensional unitary dynamics, measurement, and classical control. When the two ontologies agree on all operational predictions, the borrowed vocabulary is harmless shorthand. When they disagree, the borrowed term becomes actively misleading: it suggests experiments, error models, or engineering intuitions that the circuit model does not support.

The cost of this mismatch is concrete. A collaboration in which one party reads "photon" as a countable, distinguishable particle and the other as an indistinguishable bosonic mode can agree on every word of a design document and still build a device whose interference statistics violate one party's expectations — with no arithmetic error anywhere. A program officer reviewing a proposal built on particle-flavored intuitions has no instrument for asking whether the proposal's vocabulary, rather than its mathematics, is the failure point. And an engineer trained on classical copyable signals imports copyability assumptions into error-correction and readout design that the formalism does not grant. What would change if a quantitative measure existed: collaborations could specify, in a design document, the system size at which each borrowed term must be replaced by an operational definition; reviewers could demand a semantic-fidelity budget alongside the gate-fidelity budget; and the field would gain a vocabulary-neutral criterion for what the quantum formalism itself asserts, as opposed to what its particle-flavored dialect asserts.

The central conjecture of this paper is that this disagreement is quantifiable. We define an operational semantic fidelity $F_S$ for a borrowed term $T$ used in a computational context $C$: the closeness, in total variation distance, between the outcome distributions that $T$'s native ontology predicts and those that $C$'s formalism predicts, restricted to the observables that actually drive decisions in $C$. A term is semantically faithful at scale $n$ if $F_S$ remains above a decision threshold; it becomes provably misleading at the smallest $n$ where $F_S$ falls below that threshold. The prior formulation of this measure and its first worked example appear as the QNFO preprint [12], whose definitions, compounding law, and threshold crossing this paper re-derives in full and extends with a sensitivity analysis and a practical-adoption pathway.

This matters for three reasons. First, semantic drift is a candidate explanation for stalled collaborations between physicists and engineers: if the two communities attach different operational content to the same word, joint designs can fail without any party making an arithmetic error. Second, a quantitative measure converts a vague worry about "ontological baggage" into a testable engineering specification, in the same way that gate fidelity converted "noise" into a budget. Third, a vocabulary-neutral formulation sharpens the question of what the quantum formalism itself asserts. The companion critique [13] argues that the qubit-gate-circuit model imports a particle ontology into quantum mechanics — as if qubits were little quantum billiard balls poked with gates — and that this ontology is inconsistent with quantum field theory, relational quantum mechanics, and the physics of continuous, correlated quantum systems; it further states that $35$ billion dollars and two decades of global effort have produced zero commercially viable quantum computers under the incumbent paradigm. Our paper accepts the diagnosis of ontological projection but differs in method: rather than arguing the failure globally, we localize it with an operational divergence and a scale.

Our contributions are:

- A formal definition of operational semantic fidelity $F_S^{(n)}(T, C) = 1 - D_{TV}^{(n)}(T, C)$, with the decision-threshold semantics that make the measure actionable (Section 3).
- A fully worked derivation for the term "photon," including the per-element divergence $D_{TV}^{(1)} = 1/2$, an explicit compounding law $D_{TV}^{(k)} = 1 - 2^{-k}$, and the concrete crossing index $k^* = 4$ elements at threshold $\varepsilon = 0.9$ (Section 4).
- A sensitivity analysis showing how the crossing index depends on the per-element divergence and the threshold, with computed values for $\delta \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75\}$ (Section 4).
- Gate-budget computations under explicitly assumed illustrative fidelities, showing that even the circuit model's native vocabulary has finite semantic reach (Section 4).
- A survey of the mathematical and physical literature for structural analogues of semantic divergence — invariants that are faithful in one regime and fail in another, quantum terms that vanish at a classical limit, and vocabulary-independence results — establishing that the phenomenon the measure targets is not idiosyncratic to quantum computing (Section 2).
- A discussion of validation, falsification, failure modes, and the engineering pathway, including the smallest next test that would de-risk or refute the compounding law (Sections 6 and 7).

We report only quantities we derive here or clearly labeled projections with stated assumptions; we perform no new experiments, simulations, or surveys.

## 2. Background and Related Work

We organize the related work into four strands: the prior formulation of semantic fidelity and its companion critique; mathematical precedents for invariants that are faithful in one regime and fail in another; physical precedents for "quantum terms" whose operational content is scale- or context-dependent; and quantitative, problem-driven specification cultures that the measure is meant to join.

**The prior formulation and its critique.** The QNFO preprint [12] defines operational semantic fidelity $F_S$ as one minus the total variation distance between the decision-relevant outcome distributions implied by a borrowed term's native ontology and those implied by the target circuit model, and reports the results this paper re-derives: a per-element divergence of $D_{TV}^{(1)} = 1/2$ for "photon" at a balanced two-boson beamsplitter, a compounding law $D_{TV}^{(k)} = 1 - 2^{-k}$ under stated independence assumptions, and a threshold crossing at $k^* = 4$ elements for $\varepsilon = 0.9$. Its discussion already identifies the three limitations we carry forward: the derivation compares two idealized ontologies rather than two physical devices; the compounding law assumes independence and factorization, so real shared modes, sources, and control hardware can push the joint divergence in either direction; and the threshold $\varepsilon = 0.9$ is a stipulation, not a measurement. The companion paper [13] supplies the motivating diagnosis: it argues that the qubit-gate-circuit model imports a particle ontology — treating qubits as small billiard balls poked with gates — that is inconsistent with quantum field theory, relational quantum mechanics, and continuous correlated systems, and it poses the constructive question of what the minimal ontological commitments of quantum mechanics are once classical projections of particles, waves, measurement-as-revelation, and special observers are stripped away. Our measure is the instrument that localizes [13]'s global diagnosis: where [13] argues that the scaffold is never ontologically neutral, we compute at what scale a specific borrowed word stops being neutral.

**Mathematical precedents: invariants with regime-dependent fidelity.** The closest structural analogue to semantic fidelity outside physics is the classification theory of operator algebras. The work on unitary elements of C*-algebras [3] proves the existence part of the conjecture that the Cuntz semigroup classifies unitary elements of unital AF-algebras, under a mild adjustment of the domain and codomain of the functor $\mathrm{Cu}$; but the same paper exhibits an obstruction to uniqueness beyond the AF case, constructing two unitary elements in $\mathcal{C}([0,1]) \otimes M_{2^{\infty}}$ that agree at the level of $\mathrm{Cu}$ and yet fail to be approximately unitarily equivalent. This is exactly the shape of our thesis in a different field: the label "$\mathrm{Cu}$-class" is a semantically faithful term inside the AF regime and a provably misleading one outside it, with a concrete counterexample marking the boundary. Semantic fidelity gives this familiar mathematical phenomenon an operational, decision-theoretic reading.

A second precedent comes from low-dimensional topology. The one-cocycle construction [2] proves a conjecture of Thomas Fiedler that a formula based on the Vassiliev invariant $v_3$ defines a 1-cocycle in a moduli space of knots in the solid torus, using Gauss diagrams — finite combinatorial data of signed crossings — to represent the knot and to compute invariants by evaluating the cocycle on canonical loops. The relevance is methodological: a topological object with continuous degrees of freedom is faithfully replaced, for the purpose of computing a specific invariant, by a finite vocabulary of local symbols. Whether a finite vocabulary remains faithful is a scale question, and the cocycle formalism is a worked example of checking faithfulness combinatorially rather than by intuition.

A third precedent is the equivalence-of-descriptions pattern in symplectic topology. The work on Legendrian contact homology [7] studies the unit conormal bundle $\Lambda_K$ of a compact submanifold $K$, a Legendrian submanifold of the unit cotangent bundle with a canonical contact structure, whose Legendrian contact homology is defined via pseudo-holomorphic curve techniques; the paper's stated aim is to give another description of this homology. Its machinery rests on canonical identifications between vocabularies — for instance, the canonical isomorphism between singular and de Rham homology for oriented manifolds. Two descriptions related by a canonical isomorphism have semantic fidelity exactly $1$; the interesting question, which our measure formalizes, is what happens when the isomorphism fails or is only partial.

**Physical precedents: quantum terms with scale- and context-dependent content.** Several bodies of physics literature use the phrase "quantum terms" literally, and their behavior is a template for our measure. The superintegrable-Hamiltonians work [8] introduces a family of exactly-solvable two-dimensional Hamiltonians whose wave functions are given in terms of Laguerre and exceptional Jacobi polynomials, and whose Hamiltonian "contains purely quantum terms which vanish in the classical limit leaving only a previously known family of superintegrable systems" — the classical limit recovers the Tremblay-Turbiner-Winternitz system, and the higher-order integrals are built from ladder operators for the exceptional polynomials. Here the quantum terms have exactly the scale-dependence our measure quantifies: their operational content is full at finite $\hbar$ and identically zero at the classical limit, so any classical vocabulary for the system is faithful at the limit and divergent away from it.

The quantum-plasma shock analysis [5] shows the same localization in a dissipative setting. It studies weak shocks including both thermal conduction and the dispersive quantum terms due to the Bohm potential, and finds that even a small thermal conduction may lead to a stationary shock structure; its integrated equations show explicitly that quantum effects do not influence the properties of the uniform flow behind the shock — they are important only for the shock structure — and its summary reports that soliton trains can occur at the shock front in the quantum regime. A borrowed classical term ("shock," "front") is thus faithful for some observables (the uniform flow) and divergent for others (the front structure) in the same physical system. This is the observable-dependence of semantic fidelity, encountered independently in plasma physics.

The curved-space QED calculation [6] provides a context-dependence precedent for the very term this paper analyzes. It computes one-loop corrections to the photon sector of QED in a background gravitational field, deriving an anomaly-induced effective action for the massless case that is exact and scheme-independent under minimal subtraction, and showing that in the massive fermion case there is a violation of conformal symmetry coming from the form factor in the electromagnetic sector. The term "photon," faithful as a conformally coupled free field in flat space, acquires context-dependent operational content in curved spacetime: the paper's own summary notes that the anomaly-induced representations can serve as a consistent background for investigating quantum processes in the early universe, where fermionic masses are irrelevant. Semantic drift of "photon" with geometry is thus already documented; our contribution is to attach a number and a scale to drift with system size.

Two further precedents show vocabulary-independence results, the fixed points against which semantic divergence is measured. The tunneling analysis of Hawking radiation [10] treats emission of arbitrary scalars, fermions, and spin-1 bosons to every order in $\hbar$ without back-reaction, and finds that the additional quantum terms yield no new contribution to the Hawking temperature; its discussion concludes that Hawking radiation is independent of the type of particle involved, and that the $\hbar \to 0$ limit of the WKB method is unnecessary, being replaced by the near-horizon limit $g_{tt} \to 0$. A quantity (the temperature) whose value is provably insensitive to which particle vocabulary is used is a semantic fidelity of exactly $1$ across a whole family of borrowed terms — and the paper's gauge-fixing caveat ($\nabla_\mu A^\mu = 0$ for the boson case) marks the price of the invariance. Similarly, the loop-quantum-cosmology analysis [9] derives the fully corrected equation of motion for gravitational waves with both holonomy and inverse-volume quantum terms in a unified framework, and shows that the spectrum remains exponentially infrared divergent, as for a pure inverse-volume correction, reinforcing primordial gravitational waves as a probe of quantum-gravity effects. The structure — a classical term's predictions corrected by quantum terms whose combined effect is computed and found observationally robust — is the mirror image of our compounding law: there, quantum corrections accumulate coherently into an observable; here, semantic divergences accumulate into a decision-relevant distance.

Finally, the dilaton-gravity analysis [11] contributes a structural lesson about where semantic content concentrates. It proves that extreme black holes with nonzero temperature and finite horizon curvature exist in exactly solvable models of dilaton gravity with quantum corrections, because thermal divergences in the stress-energy tensor cancel against divergences in the classical part of the field equations; and it shows that this existence depends strongly on the behavior of the action coefficients near the horizon and is insensitive to their concrete form far from it. Semantic content, in this example as in ours, is localized: the regime where the vocabulary is under strain is a small region (the horizon; the interference element; the code cycle), and the measure must be evaluated there, not averaged globally.

**Quantitative, problem-driven specification culture.** The NSUCRYPTO olympiad collection [1] documents a community that converts cryptographic vocabulary into checkable problems: its sixteen problems across two rounds in 2017 covered attacks on ciphers and hash functions, cryptographic Boolean functions, the linear branch number, addition chains, and error correction codes, with more than 1300 participants from 38 countries in 2014–2017 and detailed solutions published for each problem. The relevance is cultural, not technical: cryptography long ago converted its terms ("branch number," "differential resistance") into numbers with thresholds that designs must meet. Semantic fidelity is proposed as the analogous conversion for ontological vocabulary in quantum computing: a term passes or fails at a stated scale, by a stated rule, with the arithmetic shown.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Definitions

Fix a computational context $C$: a family of circuits (or code operations) parameterized by a size $n$, a set of decision-relevant observables $\mathcal{O}_C$, and a decision rule that maps measurement outcomes to actions. Fix a borrowed term $T$ with a native ontology $O_T$: a set of commitments (distinguishability, copyability, number conservation, locality of probes) strong enough to generate predictions. Concretely, both $O_T$ and the formalism of $C$ induce probability distributions over outcomes of the observables in $\mathcal{O}_C$:

$$P_T^{(n)}(x) \quad \text{and} \quad P_C^{(n)}(x), \qquad x \in \mathcal{X}^{(n)},$$

where $\mathcal{X}^{(n)}$ is the outcome space at size $n$. The operational divergence is the total variation distance

$$D_{TV}^{(n)}(T, C) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}^{(n)}} \left| P_T^{(n)}(x) - P_C^{(n)}(x) \right|,$$

and the semantic fidelity is

$$F_S^{(n)}(T, C) = 1 - D_{TV}^{(n)}(T, C).$$

Total variation distance (half the $\ell_1$ distance between distributions) is chosen because it has a direct decision meaning: it upper-bounds the advantage any decision rule can gain by using one description instead of the other. If $D_{TV}^{(n)} = \delta$, then no test built on $\mathcal{O}_C$ can distinguish the two ontologies with advantage exceeding $\delta$; conversely, some test achieves advantage close to $\delta$. This is the property that makes the measure operational rather than philosophical: the number is a bound on worst-case decision impact.

### 3.2 Decision threshold and failure scale

Fix a decision-relevance threshold $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$, set by the cost of a wrong design decision in context $C$. The term $T$ is **semantically faithful** at size $n$ if $D_{TV}^{(n)} \leq \varepsilon$ and **provably misleading** at the smallest $n^*$ with

$$D_{TV}^{(n^*)} > \varepsilon.$$

The qualifier "provably" is earned because $D_{TV}$ is computed from the two ontologies' own predictions, not from hardware data: it is a statement about vocabularies, conditional on each vocabulary's generating the distributions claimed. No hardware measurement enters the derivation, and none is claimed.

### 3.3 Compounding across independent elements

Realistic contexts contain many independent loci where a borrowed term is invoked. If the context decomposes into $k$ independent elements, each with per-element divergence $\delta_i = D_{TV}^{(1)}$ on element $i$, and the joint outcome distribution factorizes, then the joint divergence is

$$D_{TV}^{(k)} = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{k} (1 - \delta_i).$$

For identical elements with $\delta_i = \delta$ this gives the compounding law

$$D_{TV}^{(k)} = 1 - (1 - \delta)^{k}.$$

The derivation of the product form is standard and short: under factorization, the probability that the two ontologies agree on every element is the product of the per-element agreement probabilities $\prod_i (1 - \delta_i)$, and the total variation distance between two product distributions whose marginals agree or disagree independently is one minus the all-agree probability. The law converts a per-element semantic gap into a scale: the term becomes decision-relevantly misleading once $(1-\delta)^k \leq 1 - \varepsilon$, i.e., at

$$k^* = \left\lceil \frac{\ln(1-\varepsilon)}{\ln(1-\delta)} \right\rceil,$$

with the ceiling taken on the smallest integer $k$ satisfying the strict inequality $D_{TV}^{(k)} > \varepsilon$.

### 3.4 Method of this paper

This paper executes the analytic step of the validation program and states the projections that follow from it. The method is: (i) select a canonical borrowed term ("photon") and a canonical decision-relevant observable (bunching versus splitting at a beamsplitter); (ii) derive the per-element divergence from the two ontologies' own predictions; (iii) apply the compounding law under a stated independence assumption and locate the threshold crossing; (iv) compute the sensitivity of the crossing index to $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$; (v) apply the same budget logic to the circuit model's own vocabulary under explicitly assumed illustrative fidelities. Steps (ii)–(v) are pure arithmetic on stated inputs. A simulation step comparing photonic-ontological and circuit-model predictions on growing entangled systems, and a survey step testing whether practitioners whose descriptions diverge by $D_{TV} \geq \varepsilon$ also make divergent design choices, are future work and are specified as proposals in Section 7.

### 4.1 Per-element divergence for "photon": the two-boson beamsplitter

**Setup.** The term "photon" carries, in its native quantum-optical ontology, the commitments of bosonic indistinguishability. Consider the canonical decision-relevant observable: a balanced beamsplitter with one particle in each input port, and the output observable "do the two particles exit the same port or different ports?"

**Native-ontology (bosonic) prediction.** For two indistinguishable bosons at a $50/50$ beamsplitter, the two amplitudes for the "split" outcomes (one in each output port) interfere destructively; the bunching outcome (both in the same port, either port) occurs with probability

$$P_T(\text{same}) = 1, \qquad P_T(\text{split}) = 0.$$

This is the standard two-boson interference result; we take it as the prediction generated by the native ontology of "photon."

**Distinguishable-particle (qubit-like) prediction.** The circuit-model reading — two distinguishable two-level systems, each routed $50/50$ — assigns independent $50/50$ outcomes:

$$P_C(\text{same}) = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad P_C(\text{split}) = \frac{1}{2}.$$
