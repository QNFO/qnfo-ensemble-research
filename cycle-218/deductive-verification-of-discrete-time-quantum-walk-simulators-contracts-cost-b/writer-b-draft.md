# Contract-Based Verification of Quantum-Walk Simulators, Re-Entered: Graph-Generalized Targets, Open-System Clauses, and Machine-Checkable Periodicity

## Abstract

Discrete-time quantum walks underpin a universal model of quantum computation, a platform for topological phases, and a proposed Rydberg-atom implementation of walks on arbitrary spatial networks, yet the simulators used to design and interpret these experiments are rarely checked with the discipline applied to classical library code. The gap this paper addresses is that existing verification results for quantum-walk simulators cover only the one-dimensional Hadamard walk, leave open-system and arbitrary-graph simulators without contracts, and state no falsification conditions. We re-enter and extend a prior contract-based verification framework: simulators are checked against extracted preconditions and postconditions in the style of deductive verification of unmodified library functions, which proved 23 of 26 Linux kernel functions. We add three contract families: graph-generalized support and unitarity clauses for coin-free staggered walks, trace-conservation clauses for density-matrix evolution under phase-flip, bit-flip, and generalized-amplitude-damping noise, and a periodicity clause grounded in the proven criterion that the deterministic combination AABB of two chaotic coins yields a periodic walk on a 3-cycle. Derived costs include a sparse-over-dense unitarity-check speedup of $d_t/4 = 1000.5$ at $t = 10^{3}$ ($64{,}064{,}016$ versus $64{,}096{,}048{,}008$ multiply-adds), a 50% normalization-check overhead independent of $t$, a projected double-precision drift bound of $8.88 \times 10^{-10}$ after $10^{6}$ steps, and a 3-cycle block-diagonalization cost of 432 multiply-adds. The framework gives simulator developers a decision procedure: which check to run, at what cost, and what observation would falsify it.

## 1. Introduction

A team simulating a quantum-walk experiment before running it on hardware faces a software-quality problem with physical consequences. Quantum walks have been realized with cold atoms, ions, and photons, with control knobs that include chosen rotation operations, injected dephasing, and spatial boundaries between regions with different rotation operations [1]; a discrete-event simulator has been used to reproduce the results of a quantum-walk experiment with cesium atoms in a state-dependent optical potential [2]; and a Rydberg-atom proposal for walks on arbitrary spatial networks relies on classical simulation to find tessellation covers and to benchmark a spatial-search speedup [8]. In each case, conclusions about the physics are drawn from simulator output. A simulator that silently violates unitarity, drifts out of normalization, or corrupts an integer-valued topological invariant produces wrong physics that no amount of downstream statistics will repair, because topological invariants are exactly the quantities a small numerical error can flip from one integer to another while every local observable looks normal.

What blocks progress today is the absence of a stated, costed verification obligation for such simulators. Deductive verification of classical code has a working template: a benchmark of 26 unmodified Linux kernel library functions implementing memory and string operations had formal contracts extracted from source code as preconditions and postconditions, and the correctness of 23 functions was fully proved, with two more proved after minor modification [7]. The same benchmark showed that the toolchain matters: Alt-Ergo and CVC4 discharged all verification conditions except one for memmove, and two newly proposed ACSL constructs allowed 11 more functions to be proved without touching their source [7]. If a prior version of this framework [9] applies that template to the one-dimensional Hadamard walk, three questions remain open for anyone who must sign off on a simulator: what contracts apply when the walk has no coin and runs on an arbitrary graph, what contracts apply when the simulator evolves a density matrix under noise rather than a state vector, and what periodicity claims can be checked against a proven criterion rather than an assumed one.

This paper answers those questions by re-entering the framework of [9] with three extensions, keeping every prior number that survives re-derivation and deriving the new costs with shown arithmetic. The payoff, if the framework holds, is a decision procedure for a simulation lead: run the $8 d_t$ row-norm check when only normalization matters, the $4 d_t^{2}$ sparse check when unitarity must be certified, and the exact integer support check always, with the cost of each stated in advance.

The contributions are:

1. A graph-generalized contract set for coin-free staggered quantum walks on tessellated graphs, with derived sparse and dense unitarity-check costs and an illustrative speedup of $N/k = 100/3 \approx 33.33$ at the stated assumptions, derived in Section 4 (Derivation 10).
2. A trace-conservation clause for density-matrix simulators of the noise channels studied in [6], with the derived result that the trace check costs $d_t$ additions against $2 d_t$ multiplications for the state-vector norm check, a factor-of-2 operation saving with zero multiplications, derived in Section 4 (Derivation 11).
3. A machine-checkable periodicity contract grounded in the proven criterion of [4] that the deterministic coin sequence AABB yields a periodic walk on a 3-cycle while AB, ABBB, and ABBBB do not, with a derived block-diagonalization cost of 432 multiply-adds for the 3-cycle step operator, derived in Section 4 (Derivation 12).
4. Re-derivation and confirmation of the prior version's core numbers [9]: state dimension $d_{1000} = 4002$, dense unitarity cost $64{,}096{,}048{,}008$ multiply-adds, simulation cost $M_{1000} = 8{,}016{,}000$ real multiplications, and a projected drift bound $\delta_{10^{6}} \approx 8.88 \times 10^{-10}$, derived in Section 4 (Derivations 1 through 8).
5. Explicit falsification conditions for every contract family, stated in Section 6.

## 2. Background and Related Work

This section positions the framework against the literature, citing each work for specific results its text states. Throughout, "coin" means the internal unitary acting on the walker's internal degree of freedom, "support" means the set of graph positions with nonzero amplitude, and "staggered walk" means the coin-free discrete-time walk defined through graph tessellations.

**Topological phases in quantum walks [1].** The review defines a conventional discrete quantum walk as a dynamical protocol for controlling a spin-$1/2$ particle on a one-dimensional lattice, one step being $U = T R_{y}(\theta)$ with a spin-dependent translation $T$ and a rotation $R_{y}(\theta)$, and shows that such walks display rich topological phenomena in one and two dimensions. Its conclusion section states that the walk was associated with winding numbers, that bound states across boundaries between regions of different topological phase were argued to be topologically protected from both the spectral-gap and invariant viewpoints, and that no three-dimensional quantum walk realizing a non-trivial topological phase is yet known. For verification this fixes the highest-stakes target: the winding number is an integer extracted from simulator output, and a contract that pins it to its model value catches the class of errors that leave all local observables intact.

**Discrete-event simulation [2].** This work builds a subquantum model of quantum walks that complies with Einstein locality, in which particles follow well-defined trajectories without wave-function collapse, and generates event-by-event data agreeing with the quantum-theoretical description; it reproduces the results of the cesium-atom experiment of Robens et al., in which about 400 repetitions were used to estimate $\langle Q(t_{3}) \rangle$ for a four-level walk. Because the authors provide Python programs with which their results can be reproduced, the work also demonstrates the reproducibility standard this paper advocates. Crucially for contract design, a discrete-event simulator keeps no state vector, so the unitarity contract does not apply to it; the applicable clause is statistical reproduction of the quantum distribution, a categorically different obligation that Section 3 flags but does not cost.

**Comprehensive review [3].** The review characterizes quantum walks as the quantum mechanical counterpart of classical random walks, an advanced tool for building quantum algorithms, and states that they have been shown to constitute a universal model of quantum computation. Its section on limit theorems records that the work of Konno and collaborators was central to deriving limit distributions for quantum walks, and its survey of discrete-walk results records specific findings such as the piecewise stretched exponential survival probability in walks on cycles with traps and history-dependent discrete-time walks. Universality raises the stakes of verification: a verified quantum-walk simulator is a verified simulator for a universal family of computations, so a contract framework for the simulator is a contract framework for that family.

**Order from chaos on cyclic graphs [4].** This work seeks the quantum analog of the classical result that combining two chaotic random walks can yield a periodic walk, framed through Parrondo's paradox. Its stated criteria are concrete: if a chaotic quantum walk is obtained by repeatedly applying coins $A$ or $B$, then repeatedly applying $AB$, $ABBB$, or $ABBBB$ does not yield a periodic walk, while the deterministic combination $AABB$ does yield a periodic quantum walk on a 3-cycle graph. Methodologically it block-diagonalizes the step operator $U_{k}$ using the commensurate Fourier matrix $F^{M}$ of dimension $M$, defined as $F^{M}_{m,n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}} e^{2\pi i mn/M}$, to read off periodicity from eigenvalues. This supplies exactly what a periodicity contract needs: a proven criterion and a canonical algorithmic route to checking it, which Section 4 costs explicitly.

**Limit distributions [5].** This review analytically states the weak limit theorem for the one-dimensional discrete-time quantum walk with a general two-dimensional coin $C = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ and initial coin state $|\phi_{0}\rangle = q_{L}|L\rangle + q_{R}|R\rangle$, citing Konno's theorem, and derives the free Dirac equation from the walk with the coin $C(\epsilon) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\epsilon & -i\sin\epsilon \\ -i\sin\epsilon & \cos\epsilon \end{pmatrix}$. For verification, the limit theorem supplies an asymptotic reference distribution: a simulator that passes the algebraic contracts but whose long-time histogram departs from the stated limit distribution has failed a statistical clause that the algebraic clauses cannot see.

**Symmetries and noise [6].** This work studies discrete symmetries of unbiased (Hadamard) and biased walks on a line, generated by the operations $Z$ and $PRX$ applied at each step, and shows that the equivalence of these variants survives noise modeled by phase-flip, bit-flip, and generalized-amplitude-damping channels, with numerical solutions obtained by evolving the density matrix. It further notes that noise is a major issue in scaling the number of steps in already realized systems and discusses realization in NMR processors and ultra-cold atoms. This is the bridge from verified unitary simulators to verified open-system simulators: the density-matrix loop has the same contract structure with trace conservation replacing probability conservation, and the three named channels are the first operations whose contract clauses one would write. Section 4 costs the trace clause.

**Deductive verification of systems code [7].** The benchmark consists of 26 unmodified Linux kernel library functions for memory and string operations, with contracts extracted from source as preconditions and postconditions; 23 functions were fully proved, 2 after minor source modification, and 1 could not be completely proved under the existing memory model. The evaluation shows Alt-Ergo and CVC4 discharging all verification conditions except one for memmove, under a stated configuration (60-second time limit, 6000 MB memory limit). This supplies both the methodological template, extract the contract from the object as defined and discharge the obligations, and a calibration point for solver capacity: obligations numbering in the thousands per run, as Section 4 derives for the clause count, are within the demonstrated regime of this benchmark.

**Rydberg-atom implementation of walks on networks [8].** This proposal encodes the walker as an excitation and diagonalizes the walk operators of a staggered quantum walk, a coin-free model defined through graph tessellations that encompasses Szegedy's quantization of bipartite Markov chains, using Rydberg multiqubit gates and dynamic atom rearrangement. It documents why the coined model generalizes poorly: regularizing the degree of an arbitrary graph so a global coin can be used introduces artificial self-loops, which can bias the evolution and localize the walker. It also records the cost of the continuous-time alternative, $O(N^{2})$ gates for a circuit of $n = \lceil \log_{2} N \rceil$ qubits with $O(N^{3})$ decomposition complexity, and demonstrates performance consistent with a quadratic speedup in spatial search. This makes the staggered walk on an arbitrary graph the natural next verification target beyond the line, and Section 4 derives its contract costs.

**Prior version of this framework [9].** The prior paper framed quantum-walk simulator verification as contract extraction and discharge, stated five contracts K1 through K5 for the one-dimensional Hadamard walk, and derived with shown arithmetic: the state dimension $d_{1000} = 4002$, the dense unitarity-check cost $d_t^{3} = 64{,}096{,}048{,}008$ multiply-adds, the sparse cost $4 d_t^{2} = 64{,}064{,}016$ with speedup $d_t/4 = 1000.5$, the simulation cost $M_{1000} = 8{,}016{,}000$ real multiplications, a 50% normalization-check overhead, a naive clause count $K_t = 5t$, a projected drift bound $\delta_{10^{6}} \approx 8.88 \times 10^{-10}$, and an illustrative 3-cycle return probability of $1/3$ under an explicitly assumed uniform-amplitude condition. Its discussion already flagged that the return probability was an assumption of that paper and that the winding-number contract was stated but not instantiated. The present paper re-derives the surviving numbers, drops the assumed return probability as a headline claim, and extends the contract set along the three axes above.

**Hidden-radix cryptography [10].** This work validated every core claim of its construction with a computational verification suite of over 2,200 lines of Python across four notebooks, reporting specific outcomes including exact recovery of the secret under known modulus for all tested $t \in \{0,1,2,3,4\}$ with $O(n^{3})$ scaling confirmed from $n = 4$ (0.04 ms) to $n = 128$ (135.7 ms), and a GCD-based modulus-recovery rate falling from 1.00 at $t = 0$ to 0.00 at $t \geq 2$. The precedent relevant here is procedural: claims of the kind this paper makes, cost bounds and checkability, are the kind that can and should be machine-checked against an implementation, and [10] demonstrates the suite format for doing so.

**Ultrametric quantum computation [11].** This work states a threshold conjecture for ultrametric quantum computation with an explicit argument sketch decomposing the physical error rate at depth $d$ into thermal, gate, dephasing, and leakage terms, and, importantly, a dedicated section listing the negative results that would falsify the hypothesis, the first being the failure to engineer coupling strengths that decay exponentially with depth. This is the falsification-first style Section 6 adopts: every contract family here carries a stated observation that would refute it.

**Prime-valuation reading of error-correcting codes [12].** This work reports a negative result reached by its own falsifiability machinery: reading the parameters $[[n,k,d]]$ of quantum error-correcting codes as prime valuations is definitional for $n$ and $k$ and unavailable for $d$, hence pure relabeling, and the paper says so rather than papering over it. The lesson for contract design is direct: an interpretive mapping between vocabularies earns its place only if it yields checkable consequences beyond the standard formalism. Every clause in Section 3 is therefore stated as a decidable predicate over the simulator's data structures, not as a reinterpretation.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Model and scope

The base model is the one-dimensional Hadamard walk of [9] and [5]: Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_{p} \otimes \mathcal{H}_{c}$ with $\mathcal{H}_{p} = \mathrm{span}\{|x\rangle : x \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ and $\mathcal{H}_{c} = \mathrm{span}\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ of dimension $d_c = 2$; one step $|\psi_{t+1}\rangle = \hat{U}|\psi_{t}\rangle$ with $\hat{U} = \hat{S}(\hat{I} \otimes \hat{C})$, the Hadamard coin $\hat{C} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, and the shift $\hat{S}|x,0\rangle = |x-1,0\rangle$, $\hat{S}|x,1\rangle = |x+1,1\rangle$. The walk starts at $x_0 = 0$ with $\alpha^{(0)}_{0,0} = 1$. After $t$ steps the support lies in $\{-t, \ldots, t\}$, of size $N_t = 2t + 1$, and the state dimension is $d_t = 2(2t+1)$.

Two generalized targets are added. **(G1) Staggered walk on a tessellated graph [8]:** a coin-free discrete-time walk whose step operator is defined through a graph tessellation, on a connected graph $G$ with $N$ vertices and maximum degree $\Delta$. The state dimension is $N$ (no coin factor), and one step moves amplitude only between vertices that share a tile edge, so the support after $t$ steps lies within graph distance $t$ of the start vertex. **(G2) Open-system evolution [6]:** the state is a density matrix $\rho_t$ on the same one-dimensional walk space, evolved under a noise channel from the phase-flip, bit-flip, or generalized-amplitude-damping family studied in [6], with numerical solution by density-matrix evolution as in [6].

### 3.2 Contract set

In the extract-then-discharge style of [7], the following clauses attach to the simulator's step function. K1 through K5 carry over from [9]; K6 through K8 are new.

- **(K1) Unitarity:** $\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{U} = I$, imposing $d(d+1)$ real constraints for state dimension $d$.
- **(K2) Probability conservation:** $\sum_{x}(|\alpha_{x,0}|^{2} + |\alpha_{x,1}|^{2}) = 1$ within tolerance $\varepsilon$, at every step.
- **(K3) Exact support bound:** $\mathrm{supp}(\psi_t) \subseteq \{-t, \ldots, t\}$ on the line, or within graph distance $t$ of the start vertex on $G$; checkable with zero floating-point error by integer arithmetic.
- **(K4) Topological invariant:** the winding number extracted from the walk's band structure equals its model value exactly; per [1], the winding number is the invariant associated with the walk's topological phases and protects boundary bound states.
- **(K5) Periodicity:** for a walk claimed periodic with period $T$, the state at step $t + T$ equals the state at step $t$; instantiated against the proven criterion of [4] that the coin sequence $AABB$ is periodic on the 3-cycle while $AB$, $ABBB$, and $ABBBB$ are not.
- **(K6) Trace conservation (new, for G2):** $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_t) = 1$ within tolerance at every step, the density-matrix analog of K2, required because the channels of [6] are trace-preserving by construction.
- **(K7) Graph support closure (new, for G1):** after $t$ steps, every vertex with nonzero amplitude satisfies $\mathrm{dist}_{G}(v, v_0) \leq t$; checkable by breadth-first distance labeling in integer arithmetic.
- **(K8) Statistical limit clause (new):** for large $t$, the position histogram of the noiseless walk converges to the weak limit stated in [5] for the implemented coin; a statistical, not algebraic, clause.

Clauses K1, K2, and K6 follow from the algebra: $\hat{C}^{\dagger}\hat{C} = \hat{I}$ and $\hat{S}^{\dagger}\hat{S} = \hat{I}$ give $\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{U} = \hat{I}$, hence conservation of $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$; trace-preserving channels give conservation of $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)$. K3 and K7 hold because one step moves amplitude at most one edge. K1 through K7 are decidable predicates over the simulator's data structures, checkable by an SMT-backed verifier of the kind used in the benchmark of [7], where Alt-Ergo and CVC4 discharged all verification conditions but one. K8 is statistical and is discharged by histogram comparison, not by an SMT solver.

### 3.3 Cost model

Costs are counted in multiply-adds (or additions where stated), not wall-clock time; any conversion to time states the assumed machine rate as a projection. For a dense unitarity check on a $d$-dimensional step operator, all $d$ row inner products of length $d$ are formed: cost $d^{3}$. For a sparse check exploiting $k$ nonzeros per column, each of the $d^{2}$ entries of $\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{U}$ costs $k^{2}$ multiply-adds: total $k^{2} d^{2}$. The row-norm-only check costs $2 d$ multiplications plus $d$ additions and certifies normalization, not orthogonality, so it discharges K2-style clauses but not K1. For the periodicity instantiation, the route of [4] block-diagonalizes $U_{k}$ by the similarity transform $U_{k'} = F U_{k} F^{\dagger}$ with the commensurate Fourier matrix, at a cost derived in Section 4.

### 3.4 What is assumed versus what is proven

Three items are assumptions of this paper, stated here so they are never mistaken for results of the cited works: (i) the illustrative graph parameters $N = 100$, $\Delta = 3$ used in Derivation 10; (ii) the specific interleaving schedule of any 3-cycle simulation, which [4] does not specify in the form needed for an implementation; (iii) the linear-accumulation model underlying the drift projection of Derivation 8. The periodicity criterion itself, $AABB$ periodic and $AB$, $ABBB$, $ABBBB$ not, is proven in [4] and is used here as a given.
