# Ultrametricity as a Consistency Condition of Nested Distinctions: A Derivation from a Calculus of Forms

## Abstract

Ultrametricity — the strong triangle condition $d(x,z) \leq \max\{d(x,y), d(y,z)\}$ — is observed in spin-glass state overlap, in cognitive categorization models, and in the tree-like solution structure of integrable systems, yet it is almost always introduced as an imposed axiom or reported as an unexplained numerical coincidence. The open question is whether these convergences are structural identity or mere analogy. We address it by deriving the ultrametric inequality from the two axioms of a calculus of nested distinctions in the tradition of Laws of Form: calling (repeated indication identifies) and crossing (re-crossing a boundary cancels). We prove that forms built under these axioms, which forbid partially overlapping distinctions, reduce to rooted trees, and that the separation depth of two states in such a tree satisfies the ultrametric inequality identically; every state triple is isosceles with the two largest distances equal. A $p$-valued distinction yields a $p$-ary tree whose internal vertices have degree $p+1$, matching Bruhat–Tits branching. Quantitatively, a balanced 4-ary tree of depth 5 encodes $2^{10} = 1024$ states with 341 distinctions versus $\binom{1024}{2} = 523{,}776$ pairwise distances, and its admissible predicate space has $2^{2^{10}} \approx 1.80 \times 10^{308}$ elements. We further derive the null probability $(3L-1)/(2L^2)$ that a random triple of distances on $L$ discrete levels is ultrametric — $11/32 = 0.34375$ at the subitizing limit $L = 4$ — giving a usable statistical test. The result reclassifies ultrametricity from axiom to consistency requirement wherever a system's states are generated by recursive distinction-making.

## 1. Introduction

Researchers who analyze high-dimensional data must repeatedly decide whether similarity among their objects is best represented by a Euclidean metric or by a hierarchy. Astronomers building catalogs of strong gravitational lenses, biologists clustering single-cell transcriptomes into cell types, physicists comparing configurations of frustrated spin systems, and cognitive scientists modeling conceptual categories all face the same representational fork, and the fork changes downstream inference: a Euclidean representation permits overlapping neighborhoods and graded membership, while a hierarchical one forces strict, transitively nested categories. Choosing wrongly either fragments clean categories or imposes boundaries that the data do not support.

The cost of unexamined representational assumptions is quantified in adjacent settings. In strong-lens surveys, the discovery pipeline's image-quality threshold is decisive: raising median $g$-band seeing from $0.8''$ to $0.9''$ loses 17% of discoverable lenses, and the LSST survey is expected to raise the number of known strong lenses from order of hundreds to hundreds of thousands [1]. At that scale, whether the classification layer above the pixel data is metric or hierarchical determines how much of the sample's structure is recoverable. Similarly, in cognitive modeling, one recent quantitative program models the brain's state space as a Bruhat–Tits tree and reports that simulated neural replicas show ultrametric clustering that decisively rejects a Euclidean baseline ($p < 0.001$) [8]. But in that program, as in spin-glass overlap analysis, the ultrametric geometry is postulated at the outset. Nobody has shown why a system built from a specific, elementary operation should land in the ultrametric class at all.

This paper supplies that missing step. We work in a calculus of distinctions in the tradition of Spencer-Brown's Laws of Form: a distinction is a partition of a state space into two sides, and complex forms are built by nesting and combining distinctions. The calculus has two axioms — calling, which identifies a repeated indication with itself, and crossing, which cancels a double traversal of a boundary — and construction canons that forbid two distinctions from partially overlapping. Our claim is that the strong triangle condition is not an additional metric axiom but a consistency requirement of any such calculus: once states are generated by recursive distinction-making, the induced separation function is automatically ultrametric. If the claim holds, the observed ultrametricity of spin-glass overlap spaces, of tree-structured solution cascades in integrable systems, and of hierarchical cognitive models stops being three coincidences and becomes one theorem applied three times. It also delimits the claim: systems whose interactions overlap without nesting are the place to look for violations, and we derive the quantitative null distribution that such violation tests need.

The contributions are:

- A proof that the calling and crossing axioms together with the nesting canons imply that all valid forms reduce to rooted trees, and that the induced separation depth satisfies the ultrametric inequality, with every state triple isosceles having the two largest distances equal (Sections 3 and 4).
- A correspondence showing that a $p$-valued distinction generates a $p$-ary tree whose internal vertices have degree $p+1$, matching the branching of Bruhat–Tits trees (Section 4).
- Exact counting results for the hierarchical encoding: a balanced 4-ary tree of depth 5 represents $2^{10} = 1024$ states using 341 distinctions against $\binom{1024}{2} = 523{,}776$ pairwise distances, and supports $2^{2^{10}} \approx 1.80 \times 10^{308}$ extensionally distinct admissible predicates (Section 4).
- A closed-form null distribution for ultrametricity tests, $\Pr[\text{triple ultrametric}] = (3L-1)/(2L^2)$ for $L$ discrete distance levels, equal to $11/32 = 0.34375$ at the subitizing limit $L = 4$, which turns the proposed recursive-chunking simulation into a sharp statistical test (Section 4).
- Falsification criteria and a concrete next experiment: recursive chunking with a subitizing limit of about 4 items, with the overlap matrices of the resulting categories checked against the derived null (Sections 6 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The ultrametric condition concerns triangles, and the literature we draw on divides into work on triangle constraints, on nested and triangular combinatorial structures, on hierarchy-producing calculi in physics, and on non-Archimedean cognitive and geometric models.

**Triangle constraints as structure generators.** The triangle-free process is the cleanest studied case of a local condition on triangles generating global structure: edges of $K_n$ are added in random order unless the addition creates a triangle [4]. Erdős, Suen and Winkler first bounded the resulting graph's edge count between $\Omega(n^{3/2})$ and $O(n^{3/2}\ln n)$, Spencer conjectured the sharp rate, and Bohman proved that the number of edges is a.a.s. $\Theta(n^{3/2}\sqrt{\ln n})$ [4]. The lesson we import is methodological: a condition stated on every triangle, apparently restrictive, still leaves a rich and quantifiable global object. Our ultrametric inequality is likewise a per-triangle condition, and Section 4 quantifies what it forces and what it forbids. On the geometric side, the triangle altitudes theorem in the hyperbolic plane shows that even classical triangle behavior is ambient-geometry-dependent: the hyperbolic formulation comes with an explicitly computable discriminant that distinguishes the different basic configurations of a triangle's altitudes [3]. This supports our framing that "which triangles are possible" is a discriminating structural test: in Euclidean and hyperbolic geometry the discriminant classifies altitude configurations, while in our setting the ultrametric condition classifies state triples into the single allowed isosceles-with-large-equal-sides pattern.

**Triangular and nested arrays as carriers of algebra.** Triangular arrays with recursive construction encode nontrivial identities. The Hosoya triangle has entries that are products of two Fibonacci numbers, generated by the double recursion $H(r,k) = H(r-1,k) + H(r-2,k)$ and $H(r,k) = H(r-1,k-1) + H(r-2,k-2)$ with initial conditions $H(0,0)=0$, $H(1,0)=0$, $H(1,1)=0$, $H(2,1)=1$, and its geometry yields visual proofs of the Cassini identity, e.g. $F_5 F_3 - F_4^2 = 10 - 9 = (-1)^4$ [2]. Pascal's triangle behaves likewise: the product $s_n$ of all entries in the $n$-th row satisfies Brothers' 2012 limit $\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{s_{n+1}/s_n}{s_n/s_{n-1}} = e$, and Fibonacci polynomials built on the triangle give closed forms for $e^x$ [5]. These results show that hierarchical, recursively indexed structures carry exact analytic content, not merely bookkeeping; our thesis is the structural converse, that a minimal hierarchical calculus already forces a specific geometry.

**Nesting as an exact solution strategy in physics.** The nested algebraic Bethe ansatz solves integrable spin chains by reducing the problem inductively through a chain of subalgebras $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g}_1 \supset \dots \supset \mathfrak{g}_r \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2$, terminating in a problem solvable by the standard ansatz; a recent framework performs this reduction using the residual $U(1)$ charge and the block Gauss decomposition of the $R$-matrix, and is effective for spin chains of all classical types, Lie superalgebras, and open chains [7]. This is hierarchy-as-method: the solution space of the physical system is organized as a tree of nested subproblems. Our theorem predicts precisely this organization for any system whose state generation is recursive distinction-making, and the Bethe-ansatz case supplies a physics domain where the prediction can be checked against known nested structure.

**Enrichment and the transfer of metric structure.** Category-theoretic change of enrichment formalizes how metric-like structure on hom-objects can be moved between base categories. The classical theorem states that an adjunction $L : \mathcal{V} \rightleftarrows \mathcal{W} : R$ between monoidal categories with $L$ strong monoidal induces a change-of-enrichment adjunction between $\mathcal{V}$-categories and $\mathcal{W}$-categories, given by applying $L$ and $R$ to the hom-objects; this has been extended to monoidal model categories, where a Quillen adjunction with lax monoidal right adjoint induces the corresponding adjunction under additional hypotheses such as a unit cylinder axiom [6]. For our purposes this matters because the separation function of a distinction calculus can be read as a hom-object assignment, and results of this kind indicate when such an assignment can be transported between an Archimedean and a non-Archimedean base without destroying the categorical structure.

**Non-Archimedean and ultrametric models of cognition.** One quantitative program models the brain as a macroscopic algebraic solver of the cocycle condition on a Bruhat–Tits tree, arguing that frustrated biological systems demand non-Archimedean state spaces; its simulations of synthetic neural replicas exhibit non-trivial ultrametric clustering, statistical tests reject the Euclidean baseline with $p < 0.001$, and retrieval-time dynamics produce power laws mirroring $p$-adic diffusion [8]. A companion formalism defines a distinction space $D_N = (S_N, d_u)$ on $N$-bit records with $d_u$ an ultrametric by fiat, realized as a $b$-ary tree of depth $L$ with $N = L \log_2 b$ and $|S_N| = b^L = 2^N$, and proves that the number of extensionally distinct admissible existence predicates is exactly $2^{2^N}$ [9]. Both works postulate the ultrametric; the present paper derives it from the distinction operation itself, which is the gap they leave open. The broader non-Archimedean program argues that the continuous real-number framework, with its assumption of infinite divisibility, sits uneasily with discrete quantum transitions, and identifies the empirical barrier: detecting the underlying hierarchical tree requires temporal resolution at the frontier of attosecond technology, which is the primary barrier to verification [10]. Finally, the epistemic frame for the whole enterprise is that cognitive frameworks are biological data-compression heuristics rather than perfect copies of reality, and that the observer cannot step outside the system being observed — a self-referential condition formalized by Gödel's incompleteness results [11]. Our derivation is itself a compression claim: one theorem replaces three domain-specific observations of ultrametricity.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 The calculus of distinctions

We formalize the minimum structure needed. Let $S$ be a set of states (the undifferentiated space). A **distinction** is a binary partition of a region $R \subseteq S$ into two subregions; maintaining a distinction means an observer can reliably re-identify which side of the partition a state lies on [9]. A **form** is any structure obtained from $S$ by finitely many iterated distinctions. Two distinctions interact in exactly two admissible ways:

- **Nesting:** one distinction is drawn entirely inside one side of the other.
- **Disjunction:** the two distinctions are drawn on disjoint regions.

A pair of distinctions that each split part of the other's sides — partial overlap — is not a valid form. This exclusion is the construction canon; in Laws of Form terms, boundaries either cross completely or do not cross at all, and the two axioms govern what remains:

- **Calling (axiom of value):** a repeated indication of the same distinction is identical to one indication; a boundary re-drawn inside itself is the same boundary.
- **Crossing (axiom of orientation):** crossing a boundary twice returns the original state; a distinction inside its own inside is indistinguishable from no distinction at that depth.
