# Noise-Floored Relaxation: A Quantitative Separation between Relaxation in Noisy Quantum Maps and Classical Iterative Algorithms

## Abstract

Iterative relaxation — repeated application of a contraction that drives a state toward a fixed point — appears in two guises: classical iterative linear-algebra algorithms such as Jacobi-type eigenvalue methods, and noisy quantum maps, whose relaxation time has been shown to depend on the chaos of the underlying classical system. What has been missing is a direct, quantitative statement of *how* relaxation in the two settings differs, in a form a practitioner can use to decide which to deploy for a given accuracy target. We formulate two minimal models: a classical deterministic relaxation with contraction factor $\rho$, and a noisy relaxation with deterministic contraction $c$ perturbed each step by independent noise of strength $\epsilon$. We derive exactly the step count to reach a tolerance $\delta$ and the noise floor $\sqrt{\epsilon/(2-\epsilon)}$ that noisy relaxation cannot go below. For representative parameters ($\rho = 0.9$, $\delta = 10^{-6}$), classical relaxation needs 132 steps; a diffusive noisy map with $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ has floor $0.0223672$ and can never reach $\delta$, and would need $\approx 1.42 \times 10^{13}$ steps even at $\epsilon = 10^{-12}$. When chaotic contraction is strong ($c = 0.5$) and $\epsilon = 10^{-7}$, noisy relaxation reaches $\delta$ in 20 steps — a $6.6\times$ step advantage — but only inside the noise window $\epsilon < 8.660254 \times 10^{-7}$. Relaxation in noisy quantum maps therefore differs from its classical counterpart not in kind but in an accuracy-dependent feasibility window, which sets a concrete noise budget for any deployment.

## 1. Introduction

Computational scientists who solve large eigenvalue, linear-system, or fixed-point problems face a recurring choice: iterate a well-understood classical relaxation scheme, or hand the relaxation task to a quantum or quantum-inspired process whose convergence behavior is governed by physics rather than by numerical analysis. The classical side is mature — parallel Jacobi-type algorithms compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors of Hermitian matrices with high relative accuracy when the matrices permit it [4], and exact combinatorial solvers can be distributed across clusters with modest engineering effort [1] — so its cost per step and its convergence rate are known quantities that enter procurement and scheduling decisions directly. The quantum side is far less predictable: the relaxation time of a noisy quantum map on a $2d$-dimensional torus is sensitive to the chaotic behavior of the corresponding classical system, in a way that only reveals itself when the semiclassical limit ($\hbar \to 0$) and the small-noise limit ($\epsilon \to 0$) are taken simultaneously [6]. A practitioner who cannot translate that statement into a step count and an accuracy floor has no way to decide whether the quantum route is a speedup or a trap.

The cost of not having this translation is concrete. If a team adopts a noisy relaxation process on the strength of its fast transient convergence, and the process carries an irreducible noise floor above the required tolerance $\delta$, the computation silently stalls: every additional step buys nothing, and the accuracy target is unreachable at any runtime. Conversely, if the team demands classical-grade accuracy from every quantum procedure, it may discard procedures that reach moderate tolerances several times faster than the classical baseline. Both errors stem from the same gap: the literature states that noisy quantum relaxation *differs* from classical relaxation [6], and that classical–quantum interpolations trade speedup against depth continuously [5], but neither statement is expressed as a feasibility condition on $(\epsilon, \delta, c, \rho)$ that an implementer can evaluate. This paper supplies that condition and its arithmetic.

The approach is deliberately minimal. We write down two scalar recursions — a deterministic classical relaxation $d_{t+1} = \rho\, d_t$ and a noisy relaxation $d_{t+1} = c\, d_t + \epsilon\, \eta_t$ with independent, zero-mean, unit-second-moment perturbations $\eta_t$ — and derive, with every arithmetic step shown, (i) the exact step count $t^*$ to reach tolerance $\delta$ in each model, (ii) the noise floor of the noisy model, (iii) the feasibility window $\epsilon < \delta\sqrt{1-c^2}$ under which the noisy model can reach $\delta$ at all, and (iv) the step ratio between the models inside that window. The models are not simulations of any specific device; they are the common skeleton that any relaxation process, classical or noisy-quantum, must reduce to at the level of a single error mode. The contribution is the exact algebra of that skeleton and the deployment decision it settles.

The paper's contributions are:

- A closed-form step count $t^*_{\mathrm{cl}} = \ln\delta / \ln\rho$ for deterministic classical relaxation, evaluated to 132 steps for $\rho = 0.9$ and $\delta = 10^{-6}$ (Section 4.1).
- An exact noise floor $d_\infty = \sqrt{\epsilon/(2-\epsilon)}$ for diffusive noisy relaxation, evaluated to $0.0223672$ at $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$, together with the derived feasibility threshold $\epsilon_{\max} = 2\delta^2/(1+\delta^2) \approx 2.0\times 10^{-12}$ for $\delta = 10^{-6}$ and the corresponding step count $\approx 1.42\times 10^{13}$ (Section 4.2).
- A general feasibility window $\epsilon < \delta\sqrt{1-c^2}$ for noisy relaxation with deterministic contraction $c$, evaluated for $c = 0.5$ to the threshold $8.660254\times 10^{-7}$, and a step count of 20 steps at $\epsilon = 10^{-7}$, giving a $6.6\times$ step advantage over the classical baseline inside the window (Section 4.3).
- A falsification criterion and a limitations analysis identifying exactly which modeling assumptions each number rests on (Section 6), and a deployment decision rule with technology-readiness assessment and a smallest-next verification calculation (Section 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

This section reviews the twelve supplied works and states, for each, only what its supplied entry supports.

The classical side of the comparison is anchored by parallel Jacobi-type eigenvalue methods. The entry on novel modifications of parallel Jacobi algorithms [4] describes two main classes of one-sided trigonometric and hyperbolic Jacobi-type algorithms for computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors of Hermitian matrices, states that these algorithms exhibit significant advantages over many other eigenvalue algorithms, and states that both types compute eigenpairs with high relative accuracy when the matrices permit; it also announces novel parallel variants. This is the canonical instance of what we call classical relaxation: a repeated, contractive rotation sweep whose per-step cost and convergence behavior are properties of the algorithm, not of a physical device. Our classical model in Section 3.1 is an abstraction of exactly this kind of iteration. The entry supplies no convergence-rate numbers, so we choose the contraction factor $\rho = 0.9$ as an explicit model parameter rather than as a claim about any specific Jacobi implementation.

The engineering context for classical exact computation is given by the distributed maximum-clique work [1], which takes an existing implementation of an exact maximum-clique algorithm, modifies it to run over an ad-hoc cluster of machines, and aims at a maximum "costup" — a significant speedup for minimal development effort — via a simple modification to a state-of-the-art exact algorithm. The relevance to this paper is the framing it establishes: the practical value of an algorithmic change is measured as a ratio of achieved speedup to engineering cost, which is precisely the ratio (step advantage versus noise-budget burden) that we compute for noisy relaxation in Section 4.3. The entry supplies no speedup figures, so we borrow only its framing.

Two further classical-algorithm entries fill out the landscape in which relaxation-based solvers compete. The submodular-maximization entry [2] studies maximizing a non-negative, monotone, submodular function over $n$ elements under a cardinality constraint $k$ (SMCC), an NP-hard problem with applications in machine learning and influence maximization; it notes that although polynomial-time approximation algorithms exist in theory, practical solving often involves frequently querying the submodular function. Frequent querying is a relaxation-like cost driver, and the entry's observation that theory and practice diverge parallels the divergence between asymptotic step counts and feasibility windows that we formalize. The TSP-with-neighborhoods entry [3] presents approximation results for the Euclidean TSP with neighborhoods (TSPN), a generalization of the classical Euclidean TSP that is also NP-hard, including a constant-factor approximation for arbitrary connected neighborhoods. It is included here as a further example of the classical algorithmic baseline; its entry supplies no relaxation content, and we draw no quantitative claim from it. Two consensus and labeling entries complete the classical set: the consensus-tree entry [7] presents two deterministic algorithms — majority-rule (+) consensus in $O(kn)$ time, which is optimal since the input size is $\Omega(kn)$, and frequency-difference consensus in $\min(O(kn^2), O(\cdot))$ time — illustrating that classical algorithmics can sometimes achieve provably optimal runtime, a standard the noisy models of this paper cannot meet asymptotically; and the odd-gracefulness entry [8] defines odd graceful labelings of a graph $G=(V,E)$ and provides generalized odd graceful solutions for the tensor product of two line graphs, noting that despite many published graph-labeling results there are few algorithms for actually labeling graphs. Its relevance is limited to the observation, from its own text, that algorithmic supply can lag behind structural knowledge — a gap this paper addresses on the relaxation side.

The quantum side begins with the quantized toral maps entry [6], which introduces the notion of relaxation time for noisy quantum maps on the $2d$-dimensional torus as a generalization of previously studied dissipation time, and shows that relaxation time is sensitive to the chaotic behavior of the corresponding classical system when the semiclassical limit ($\hbar \to 0$) and the small-noise limit ($\epsilon \to 0$) are taken simultaneously. This is the paper's central quantum-side source: it establishes that relaxation in the quantum setting is a noise-controlled quantity entangled with classical chaos, which is exactly the structure our model $d_{t+1} = c\, d_t + \epsilon\, \eta_t$ isolates — the contraction $c$ encodes the classical dynamics (chaotic dynamics permit strong contraction), and $\epsilon$ encodes the noise. The entry supplies no explicit scaling law, so Section 4 derives the consequences from the model, not from [6].

The interpolation entry [5] shows that the scaling of the $\alpha$-QPE family of phase-estimation algorithms — for which Wang, Higgott and Brierley showed a continuous trade-off between quantum speedup and circuit depth — can be naturally and succinctly derived within the quantum singular value transformation framework, and notes that phase (or amplitude) estimation admits a quadratic quantum speedup. This supports the paper's premise that quantum advantage is a continuous, parameter-dependent quantity rather than a binary one; our feasibility window $\epsilon < \delta\sqrt{1-c^2}$ is the analogous continuous boundary for relaxation. The entry supplies no relaxation-specific numbers.

Three QNFO corpus entries connect the analysis to the broader program of programming physical systems. The rule-110 emulation entry [10] reports that elementary cellular automaton rule 110, though Turing complete, has a manifestly chiral local update rule, and that it can nevertheless be emulated by alternating, in a fixed spatial sequence, three constituent elementary cellular automaton rules, yielding emergent universality from symmetric rules. We cite it as evidence that dynamical systems with simple local update structure can realize complex computation, the same substrate question that noisy quantum maps raise; the entry supplies no relaxation content. The QEC–Darwinism entry [11] reports that Maity et al. established a model-independent no-go theorem under which quantum error correction and Quantum Darwinism cannot coexist above a critical logical fidelity $F_L > 0.874$, an exact tradeoff between protected quantum information and emergent classical objectivity. This is directly analogous in form to our feasibility window: a sharp threshold separating two operational regimes. We use the threshold $F_L = 0.874$ only as quoted in the entry, as a qualitative parallel; no derivation in this paper depends on it. Finally, the pre-registered falsification entry [12], titled "A Pre-Registered Falsification of Deterministic Measurement-Triggered Relaxation," has an empty supplied summary, which gives no further detail; we relate it to this work only through its title, which indicates that relaxation claims in this area are subject to pre-registered falsification practice — a practice our Section 6 adopts by stating in advance exactly what observation would falsify the paper's central result. The corpus overview entry [9], "QNFO: Programming the Vacuum," likewise has an empty supplied summary and supplies no further detail; it is listed for completeness of the corpus and supports no claim in this paper.

In summary, the supplied literature establishes (a) a mature classical relaxation baseline with high relative accuracy [4] and an engineering culture of measuring speedup per unit effort [1, 2], (b) a quantum relaxation concept whose time scale is noise- and chaos-dependent [6] and whose advantages are continuous trade-offs [5], and (c) precedent for sharp feasibility thresholds in quantum–classical tradeoffs [11] and for pre-registered falsification of relaxation claims [12]. What no supplied entry provides is the explicit algebra connecting $\epsilon$, $\delta$, $c$, and $\rho$ to a step count and a feasibility verdict; that algebra is the subject of Sections 3 and 4.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Model definitions

We track a single scalar error mode: the deviation $d_t \geq 0$ of the iterate from its target at step $t$, with initial deviation $d_0 = 1$ in normalized units. All results are stated for one mode; a full problem with many modes is treated mode-by-mode under the same algebra, and we return to this assumption in Section 6.

**Model C (classical deterministic relaxation).** The deviation evolves as

$$d_{t+1} = \rho\, d_t, \qquad 0 < \rho < 1,$$

where $\rho$ is the spectral radius of the iteration matrix in the relaxation's asymptotic regime. This is the standard asymptotic form of any stationary contractive iteration, including the Jacobi-type sweeps of [4]. The model is deterministic: absent finite-precision arithmetic, the deviation decays without bound. A finite-precision implementation has its own floor set by its unit roundoff $u$, a parameter of the implementation; we carry $u$ symbolically and assert no value for it.

**Model Q (noisy relaxation).** The deviation evolves as

$$d_{t+1} = c\, d_t + \epsilon\, \eta_t, \qquad 0 \leq c < 1,$$

where $c$ is the deterministic contraction per step, $\epsilon > 0$ is a dimensionless noise strength, and $\eta_t$ are independent, zero-mean perturbations with $\mathbb{E}[\eta_t^2] = 1$. The parameter $c$ encodes the deterministic dynamics that the map implements: for the quantized maps of [6], chaotic classical dynamics can support strong per-step contraction, so we treat $c$ as a free parameter and specialize to $c = 0.5$ for the worked example. The parameter $\epsilon$ is the small-noise strength of [6], taken per step.

### 3.2 Metrics

For each model and each accuracy target $\delta \in (0,1)$ we define:

- **Feasibility:** the model is feasible for $(\epsilon, \delta, c)$ if its stationary deviation is below $\delta$, i.e. $\lim_{t\to\infty} \mathbb{E}[d_t^2] < \delta^2$.
- **Step count $t^*$:** the smallest integer $t$ with $\mathbb{E}[d_t^2] \leq \delta^2$, computed when feasible.
- **Step ratio $S$:** $S = t^*_{\mathrm{cl}} / t^*_{\mathrm{q}}$, the classical step count divided by the noisy step count, both for the same $\delta$.

We use second moments throughout because the noisy model's deviation is a random variable; for the deterministic model the second moment is the square of the deviation itself.

### 3.3 Assumptions

(A1) The noise enters additively at the level of the deviation, independently at each step, with zero mean and unit second moment. (A2) The contraction factors $\rho$ and $c$ are constant in time. (A3) The initial deviation is $d_0 = 1$. (A4) The accuracy target $\delta$ is specified on the same normalized scale as $d_0$. Each of these is examined in Section 6.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Classical step count

From Model C, $d_t = \rho^t d_0 = \rho^t$ by (A3). Requiring $d_t \leq \delta$:

$$\rho^t \leq \delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t \geq \frac{\ln \delta}{\ln \rho},$$

where both logarithms are negative, so the ratio is positive. Hence

$$t^*_{\mathrm{cl}} = \left\lceil \frac{\ln \delta}{\ln \rho} \right\rceil.$$
