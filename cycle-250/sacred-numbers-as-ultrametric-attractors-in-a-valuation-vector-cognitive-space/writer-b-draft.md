# Sacred Numbers as Ultrametric Attractors: A Valuation-Vector Model of Cross-Cultural Numerical Convergence

## Abstract

Certain integers recur as ritually significant across unconnected civilizations: 12, 60, 108, and 360 appear as subdivision and calendrical numbers, while 7, 13, and 17 appear as numbers of indivisibility, completion, or transcendence. Existing accounts treat these convergences as independent cultural accidents. The gap this paper addresses is the absence of a formal, base-independent quantity that predicts which integers become sacred. We model each integer by its prime-exponent valuation vector and place these vectors in an ultrametric space generated by a hierarchical divisibility tree, drawing on the representation of ultrametrics by rooted trees and combs. We define a subdivision utility equal to the divisor count $\tau(n)$ and compute it exactly for a candidate sacred set, comparing each member against an exactly enumerated magnitude-matched window. The subdivision-attractor members exceed their matched-window mean divisor counts by factors of $1.82$ ($12$), $2.29$ ($60$), $2.09$ ($108$), and $3.08$ ($360$), with a geometric mean of $2.27$; the indivisibility-attractor members ($7$, $13$, $17$) sit at the minimal divisor count $\tau=2$ and occupy a single deep leaf of the divisibility tree. The ultrametric distance table reproduces the two-cluster structure reported across cultures. This supplies a falsifiable, base-independent target for cognitive experiments and cultural-transmission models.

## 1. Introduction

Calendar designers, temple builders, ritual legislators, and merchants in unconnected societies repeatedly converge on the same small integers. The Babylonian base-60 system, whose superior divisibility still shapes our measurement of angles and time, is documented in the comparative analysis of number systems in [9], which also notes that the Mayan vigesimal system arose from counting on fingers and toes and that base-10 itself is a historical contingency of human anatomy rather than a mathematical necessity. A designer who must partition a circle, a month, or a herd into equal parts faces a real constraint: the number of equal groups available is exactly the divisor count of the chosen integer. Choosing 360 over 359 for angular degrees is not aesthetic preference; it is the difference between twenty-four exact subdivisions and exactly two. What is missing is an explanation of why the *same* integers, and not merely any highly divisible ones, recur, and why a second, opposite class of numbers — primes such as 7, 13, and 17 — recurs as a symbol of indivisibility and transcendence.

If the recurrence is driven by invariant multiplicative structure rather than by cultural contact, three things change. First, the set of integers expected to become sacred across civilizations becomes quantitatively predictable before any ethnographic corpus is consulted. Second, cognitive experiments on number intuition gain a controlled variable: integers can be matched in archimedean magnitude while differing arbitrarily in multiplicative structure, isolating divisibility as a perceptual primitive. Third, models of cultural transmission gain a concrete selection pressure — subdivision utility — whose equilibrium set can be computed and compared against the historical record.

This paper builds that formal object. Each integer $n$ is represented by its valuation vector $v(n) = (a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots)$, where $n = \prod_i p_i^{a_i}$ and $p_i$ is the $i$-th prime. We place valuation vectors in an ultrametric space — a metric space in which the triangle inequality is strengthened to $d(x,z) \le \max\{d(x,y), d(y,z)\}$, so that every triple of points forms an isosceles triangle with the two long sides equal — by means of a hierarchical divisibility tree. Ultrametric spaces of exactly this tree-generated kind are well studied: every dendrogram, that is, every nested family of partitions of a finite set, is represented by an ultrametric on that set [7]; compact ultrametric spaces are isometric to comb metric spaces built from hierarchical functions on an interval [6]; and ultrametrics arise generically in high-dimensional Euclidean data, with the distance matrix completely determined by coordinate variances [4]. The divisibility tree is a dendrogram over the integers, so the machinery of these papers applies directly. The cognitive claim — that a non-archimedean sense of divisibility operates as a primitive alongside the archimedean sense of magnitude — is situated in a literature that treats cognitive value assignment as functional and evolutionarily shaped [2], that documents autonomously active, self-organized neural dynamics as a substrate for cognition [3], and that models conceptual hierarchies with $p$-adic, ultrametric structures in cognitive science [12]. The claim that symbols such as numbers emerge through social interaction rather than design, and must be studied as dynamically evolving sign systems, is the central thesis of the symbol-emergence survey [8]; the claim that what survives cultural transmission is invariant structure rather than arbitrary notation is argued in the relational-patternist synthesis, which holds that words and bases are arbitrary transformations while the structural relationships they describe are not, and that the universe "does not speak in base-10 integers" [10]. The functional anatomy of the sacred, which treats sacredness as a universal human function rather than a content of belief [11], supplies the framing for why a cognitive primitive would be recruited into ritual.

The contributions are:

- A base-independent representation of integers as valuation vectors embedded in an ultrametric divisibility tree, with the ultrametric inequality verified explicitly on the sacred-number set (Section 3 and Section 4).
- Exact divisor-count computations for the candidate sacred set $\{7, 12, 13, 17, 60, 108, 360\}$ and for four magnitude-matched control windows, yielding separation ratios of $1.82$, $2.29$, $2.09$, and $3.08$ and a geometric-mean separation of $2.27$ (Section 4).
- A two-attractor classification — subdivision attractor (high $\tau$) and indivisibility attractor ($\tau = 2$) — shown to coincide with the two deep leaves of the divisibility tree, with the full ultrametric distance table computed (Sections 4 and 5).
- A statement of the cognitive, corpus-statistical, and transmission-model tests that would confirm or falsify the model, with the smallest next experiment specified (Sections 6 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The mathematical backbone of this paper is the theory of ultrametric spaces. An ultrametric satisfies the strong triangle inequality $d(x,y) \le d(x,z) \lor d(z,y)$, where $\lor$ is the maximum operator [1]; the canonical examples are the field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ of $p$-adic numbers and the ring of $m$-adic numbers [4]. Three representation theorems make the divisibility-tree construction rigorous. First, for every dendrogram — a nested family of partitions over a finite set — there exists an ultrametric representing it [7]; our tree that splits integers by divisibility by $2$, then $3$, then $5$, and so on, is precisely such a nested family, so the induced distance on integers is an ultrametric by this theorem. Second, the comb representation: a comb-like function $f$ on an interval induces an ultrametric $\bar{d}_f(s,t) = \sup_{(s \wedge t, s \vee t)} f$, and every compact ultrametric space endowed with a suitable measure is globally isometric to such a comb metric space [6]. This matters here because it guarantees that the hierarchical structure we assign to the integers is not one representation among many but the canonical metric content of the space. Third, the embedding, extension, and interpolation theory of ultrametrics [1] shows that ultrametric spaces behave analogously to ordinary metric spaces under isometric embedding (an ultrametric version of the Arens–Eells theorem) and extension (an ultrametric Hausdorff theorem), so the finite divisibility-tree space constructed in Section 3 can be extended without obstruction if finer prime levels are added.

The stochastic-generation result of [4] supplies the reason ultrametric structure is cognitively plausible rather than exotic. That paper proves that the Euclidean metric on random points in an $n$-dimensional Euclidean space, for a special class of distributions, converges in probability to an ultrametric as $n \to \infty$, with the ultrametric distance matrix completely determined by the variances of the point coordinates, and demonstrates the algorithm by explicit calculations of distance matrices and ultrametricity indexes. Read cognitively: when a mind represents many objects along many partially independent features, hierarchical (tree-like) similarity structure is the generic outcome, not a special construction. The valuation vector of an integer is exactly such a feature vector — its coordinates are the exponents of $2, 3, 5, 7, \ldots$ — and the ultrametricity that emerges generically in high dimensions is the structure we impose deliberately.

On the cognitive side, three works frame the claim that divisibility is a perceptual primitive. The survey of autonomously active neural networks argues that the brain's self-generated eigendynamics has a central regulating role in cognitive information processing rather than serving as background noise, and that most neural-network models to date are stimulus-response systems unlike the brain's continuously active architecture [3]. A cognitive sense of divisibility would be carried by such endogenous dynamics, not by a stimulus-response lookup of multiplication tables. The cognition-and-emotion analysis argues that emotional control functions as an intermediate layer in the space of policies, that optimizing life-long Darwinian fitness is a primary task of emotional control, and that emotional control operates by diffusive neuromodulation linked to reinforcement signals [2]. This supplies the motivational mechanism: a number that affords many exact subdivisions of food, land, or time carries reinforcement value, and that value is tagged affectively rather than computed arithmetically at each use. The $p$-adic primer in the simulation-inconsistency outline reports that $p$-adic numbers are already used in cognitive science to model conceptual hierarchies, proposing that the brain's cognitive architecture may reflect an underlying $p$-adic, ultrametric substrate with a hierarchical temporal window of integration [12]. Our proposal is the number-theoretic specialization of that general claim: the hierarchy is the divisibility tree.

On the cultural side, the symbol-emergence survey establishes that symbols in human society are not given by a designer but emerge and dynamically evolve through social interaction, and that symbol systems include culturally determined signs beyond language [8]. Sacred numbers are paradigm cases of such emergent signs: no committee chose 108, yet it recurs. The relational-patternist synthesis argues that words and bases are arbitrary transformations while structural relationships are invariant, and identifies the scientific project as finding invariant proportions hidden beneath anthropocentric coordinate systems [10]. Our valuation vector is exactly such an invariant: it is unchanged by rewriting $n$ in any base, which is why the model predicts convergence across civilizations that use different numeral systems. The geometric-physics analysis independently documents the cultural contingency of base choice — Babylonian base-60 with its superior divisibility, Mayan base-20, and the anatomical accident behind base-10 [9] — and its observation that base-60 "continues to influence our measurement of angles and time" is the historical anchor for our subdivision-attractor claim. Finally, the functional anatomy of ultimate concern argues that the sacred/secular division is not a division of cognitive machinery — the same hardware of belonging, coherence, and self-transcendence serves monk and physicist alike — and proposes universal functions, including a perceptual function that decides what counts as real, that appear in every culture [11]. Sacred numbers sit at the junction of that perceptual function and the subdivision utility formalized here: a number is sacred when its multiplicative structure is extreme in one of two directions, and extremity in either direction makes it perceptually salient as a category of one.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Valuation vectors

Every integer $n \ge 1$ has a unique factorization

$$n = \prod_{i=1}^{\infty} p_i^{a_i}, \qquad a_i \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0},$$

where $p_1 = 2, p_2 = 3, p_3 = 5, p_4 = 7, \ldots$. The valuation vector is $v(n) = (a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots)$, with finitely many nonzero entries. This representation is base-independent: $v(n)$ is a theorem about the integer $n$, not about the numeral that denotes it, so two cultures using base-10, base-60, or base-20 notations confront the same object. The divisor count is

$$\tau(n) = \prod_{i} (a_i + 1).$$

We take $\tau(n)$ as the subdivision utility: a set of $\tau(n)$ equal shares (and every intermediate regular subdivision) is available for a quantity of size $n$.

### 3.2 The divisibility tree and its ultrametric

Define a nested family of partitions of the positive integers. Level $k$ refines level $k-1$ by the answer to the question "is $n$ divisible by $p_k$?" Level 1 separates integers divisible by 2 from those not; level 2 crosses in divisibility by 3; and so on. The cells after level $k$ are the $2^k$ residue-pattern classes with respect to $(p_1, \ldots, p_k)$. This is a dendrogram — a nested family of partitions — over the finite sets it is restricted to, and by the representation theorem for dendrograms [7] there is an ultrametric representing it. Explicitly, for integers $n, m$ define

$$d(n, m) = 2^{-k(n,m)},$$

where $k(n,m)$ is the largest $k$ such that $n$ and $m$ give the same divisibility answer for every prime $p_1, \ldots, p_k$. Because the cells form a nested hierarchy, $d$ satisfies the strong triangle inequality: for any triple, two of the three pairwise "first divergence" levels coincide and are minimal, so the two largest distances are equal, which is the defining isosceles property of ultrametric triples [4]. We verify this directly on the sacred set in Section 4.
