# The Emergence of Spacetime: Relational Order, Dimensional Selection, and the Boundary-Walk Bound on Geometric Emergence

## Abstract

Spacetime is conventionally treated as a pre-existing arena, yet its ultimate nature remains unresolved, and research programmes that treat geometry as emergent lack computable, falsifiable measures of how much geometric structure a relational substrate actually produces. This paper addresses that gap by formalizing the claim that spacetime is an emergent relational structure as a quantitative statement about networks of interacting elementary processes. We model the relational substrate as a $d$-dimensional lattice of process-events split into observational modules and apply a predecessor result of the framework: for route-accumulating observations, the emergence discrepancy equals the count of boundary-touching walks. We derive three results with full arithmetic. First, the expected number of boundary-touching steps of an $n$-step observation is $2n/(dL)$ on a lattice of linear size $L$, so geometric emergence is linear in observation horizon and inverse in system size; for $d=3$, $L=100$, $n=100$ this gives $0.667$. Second, the count of distinct influence paths between events separated by graph distance $r$ scales as $(2d-1)^r$, giving $9{,}765{,}625$ paths at $r=10$ in three dimensions against $59{,}049$ in two. Third, a random-walk recurrence argument shows $d=3$ is the marginal dimension separating recurrent from transient influence propagation. These results convert the thesis that spacetime is emergent into testable scaling laws and identify which observational quantities a simulation of geometrogenesis must measure.

## 1. Introduction

The problem this paper bears on is concrete: physicists, cosmologists, and philosophers of physics currently have no accepted account of what spacetime is at the deepest level, and this gap blocks progress on quantum gravity, on the interpretation of dark matter and dark energy, and on any programme that hopes to derive rather than assume the geometric stage of physics. The standard model of cosmology treats the metric of spacetime as a fundamental dynamical variable coupled to matter; quantum mechanics treats time as an external parameter. The two treatments coexist uneasily, and every attempt to quantize gravity inherits that unease. A researcher deciding whether to invest in a programme that derives geometry from a substrate — rather than quantizing a classical metric — currently has no computable quantity to guide that decision: no measure of how much geometry a given relational substrate produces, at what rate, or in which dimensions.

The cost of this gap is visible in the fragmentation of the field. One line of work modifies the gravitational action itself to explain cosmic acceleration; a specific instance is Palatini $f(R)$ gravity, which [3] shows to be the unique fixed point of a transformation involving a special conformal rescaling of the metric, a peculiarity the authors read as signaling deep problems that make the theory unphysical. Another line infers new substances: dark matter is standardly taken to constitute about $25\%$ of the mass-energy of the Universe and to interact with ordinary matter only gravitationally [4]. A third line builds geometry from entanglement, but a direct test of that programme would require a complete theory of quantum gravity, which does not yet exist, and the natural informational measures of integration are computationally intractable: the paper connecting geometrogenesis and biogenesis notes that computing integrated information $\Phi$ is an NP-hard problem, intractable for any system larger than a few components [13]. In each case the researcher faces the same decision under uncertainty: is geometry fundamental, modified, or emergent, and if emergent, can its emergence be measured at all?

If the results of this paper hold, that decision changes. The thesis that spacetime is not a container but the ordered totality of relations among elementary processes — the position developed in the autaxic framework, in which distinguishable process-patterns and their ordered relations constitute what we measure as space, and the sequential unfolding of process constitutes time [12] — becomes a statement with quantitative content: the amount of emergent geometric structure produced by a relational substrate is bounded by a quantity computable from the interface between observational modules, and the dimensionality of the emergent geometry is selected by a computable stability criterion. A researcher can then ask of any candidate substrate: what is its interface fraction, its path-branching factor, and its recurrence threshold? These are numbers, not slogans.

This paper is a revision and strengthening of the chapter "The Emergence of Spacetime" [12]. The original chapter argued the emergence thesis qualitatively: space arises from distinguishability and ordered relations among process-patterns, dimensionality reflects the optimal complexity of the relational matrix, distance measures relational effort, time is processual unfolding, and gravity is the dynamics of the emergent relational matrix rather than a force in it. Those arguments are retained here. What is added is formalization: the relational substrate is modeled as a graph, the emergence discrepancy is given an exact combinatorial meaning, and the dimensional-selection argument is given an explicit calculation.
