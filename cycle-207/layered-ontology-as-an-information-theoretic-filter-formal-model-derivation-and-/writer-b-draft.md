# The Brain as a Capacity-Limited Filter: A Channel Model That Distinguishes Filtering from Production Theories of Consciousness

## Abstract

Clinical investigators who study near-death experiences, psychedelic therapy, and deep meditation currently lack a falsifiable framework that says whether the brain generates consciousness or merely constrains access to it; without one, phenomenological reports from altered states cannot be turned into quantitative predictions that discriminate between these families of theories. We address this gap by formalizing the "brain as filter" conjecture as a channel-capacity model. A three-layer ontology (public-physical, private-phenomenal, transcendent-informational) is mapped onto a communication channel in which the brain imposes a state-dependent attenuation $A_s$ on a substrate signal, so that phenomenal capacity is $C_s = \log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR}_0/A_s)$. Under stated assumptions ($\mathrm{SNR}_0 = 10$, representative attenuations of 100 for waking and 4 for psychedelic states), the model yields a capacity ratio of $13.1$ between altered and waking states, a predicted self-model-dissolution prevalence of $0.80$ versus a negligible waking baseline, and a required sample of $15$ participants per group to separate this from a production-model prevalence of $0.30$ at 80% power. The model also predicts scale invariance—capacity unchanged under joint rescaling of signal and neural noise—which activity-proportional production models violate. We specify the reanalysis that would falsify each prediction and assess the framework at TRL 1–2.

## 1. Introduction

Researchers who run clinical studies of altered consciousness face an interpretation problem that costs their field its most distinctive data. Near-death experiences, psychedelic sessions, and deep meditative states produce reports of self-model dissolution, timelessness, and unity, but the leading theories of consciousness—production theories, in which the brain generates phenomenal content, and filtering theories, in which the brain constrains access to a wider substrate—can both accommodate these reports after the fact. A clinical team designing a psychedelic-assisted therapy trial, or a thanatology group documenting near-death reports, currently has no quantitative criterion that would let a dataset vote for one family of theories over the other. The practical stakes are concrete: trial designs, report instruments, and analysis endpoints are chosen differently depending on whether altered-state phenomenology is treated as disordered generation (a target for normalization) or as reduced constraint (a signal to be measured). If a filtering model produced numerical predictions that a production model does not share, endpoints could be chosen to discriminate between them before the next wave of studies is run.

The decision that would change if the result of this paper holds is the choice of analysis endpoint and sample size in altered-states research. Under a filtering model with the parameters derived here, the informative measurement is a capacity proxy that is invariant to overall neural activation, and the informative comparison is a binary signature prevalence across states; under a production model, the informative measurement is the activation level itself. These are different experiments.

This paper builds the missing formal bridge. We adopt a three-layer ontology—public-physical (Layer 1), private-phenomenal (Layer 2), and transcendent-informational (Layer 3)—in which the brain tightly couples Layer 2 to Layer 1 while filtering direct awareness of Layer 3. The philosophical motivation for an interface view of perception, in which experience is a simplified representation rather than a copy of the underlying substrate, has been developed in the QNFO corpus [13], [14], and the terminological care needed to handle first-person reports of transcendent experience has been laid out in [12]. Our contribution is to convert this picture into a channel-capacity model with explicit, checkable arithmetic.

The contributions are:

1. A formalization of the three-layer filter hypothesis as a channel-capacity model with a state-dependent attenuation parameter $A_s$, an effective signal-to-noise ratio, and a capacity formula $C_s = \log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR}_0/A_s)$ (Section 3).
2. A derived capacity ratio of $13.1$ between representative altered and waking states, with all inputs stated and every arithmetic step shown, plus an ordinal ranking of four states (Section 4).
3. A scale-invariance discriminator: the filter model predicts capacity invariance under joint rescaling of substrate signal and neural noise, while production models in which capacity is proportional to neural activation predict strict proportionality (Section 4).
4. A sample-size derivation: $15$ participants per group to separate a predicted altered-state signature prevalence of $0.80$ from a production-model prevalence of $0.30$ at 80% power and two-sided $\alpha = 0.05$ (Section 4).
5. A falsification protocol and a technology-readiness assessment locating the work at TRL 1–2, with the smallest next test named (Sections 6 and 7).
