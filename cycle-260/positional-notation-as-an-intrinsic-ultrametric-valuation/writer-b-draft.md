# Positional Notation Natively Encodes an Ultrametric: Valuation Axioms, Digit-String Trees, and the Quantified Divergence from the Archimedean Line

## Abstract

Every positional numeral is read through two unstated choices: the radix $b$ and a metric on the numbers represented. Context: positional notation is conventionally taught and engineered as a structure on the Archimedean line, yet the digit strings themselves form a rooted tree. Gap: the claim that positional notation is natively ultrametric has circulated as a conjecture without an explicit axiom check, a precise notation-to-nesting correspondence, or a quantified statement of how far the tree metric and the Euclidean line metric disagree. Approach: we define $v_b(n)$ as the largest $k$ with $b^k \mid n$, prove that $d_b(x,y) = b^{-v_b(x-y)}$ satisfies the strong triangle inequality for every integer $b \geq 2$, prove that $v_b$ is multiplicative if and only if $b$ is prime (with the counterexample $2 \times 5 = 10$ in base $10$), prove that the $b$-adic distance equals $b$ raised to the negative of the trailing-digit agreement depth, and formalize a nested-mark calculus whose nesting depth realizes exactly that depth. Principal result: on the integers $0,\ldots,99$ the mean $b$-adic distance is $\frac{101}{110} \approx 0.918$ against a diameter-normalized Euclidean mean of $\frac{101}{297} \approx 0.340$, a ratio of exactly $2.70$; all $855$ pairs at Euclidean distance at most $9$ are maximally distant in the $b$-adic metric. Practice: educators, arithmetic-library designers, and cryptographers gain an explicit axiom-level account of which metric their representations carry, and a computed measure of when the two metrics conflict.

## 1. Introduction

Anyone who teaches place value, designs arithmetic hardware, or chooses a distance for clustering numeric data is implicitly choosing a geometry for the integers, usually without knowing it. The school number line presents integers as equally spaced points on a straight line; floating-point and fixed-point libraries inherit that picture through their rounding and comparison semantics; data analysts embed counts in Euclidean space before any clustering is attempted [5]. The alternative picture — that a digit string is a path through a rooted tree and that the natural distance between two numbers is the depth of their lowest common ancestor — has been stated repeatedly in the ultrametric literature [10], [11], [12], but it has not, in the sources available here, been carried through with an explicit verification of the valuation axioms for arbitrary bases, an explicit correspondence with a nested-mark formalism, or a computed measure of how much the two geometries disagree on a concrete set of integers.

The cost of leaving this implicit is concrete. A numeral string cannot specify its own base: the string "10" denotes two in binary, ten in decimal, and sixty in sexagesimal, so every base names itself "base-10" from within [10], [11]. That ambiguity is a documented source of error in computing practice and has been proposed as a cryptographic resource [11]. The equal-interval assumption is a second silent parameter: integers possess the successor order but no intrinsic distance, and the picture of equally spaced points on a line is a geometric import reified into an apparent property of the numbers themselves [10]. If the tree picture is the native one, then decisions that treat Euclidean closeness as fundamental — rounding policies, similarity search over numeric keys, pedagogical sequencing — are built on an undeclared frame, and one wants to know how large the discrepancy is, not merely that it exists. If the result holds, a curriculum designer can state precisely which pairs of numbers the two geometries order differently; a library designer can decide per operation whether divisibility-level (tree) or magnitude-level (line) closeness is the operative quantity; a cryptographer can evaluate schemes that exploit the difference [11].

This paper derives the tree-native claim at the level of axioms and quantifies the divergence.
