# Ultrametric Nested Semantic Graphs: A Language-Neutral Meaning Representation with Provable Hierarchical Distance Structure

## Abstract

Cross-lingual semantic search and machine translation struggle at the morphological extremes: keyword-based systems treat a polysynthetic verb-word, which encodes an entire proposition, as one opaque token, while pipeline semantic parsers are built per language and do not transfer. This paper asks whether sentences across the morphological spectrum can share a single meaning representation whose distances are mathematically disciplined. We define the nested semantic graph (NSG), a rooted constituent tree over semantic units whose node distances derive from lowest-common-ancestor heights in a fixed semantic hierarchy, and we prove two results. First, the lowest-common-ancestor height function is an ultrametric on nodes, and the Hausdorff distance induced by any ultrametric on finite node sets is itself an ultrametric on graphs; hence the proposed graph distance $d_{\mathcal{G}}$ satisfies the strong triangle inequality exactly, with no approximation. Second, the natural alternative that averages pairwise node distances violates the ultrametric inequality on a three-graph counterexample ($5 > \max\{3,3\}$). We compute the search space this representation faces: $58{,}786$ binary bracketings for a 12-word sentence, $1{,}860{,}480$ injective node mappings for a 5-node pattern in a 20-node graph, and a $500\times$ parameter reduction from pairwise distances to tree merges at 1,000 nodes. The representation is a candidate substrate for typology-independent retrieval; its empirical adequacy on parallel corpora remains an open, testable question.

## 1. Introduction

A speaker of Inuktitut or Mohawk can pack an entire proposition — who acts on whom, with what — into a single verb word. Every mainstream retrieval and translation stack is built on the opposite assumption: that meaning is distributed over a sequence of word-sized tokens. A keyword index over a polysynthetic corpus cannot match an English query sentence to a document whose relevant sentence is one word long; the query's tokens simply do not co-occur with the document's tokens. The people who face this problem daily are documentary linguists building searchable archives for endangered languages, translators and machine-translation developers working between typologically distant languages, and archivists of multilingual parliamentary or legal corpora. What blocks them today is that each language typically receives its own parser, its own annotation scheme, and its own similarity function, so a system built for English is rebuilt, not retuned, for Turkish, and is often never built at all for polysynthetic languages. A representation in which an English sentence, a Turkish sentence, and a one-word polysynthetic sentence all become graphs of the same kind, with distances that obey a provable three-point consistency law, would change the design decision: one similarity function and one indexing structure would serve every morphology, and retrieval quality would no longer depend on how a language packages meaning into words.

This paper does not claim that such a representation has been validated on data. It establishes the mathematical skeleton that any such claim must stand on, and it derives the combinatorial costs that any implementation will pay. The central objects are nested semantic graphs: rooted trees whose leaves are semantic units (participant roles, event types) and whose internal nodes are semantic constituents, with every node carrying a signature that locates it in a fixed, language-independent semantic hierarchy. Because the hierarchy is a tree, the distance between two nodes — the height of their lowest common ancestor — automatically satisfies the strong triangle inequality, the defining property of ultrametrics [1]. Because the Hausdorff construction lifts any ultrametric on points to an ultrametric on finite sets, the same inequality holds for whole graphs. This gives the representation a topology that matches the hierarchical structure long attributed to semantic memory in cognitive modeling [9] and recently exploited for auditable attention mechanisms [12].

The contributions are:

- A formal definition of the nested semantic graph and its node signature hierarchy, and a proof that the induced node distance is an ultrametric (Section 4.1).
- A proof that the Hausdorff distance over an ultrametric base is an ultrametric on finite graphs, so the graph distance $d_{\mathcal{G}}$ satisfies $d_{\mathcal{G}}(G_1,G_3) \le \max\{d_{\mathcal{G}}(G_1,G_2),\, d_{\mathcal{G}}(G_2,G_3)\}$ exactly (Section 4.2).
- An explicit three-graph counterexample showing that the averaging alternative $d_{\mathrm{avg}}$ violates the ultrametric inequality, with all distances displayed (Section 4.3).
- Exact computations of the representation's combinatorial costs: $C_{11} = 58{,}786$ binary bracketings for a 12-word sentence, $1{,}860{,}480$ injective mappings for a 5-node pattern in a 20-node graph, and a compression factor of exactly $500$ from pairwise distances to tree parameters at $n = 1000$ nodes (Section 4.4).
- A statement of the empirical program — parallel-corpus parsing and retrieval simulation — as a proposal with explicit falsification conditions, not as a completed study (Sections 6 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

Ultrametricity is the mathematical thread that connects this paper's proposal to prior work in geometry, representation theory, and cognitive modeling. An ultrametric is a metric $d$ satisfying the strong triangle inequality $d(x,y) \le d(x,z) \lor d(z,y)$, where $\lor$ is the maximum operator; this is the definition given at the start of [1], which then proves ultrametric analogues of three classical theorems for ordinary metric spaces: the Arens–Eells isometric embedding theorem, the Hausdorff extension theorem, and the Niemytzki–Tychonoff characterization of ultrametrizable spaces. The extension theorem in [1] is proved by adapting Toruńczyk's method and replacing the Dugunddy extension theorem with a zero-dimensional Michael continuous selection theorem for ultrametrizable spaces. These results matter here because they establish that ultrametric spaces are not degenerate curiosities but support embedding and extension machinery comparable to Euclidean geometry; if graph-level semantic distances are ultrametric, this machinery becomes available to semantic applications.

The canonical construction of ultrametrics from trees appears in [7]. That work defines a comb as a map $f$ from a compact interval to $[0,\infty)$ with $\{f \ge \varepsilon\}$ finite for every $\varepsilon > 0$, and shows that the induced distance $\bar{d}_f(s,t) = \sup_{(s \wedge t, s \vee t)} f$ is ultrametric, with the proof resting on the observation that for any rooted tree, the sphere of points at equal graph distance from the root carries an ultrametric. Theorem 3.1 of [7] goes further: every compact ultrametric space with a suitable measure admits a comb representation via a global isometry. This is exactly the construction pattern this paper reuses — distances from ancestor heights in a tree — and it supplies the guarantee that our node-level distance is not an ad hoc formula but an instance of a well-studied correspondence between trees and ultrametrics, one that also underlies phylogenetic and population-genetic applications described in [7].

Within artificial intelligence, the claim that semantic memory is hierarchically organized is asserted in [9], which argues that semantic memory relies on ultrametric topologies to compress and retrieve abstract concepts, that statistical $n$-gram models suffer a combinatorial explosion that makes long-term reasoning intractable, and that cross-lingual zero-shot inference in large language models suggests internalized invariant structure. Section 7.4 of [9] concedes that mapping such invariant structures across massively scaled datasets remains open work. Our paper takes a different route to the same destination: rather than extracting topology from trained model weights, we define it axiomatically and prove its properties, which makes the resulting distance auditable by construction. The same orientation appears in [12], which presents a proof-of-concept ultrametric attention mechanism using a binary-tree proxy encoder; its reported limitations are instructive for us — the rigid 4-bit vocabulary restricts latent semantic discovery, the $N=10$ sequence constraint prevents observation of large-scale sparsity dynamics, and the authors state that the design trades generative flexibility for interpretability. Our proposal inherits exactly this trade-off and generalizes the node structure from a fixed binary vocabulary to a typed semantic hierarchy. [10] supplies the broader non-Archimedean framing: it argues that discrete, tree-like (Bruhat–Tits) topologies and continuous Archimedean geometries should be treated as complementary views of one system, and its Section 5.0 explicitly reserves a slot for cognitive and linguistic applications of tree-navigation metrics. We cite it for the architectural claim that hierarchical and continuous representations can coexist in one framework, which is the position our retrieval design assumes when it keeps a continuous similarity score alongside the ultrametric filter.

On the graph-theoretic side, three works constrain what our nested graphs can look like and how they can be drawn or decomposed. [2] studies square-contact representations of planar graphs and characterizes the partial 2-trees and triconnected simply-nested graphs that admit them, using SPQ-tree decompositions traversed bottom-up to assemble drawings from per-node pieces. This bottom-up, tree-decomposition-driven methodology is the pattern we propose for rendering or laying out nested semantic graphs, and the class results tell us which constituent-tree shapes remain planar and therefore drawable without edge crossings in a human-facing interface. [5] proves bounds on the planar slope number $\mathrm{psn}(G)$ — the minimum number of edge slopes in a planar straight-line drawing — showing $\mathrm{psn}(G) \le \max\{4, \Delta\}$ for Halin graphs and a quadratic bound for nested pseudotrees, and notes that this is the first polynomial slope bound for graphs of treewidth 4. Since our nested graphs with cross-edges between constituents are close relatives of nested pseudotrees, these bounds indicate that compact, low-complexity visualizations exist for the structures we define. [4] contributes the word-to-graph direction: a graph is word-representable when its edges are exactly the pairs of letters that alternate in some word, and [4] develops the representation number for word-representable split graphs, building on the characterization of split graphs as $(2K_2, C_4, C_5)$-free graphs. This matters because a polysynthetic word-sentence is literally a word, and word-representability theory is the existing formal bridge between linear word order and graph structure; our leaf-ordering constraint (constituent leaves must appear in a linear order compatible with the surface word) is a restricted version of the representability question [4] studies.

Two algebraic works supply the compositional machinery. [8] proves that an algebra with nilpotent Jacobson radical and a generalized matrix unit is isomorphic to a generalized path algebra with weak relations, and develops the representation theory of such algebras through module systems indexed by the graph's vertex set. Path algebras are the standard device for turning a directed graph with relations into an algebra whose representations are the graph's representations; for nested semantic graphs, this offers a route to treating semantic composition as an algebraic operation with provable functorial properties. [3] shows how a structured object with infinitely many levels — the ring of symmetric functions — is built as a limit of an inverse system $B_n = \mathbb{Z}[x_1,\dots,x_n]^{\mathfrak{S}_n}$ with maps that forget the last variable, and carries out a categorification of this limit. The inverse-limit pattern is precisely what a "language-neutral" hierarchy needs: the level-$n$ semantic hierarchy should map onto the level-$(n-1)$ hierarchy by forgetting the finest distinctions, and [3] demonstrates that such limits can carry rich algebraic structure rather than collapsing. Finally, [6], which classifies irreducible representations of hyper loop algebras over non-algebraically closed fields and constructs Weyl modules with base-change results between field extensions, is cited here for a single methodological point its proofs make explicit: in its Appendix A.7, an object defined over the larger field is shown to be generated over the subfield by an explicit polynomial construction. The analogy we draw is methodological only — that representations defined over a richer structure can be descended to a sparser one by explicit constructions — and we flag it as an analogy, not a dependency.

## 3. Methods

This is a theory paper. Its method is formal definition, proof, and exact combinatorial computation; it performs no experiment, no corpus annotation, and no simulation, and every empirical suggestion appears in Sections 6 and 7 as a proposal.

**Definition 3.1 (Semantic hierarchy).** A semantic hierarchy is a finite rooted tree $H$ with height function $h_H: V(H) \to \mathbb{N}$, where leaves have $h_H = 0$ and $h_H(\text{parent}) = 1 + \max_{\text{children}} h_H$. Leaves represent atomic semantic types (for example, person, artifact, transfer-event); internal nodes represent superordinate classes. We assume a single fixed $H$ shared by all languages; the construction of $H$ itself is an empirical problem we do not solve here (Section 6).

**Definition 3.2 (Nested semantic graph).** A nested semantic graph (NSG) is a triple $G = (T, \lambda, R)$ where $T$ is a rooted constituent tree whose leaves are semantic units, $\lambda$ labels each node $u$ with a signature $\sigma(u)$ drawn from a signature set $\Sigma$, and $R$ is a set of directed relation edges between non-ancestor node pairs (for example, agent-of, theme-of). The signature $\sigma(u)$ is the pair (semantic type of $u$ in $H$, multiset of relation-labeled child signatures). The signature set $\Sigma$ is itself organized as a refinement tree: two signatures are siblings under a parent signature when they agree on all but the finest structural detail.

**Definition 3.3 (Node distance).** For nodes $u \in G_1$, $v \in G_2$, let $\ell(u), \ell(v) \in \Sigma$ be their signatures, and define
$$d_n(u,v) = h_\Sigma\big(\mathrm{lca}(\ell(u), \ell(v))\big),$$
the height in the signature tree $\Sigma$ of the lowest common ancestor of the two signatures.

**Definition 3.4 (Graph distance).** For NSGs $G_1, G_2$ with node sets $U_1, U_2$, define
$$d_{\mathcal{G}}(G_1, G_2) = \max\Big\{ \max_{u \in U_1} \min_{v \in U_2} d_n(u,v),\; \max_{v \in U_2} \min_{u \in U_1} d_n(u,v) \Big\},$$
the Hausdorff distance between $U_1$ and $U_2$ under $d_n$.

The proof strategy is: (i) show $d_n$ is an ultrametric using the standard lowest-common-ancestor argument, mirroring the tree-sphere construction of [7]; (ii) show the Hausdorff lift preserves the ultrametric inequality, by a two-step triangle argument through an intermediate set; (iii) exhibit a counterexample for the averaging alternative; (iv) compute the combinatorial quantities by direct arithmetic from their definitions. All input numbers are either definitional (tree heights, node counts) or computed in Section 4 with every step shown.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 The node distance is an ultrametric

**Proposition 4.1.** $d_n$ satisfies $d_n(u,w) \le \max\{d_n(u,v),\, d_n(v,w)\}$ for all nodes $u,v,w$.

*Proof.* Write $a = \ell(u)$, $b = \ell(v)$, $c = \ell(w)$, all leaves of the signature tree $\Sigma$. Let $p = \mathrm{lca}(a,c)$ and $q = \mathrm{lca}(a,b)$. In a tree, the three pairwise lowest common ancestors $\mathrm{lca}(a,b)$, $\mathrm{lca}(b,c)$, $\mathrm{lca}(a,c)$ are totally ordered by the descendant relation, and $\mathrm{lca}(a,c)$ is either $\mathrm{lca}(a,b)$ or $\mathrm{lca}(b,c)$ — the two of them cannot be strictly incomparable, because both are ancestors of $a$, so one is an ancestor of the other, and the deeper of the two is then also an ancestor of $b$ or of $c$ respectively, forcing equality with $\mathrm{lca}(a,c)$ in the first case or being an ancestor of it in the second. Concretely: if $\mathrm{lca}(a,b)$ is a descendant of $\mathrm{lca}(a,c)$, then $\mathrm{lca}(a,b)$ lies on the path from $\mathrm{lca}(a,c)$ down to $a$; since $b$ is not under $\mathrm{lca}(a,c)$'s subtree containing $a$ unless $\mathrm{lca}(a,b) = \mathrm{lca}(a,c)$, we get $\mathrm{lca}(a,b) = \mathrm{lca}(a,c)$. Otherwise $\mathrm{lca}(a,b)$ is an ancestor of (or equal to) $\mathrm{lca}(a,c)$, and symmetrically $\mathrm{lca}(b,c)$ must then equal $\mathrm{lca}(a,c)$. Hence
$$h_\Sigma(\mathrm{lca}(a,c)) = \max\{h_\Sigma(\mathrm{lca}(a,b)),\, h_\Sigma(\mathrm{lca}(b,c))\} \le \max\{d_n(u,v),\, d_n(v,w)\},$$
because $h_\Sigma(\mathrm{lca}(a,b)) = d_n(u,v)$
