# Holographic Quantum Error Correction on Bruhat–Tits Trees: A Conditional Threshold and Resource Analysis at $10^{-4}$

## Abstract

We assess, quantitatively and conditionally, whether a quantum error correction (QEC) architecture in which the encoding map is realized as renormalization-group (RG) flow on a Bruhat–Tits tree $\mathcal{T}_p$ — the $p$-adic analogue of hyperbolic anti-de Sitter space — can achieve a QEC threshold of at least $10^{-4}$. The proposal's own analysis reports favorable margins at specific error channels but supplies no resource accounting, and no hardware data or full error-model simulation accompanies it; the gap is that no published derivation connects the $10^{-4}$ threshold hypothesis to the qubit, distance, and measurement-bandwidth cost of operating below it. We construct an explicit conditional model: independent site errors on a tree of valency $q = p+1$, a concatenation-style logical error law $\epsilon_L = A(\epsilon/\epsilon_{th})^{(d+1)/2}$, and a target failure probability of $0.01$ over $10^9$ correction cycles. Under these assumptions we derive, with full arithmetic, that (i) at a physical error rate exactly equal to $10^{-4}$ the model provides no protection, so the threshold claim is meaningful only strictly below $10^{-4}$; (ii) at $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$ a code distance $d = 21$ suffices, requiring $88{,}573$ tree sites per logical qubit on $\mathcal{T}_3$ and a measurement bandwidth of $8.8573 \times 10^{10}$ events per second per logical qubit at a $10^6$ Hz cycle rate — about $201$ times the syndrome bandwidth of a distance-21 surface code under comparable assumptions; (iii) a shallower $d = 11$ code meets the same target only if $\epsilon \le 1.4678 \times 10^{-6}$; and (iv) operating at half the threshold inflates the site count to $\approx 2.25 \times 10^{17}$ per logical qubit. A complementary convention modeling the RG step as a quadratic map $p_{\ell+1} = \kappa\, p_\ell^{\,2}$ yields the equivalent condition that the threshold equals $1/\kappa$, so the $10^{-4}$ target holds if and only if $\kappa \le 10^{4}$. The threshold hypothesis is internally consistent but operationally demanding; we identify the measurements whose outcomes would confirm or falsify it.

## 1. Introduction

Teams engineering fault-tolerant quantum computers face a resource wall that is measured, today, in physical qubits per logical qubit and in syndrome bits per second. The main obstacle to practical quantum computing is limited qubit count, which is why fault-tolerant architecture research concentrates on floorplans that minimize the memory overhead of logical operations while preserving fault tolerance [5]. Running a code, not merely constructing one, continuously consumes qubits, measurement bandwidth, classical processing, and energy, so a threshold number is meaningless until it is paired with the cost of operating below it [10]. And the cost is not static: the operational lifetime of a fault-tolerant machine is a race between the continuous degradation of physical hardware — the system–environment coupling strength $\gamma$ grows over the processor's lifetime as material defects accumulate — and the escalating classical cost of the error correction that compensates for that degradation [11]. An architecture proposal that claims a low threshold therefore owes the community three numbers: the distance required at a realistic operating point, the physical-qubit count that distance implies, and the measurement bandwidth the correction cycle demands.

This paper supplies those three numbers, conditionally, for a specific new architecture: holographic QEC organized as renormalization-group (RG) flow on a Bruhat–Tits tree $\mathcal{T}_p$, the $p$-adic analogue of hyperbolic anti-de Sitter (AdS) space, so that the encoding map of a holographic code is literally an RG trajectory from boundary degrees of freedom into the bulk [9]. The claim under test in [9] is that this architecture achieves a QEC threshold of at least $10^{-4}$. A threshold, in the QEC sense used throughout this paper, is a physical error rate $\epsilon_{th}$ below which increasing code distance $d$ drives the logical error rate $\epsilon_L$ to zero. The proposal's own results section reports an analytic tree threshold of $p^* = 1/4$ for the bit-flip channel, an effective bit-flip rate of $2.703 \times 10^{-8}$ per mode per cycle after cat-code suppression (a margin of $3.70 \times 10^{3}$ over $10^{-4}$), and a conservative no-inner-code cross-check of $4.9 \times 10^{-5}$ that sits below $10^{-4}$ with thin margin [9]. What [9] does not report is the resource side of the ledger: how much distance, how many physical sites, and how much measurement bandwidth a design operating below $10^{-4}$ actually needs. If the answer is that a modest distance suffices at a realistic operating point, the architecture becomes a serious candidate for co-design work of the kind that produced hardware-aware training at the scale of 2,048 GPUs [8]; if the answer is that the required site count explodes as the operating point approaches the threshold, the architecture is confined to a narrow corridor of parameter space regardless of how favorable its threshold is.

Because the proposal is new and no experimental or simulated error data accompanies it, every number in this paper is either (a) an input stated explicitly as an assumption of the conditional model, or (b) derived from such inputs by arithmetic shown in full in Section 4. We do not claim to have measured or simulated anything. Our contribution is a transparent conditional analysis: given the model of Section 3, the threshold hypothesis at $10^{-4}$ has precise, checkable consequences, and we compute them.

The contributions are:

- A derivation that at $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$ (one order of magnitude below the hypothesized threshold) the reliability target of one failure in $10^9$ cycles requires code distance $d \ge 21$, with the corresponding tree volume $V_3(10) = 88{,}573$ sites per logical qubit and measurement bandwidth $8.8573 \times 10^{10}$ events per second per logical qubit (Sections 4.2, 4.4, 4.5).
- A derivation that a shallower $d = 11$ code meets the same target only if $\epsilon \le 1.4678 \times 10^{-6}$, a factor of $\approx 68.13$ below the hypothesized threshold (Section 4.3).
- A derivation of the near-threshold resource explosion: at $\epsilon = 5 \times 10^{-5}$ the required distance rises to $d \ge 73$ and the site count to $\approx 2.2514 \times 10^{17}$ per logical qubit, a factor of $\approx 2.5419 \times 10^{12}$ over the $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$ operating point (Section 4.6).
- A comparison, in shown arithmetic, of the tree architecture's bandwidth and site count against the surface-code baselines reported in [10] and [12] (Section 4.9).
- The equivalence, under a complementary quadratic-RG convention, of the $10^{-4}$ target with the condition $\kappa \le 10^{4}$ on the RG amplification constant, which makes the threshold hypothesis a single measurable proposition (Section 4.8).

## 2. Background and Related Work

This section situates the analysis relative to the twelve works numbered [1]–[12], attributing to each only what its own text states.

**[9] Holographic QEC as AdS/CFT RG flow on Bruhat–Tits trees.** This is the proposal under assessment. Its introduction frames the question quantitatively: whether the architecture, in which the encoding of a logical qubit into boundary modes is an RG flow on $\mathcal{T}_p$ and logical information lives in an infrared fixed-point subspace of that flow, achieves a QEC threshold of at least $10^{-4}$ [9]. Its results section reports, with the arithmetic carried out in its own Section 4: raw physical error rates of $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ (single-qubit), $4.4 \times 10^{-3}$ (two-qubit), and $1.3 \times 10^{-2}$ (readout); an analytic tree threshold of $p^* = 1/4$ for bit-flip and $3/8$ for depolarizing; cat-code suppression giving $p_{bf} = e^{-12} = 6.14 \times 10^{-6}$ per loss event at mean photon number $\bar{n} = 6$; an effective bit-flip rate of $2.703 \times 10^{-8}$ per mode per cycle, a margin of $3.70 \times 10^{3}$ over $10^{-4}$; a tightest-channel margin of $19.2$ (readout-charged dephasing $1.3 \times 10^{-2}$ against a dual-basis threshold of $0.25$); a depth-2 logical error of $1.4 \times 10^{-29}$ in the independence regime, labeled a mechanism demonstration rather than a prediction; and a conservative no-inner-code cross-check of $4.9 \times 10^{-5}$ at two-qubit error $4.4 \times 10^{-3}$, below $10^{-4}$ with thin margin [9]. Its outlook section names two falsifiable propositions: a survivability condition $p_{corr} \le 7.7 \times 10^{-6}$ per subtree per cycle in the readout-dominated channel, testable by crosstalk tomography, and an ultrametric signature of the encoding testable on trapped-ion simulators [9]. Our paper takes the $10^{-4}$ hypothesis from [9] as its input and supplies what [9] does not: the distance, site count, and bandwidth that operating below that threshold requires.

**[10] The resource burden of active QEC.** This work develops a transparent scaling analysis of what it costs to *run* a code rather than construct one, and its results table is directly comparable to ours: at physical error rate $10^{-3}$, a target logical error rate of $10^{-12}$ demands a distance-21 surface code with $881$ physical qubits per logical qubit, $9240$ syndrome bits per logical operation ($440$ bits per cycle, i.e. $4.40 \times 10^{8}$ bits per second per logical qubit at a $1\,\mu$s cycle), and — under a stated $10^{-9}$ J per-qubit-per-cycle assumption — $1.8501 \times 10^{-5}$ J per logical operation, $6.445 \times 10^{18}$ times the Landauer limit at 300 mK [10]. Its conclusion states that energy grows as the cube of the code distance and that the burden is logarithmically robust to model prefactors but linearly exposed to per-qubit energy [10]. We adopt its accounting frame (qubits, bandwidth, energy) and use its distance-21 surface-code numbers as the baseline against which the tree architecture's $88{,}573$ sites and $8.8573 \times 10^{10}$ events per second are compared in Section 4.9.

**[12] The scaling paradox of surface-code QEC.** This work resolves an apparent contradiction between two resource narratives about the same code: qubit overhead grows only as the square of a quantity logarithmic in the target reliability, while the Landauer erasure floor grows linearly in distance [12]. Its computed results include: at $p = 10^{-3}$, $d = 9$ with $n = 161$ qubits for $p_L = 10^{-6}$, $d = 21$ with $n = 881$ for $p_L = 10^{-12}$, and $d = 27$ with $n = 1457$ for $p_L = 10^{-15}$; at $p = 10^{-4}$, $d = 10$ with $n = 199$ for $p_L = 10^{-12}$, so a tenfold hardware improvement cuts the overhead ratio from $880$ to $198$, a factor of $4.44$; a marginal cost of $\Delta d = 2$ and roughly $176$–$192$ physical qubits per decade of logical reliability; and a syndrome stream of $4.4 \times 10^{14}$ bits per second for $10^{6}$ logical qubits [12]. Two of its findings structure our analysis: improving the physical error rate from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$ buys a $4.44\times$ overhead reduction, which is why we evaluate the tree architecture at operating points a factor of $10$ and more below its hypothesized threshold; and the thermodynamic floor rises with distance, which is why our near-threshold explosion (Section 4.6) is a resource result and not merely an aesthetic one.

**[11] Lifecycle of a fault-tolerant quantum computer.** This work models the operational lifetime of a fault-tolerant machine as a race between continuous hardware degradation and the escalating classical cost of the error correction that compensates for it [11]. Its physical model is specific: the decoherence rate is governed by the product of the dimensionless system–environment coupling strength $\gamma$ and the temperature $T$, and the effective coupling strength is not fixed but degrades — increases — over the operational lifetime as material defects accumulate [11]. This matters for our conditional analysis because it denies the assumption of a static physical error rate: an architecture designed to operate at $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$ must be designed to tolerate drift of $\epsilon$ upward over the machine's life, and Section 4.6 quantifies how expensive drift toward the threshold is in our model.

**[5] LSQCA: resource-efficient load/store architecture for limited-scale FTQC.** This work identifies the memory overhead required to perform logical operations on surface-code qubits as the drawback of surface-code-based architectures, and its mission is to design floorplans that raise memory density, on the premise that limited qubit count is the main obstacle to practical quantum computing [5]. Its evaluation uses concrete instances — $433$ logical qubits for a quantum adder, $280$ for Bernstein–Vazirani, $400$ for a multiplier, $143$ for an $11 \times 11$ Heisenberg model circuit — and concludes that its load/store architecture improves memory density while concealing memory-access latency [5]. We treat the Bruhat–Tits tree volume $V_3(R)$ computed in Section 4.4 as the analogous overhead quantity for the holographic architecture: the number of physical sites that must be fabricated and measured per logical qubit.

**[3] Dalorex: data-local program execution for memory-bound applications.** This work characterizes memory-bound workloads by three challenges: low compute-per-data ratio with little and unpredictable data reuse; frequent fine-grained irregular memory accesses that make memory hierarchies inefficient; and atomic accesses, synchronization, and load imbalance that yield poor core utilization, with the further observation that memory bottlenecks persist due to limited off-chip bandwidth even when prefetching, decoupling, or pipelining mitigate latency [3]. Its evaluation demonstrates strong scaling on four graph algorithms and one sparse linear algebra kernel [3]. Syndrome data on a $\mathcal{T}_3$ tree has exactly the low-reuse, irregular-access character Dalorex targets, so the classical decoding side of the architecture we analyze inherits this bandwidth wall; we return to it in Section 7.

**[2] Architectural implications of graph neural networks.** This work builds a GNN benchmark suite from a survey of 53 models, decomposed into stages, and finds that although GNNs have a large design space the stage-level characteristics are stable enough to guide hardware acceleration [2]. Its result analysis reports a counter-intuitive outcome: a TPU does not achieve the best end-to-end performance on graph workloads despite the best dense GEMM performance, because on the small Cora dataset it must rely on the CPU for data movement, while on the larger MUTAG dataset the GPU wins through sparse-stage fusion at higher graph sparsity [2]. Any classical co-processor that decodes a tree-structured QEC code faces the same workload class — graph-structured computation whose dense-math throughput does not predict end-to-end decoding latency — which is why we flag decoding throughput, not quantum measurement, as a co-equal risk in Section 6.

**[8] Insights into DeepSeek-V3.** This work documents how the rapid scaling of large language models exposed constraints in memory capacity, computational efficiency, and interconnection bandwidth, and how DeepSeek-V3, trained on 2,048 NVIDIA H800 GPUs, addressed them through hardware-aware model co-design [8]. Its discussion section distills architectural insights for future hardware, including the convergence of scale-up and scale-out architectures and the need for hardware-supported ordering guarantees in memory-semantic communication [8]. The transferable lesson for QEC architecture is methodological: the error channel is the workload and the tree interconnect is the hardware, and they must be co-designed — which is precisely what our conditional model does by tying the resource numbers to a stated error law.

**[6] Assessing spread in sets of software architecture designs.** This work notes that optimization-based generation of architecture design alternatives produces a different set of alternatives per experiment, and proposes the maximum architectural spread (MAS) indicator, computed by encoding the sequences of modifications that generate each architecture and applying distance metrics, to estimate how far apart the alternatives are [6]. We use its framing at the meta-level: the proposal of [9] should be evaluated as one point in the family $\{\mathcal{T}_p, d, \epsilon\}$ — or equivalently over the range of the amplification constant $\kappa$ of Section 4.8 — rather than as a single design, and our Section 4.6 is exactly a probe of how sharply the resource cost varies across that family.

**[4] QVLM architecture and SQuID dataset.** This work's main finding is architectural: patch-based vision encoders cannot preserve the pixel-level indexing required for counting and measurement, with benchmarks showing the best model reaching only $33.5\%$ accuracy on building counting against $82.7\%$ on scene classification, and with the principle that operating on spatial masks outperforms direct compressed encoding [4]. The analogy for our problem is direct and cautionary: RG flow on a tree coarse-grains error information exactly as a patch encoder coarse-grains an image, and the threshold question is whether the information needed for correction survives the coarse-graining. Our Section 4.8 makes that question quantitative through the amplification constant $\kappa$.

**[1] Stochastic model-based proxy servers for VoD.** This work states that in a video-on-demand system the main repository may be far from the user with limited streaming capacity, that high-quality video requires high bandwidth, and that a distributed architecture with multiple local servers achieves a higher hit ratio and reduced client waiting time [1]. The structural mapping onto our problem is: a central decoder fed by syndrome streams from the whole tree corresponds to the bandwidth-limited central repository, and distributing decoding across tree subtrees corresponds to the distributed-server remedy. We use it in Section 7 as the precedent for a distributed-decoding pathway.

**[7] Reconfigurable DSP architecture using field-programmable DSP arrays.** This work observes that digital signal processing functions are generally implemented either on inflexible ASICs or on FPGAs with lower utilization or speed, and proposes the Field Programmable DSP Array (FPDA) as a reconfigurable middle ground made feasible by modern integrated-circuit technology [7]. Real-time syndrome decoding at the bandwidths computed in Section 4.5 is a DSP throughput problem, so reconfigurable DSP fabrics are a candidate implementation substrate for the classical decoding layer; we return to this in Section 7.

No work among [1]–[12] provides experimental QEC data for the Bruhat–Tits-tree architecture; the quantitative content of this paper is its own conditional derivation, anchored to the threshold hypothesis and error-channel numbers that [9] states.
