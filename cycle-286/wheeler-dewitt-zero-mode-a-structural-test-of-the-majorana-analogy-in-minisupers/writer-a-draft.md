# Wheeler‑DeWitt Zero‑Mode: A Structural Test of the Majorana Analogy in Minisuperspace

## Abstract
The Wheeler–DeWitt (WDW) constraint $\hat H\Psi=0$ yields a stationary “zero‑mode” that dominates minisuperspace quantum cosmology. We test the hypothesis that this zero‑mode can be interpreted as a Majorana‑type mode, which would require (i) a particle–hole (self‑conjugate) symmetry, (ii) a non‑zero topological index, and (iii) spectral isolation with exponential protection under symmetry‑preserving deformations. Using the exactly solvable Hawking–Page wormhole minisuperspace model, we (a) map the hidden $U(1,1)$ symmetry onto a particle‑hole‑like involution and show that it fails the Bogoliubov–de Gennes anti‑unitary condition, (b) compute the Fredholm index of the WDW operator and find it to be zero, in contrast to the non‑zero chiral index of Dirac operators that protect Majorana modes, and (c) demonstrate that $E=0$ is not an isolated eigenvalue, with the benchmark exponential splitting $e^{-L/\xi}=4.54\times10^{-5}$ at $L/\xi=10$ illustrating the magnitude of protection that is absent. The analysis falsifies the Majorana‑type interpretation and indicates that the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode does not provide a decoherence‑free sector for quantum information processing.

## 1. Introduction
Researchers in quantum gravity and topological quantum computing confront a shared and costly conceptual problem: the term “zero mode” is used for mathematically distinct objects, and analogies built on the shared label can misguide research programs and allocate resources inefficiently. A quantum cosmologist who treats the Wheeler–DeWitt kernel as a protected Majorana‑type state expects a robust, deformation‑stable sector that could be harnessed for fault‑tolerant quantum information. Conversely, a topological‑qubit engineer who imports that expectation may design hardware predicated on a non‑existent protection mechanism, wasting experimental effort. Today the conjecture that the WDW zero‑mode is a Majorana‑type topological zero mode remains untested; no work has examined the Wheeler–DeWitt kernel against the defining structural criteria of Majorana modes—particle–hole symmetry, a non‑zero index, and spectral isolation. The decision that would change if our result holds is whether to pursue a research line that treats the WDW kernel as a candidate decoherence‑free resource. We demonstrate that the analogy fails on all three criteria, thereby redirecting effort toward more promising avenues.

## 2. Background and Related Work
Zero‑mode concepts appear in a variety of quantum‑field‑theoretic and condensed‑matter contexts. The eight works listed in the bibliography each provide a concrete structural element that informs our analysis.

1. **Constrained zero modes in DLCQ.** Ref. [1] shows that zero‑momentum modes in discrete light‑cone quantisation (DLCQ) are constrained by spatial averages of the Euler–Lagrange equations and that their inclusion improves convergence, reducing the error from $\mathcal O(1/K)$ to $\mathcal O(1/K^{2})$. This illustrates that zero modes can be non‑dynamical yet essential for the correct spectral structure of a constrained Hamiltonian.

2. **Functional determinant with a gauged‑out zero mode.** Ref. [2] derives the functional determinant of a second‑order operator $F=-\frac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{\ddot g}{g}$ after gauging out its zero mode $g(\tau)$. The treatment emphasizes that the presence of a zero mode modifies the one‑loop pre‑exponential factor in quantum cosmology, highlighting the subtle role of zero modes in path‑integral measures.

3. **Virasoro zero‑mode flow in critical TMG.** Ref. [3] constructs additive parameters $s,\bar s$ that generate a finite Virasoro zero‑mode flow $U(s,\bar s)=e^{-sL_{0}-\bar s\bar L_{0}}$ acting on a logarithmic graviton pair. The commuting zero‑mode generators $L_{0},\bar L_{0}$ provide a concrete example of a symmetry algebra built from zero‑mode operators.

4. **Zero‑mode action in TMG logarithmic sector.** Ref. [4] further develops the Virasoro zero‑mode flow, showing that the nilpotent part $N$ satisfies $N^{2}=0$ and that the flow separates into a semisimple conformal factor and a nilpotent mixing term. This decomposition mirrors the structure of particle–hole transformations that mix creation and annihilation operators.

5. **Zero‑mode contributions in twist‑3 PDFs.** Ref. [5] identifies explicit $\delta(x)$ contributions arising from zero‑mode residues in the matching of twist‑3 parton distribution functions. The work demonstrates that zero modes can generate singular terms that dominate low‑energy observables, underscoring their physical impact.

6. **Analytical zero modes in a modified Toda model.** Ref. [6] obtains exact analytical zero‑mode solutions for a fermion chirally coupled to a scalar field, showing that topological configurations give rise to protected bound states (including in‑gap and BIC states). The existence of such analytically tractable zero modes provides a benchmark for topological protection.

7. **Majorana zero modes in iron‑based superconductors.** Ref. [7] enumerates three defining properties of Majorana zero modes: (i) self‑conjugation (particle equals hole), (ii) association with a non‑trivial topological invariant, and (iii) exponential splitting that scales as $e^{-d/\xi}$ with separation $d$. These criteria constitute the standard test for Majorana protection.

8. **ZBW‑Majorana hypothesis as a topological observable.** Ref. [8] proposes that the Zitterbewegung (ZBW) signal constitutes a $\mathbb Z_{2}$ topological observable that vanishes for Majorana fermions while remaining non‑zero for Dirac fermions. The hypothesis links a zero‑mode‑like signature to a topological invariant, providing a broader perspective on how zero‑mode structures can encode protection.

Collectively, these works establish that (i) constrained zero modes can alter spectral properties, (ii) zero modes may appear in symmetry algebras, (iii) topological protection is tied to non‑trivial indices and exponential splitting, and (iv) zero‑mode signatures can serve as topological markers. Our paper tests whether the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode shares these hallmarks.

## 3. Methods
Our analysis proceeds in three stages:

1. **Symmetry mapping.** We write the Hawking–Page WDW operator (Eq. 1 of Ref. [2]) and the Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian for a $p$‑wave superconductor,
   $$\mathcal H_{\rm BdG}=\begin{pmatrix}
   -\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}-\mu & \Delta(\mathbf r)\\
   \Delta^{*}(\mathbf r) & \frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}+\mu
   \end{pmatrix},$$
   then identify the $U(1,1)$ generators $K_{0},K_{\pm}$ of Ref. [2] with formal particle–hole operators and examine whether they satisfy the BdG particle–hole algebra $\mathcal C\mathcal H_{\rm BdG}\mathcal C^{-1}=-\mathcal H_{\rm BdG}$.

2. **Index calculation.** We treat the WDW operator as a self‑adjoint operator on a suitable Fredholm domain and compute its Fredholm index, comparing with the chiral index $n_{R}-n_{L}=Q\neq0$ that underlies Majorana protection in lattice‑QCD and condensed‑matter systems (the index property is exemplified by the zero‑mode flow in Refs. [3,4]).

3. **Spectral isolation.** We analyse the asymptotic behaviour of the WDW solutions to determine whether $E=0$ is an isolated eigenvalue. As a benchmark we compute the exponential splitting $\Delta E\sim e^{-L/\xi}$ for a domain‑wall configuration, using the same functional form that appears in the Majorana protection criterion of Ref. [7].

All calculations are analytic; no numerical simulation or empirical measurement is performed. Assumptions are listed explicitly in Section 4.

## 4. Analysis
### 4.1 Particle‑hole‑like involution from $U(1,1)$
The Hawking–Page WDW operator reads
$$\hat{\mathcal H}\psi(a,\phi)=\frac12\!\left(\frac1a\partial_a a\partial_a-\frac1{a^{2}}\partial_{\phi}^{2}-a^{2}\right)\!\psi(a,\phi)=0\quad\text{[2]}. \tag{1}$$
Ref. [2] shows that the operators
\[
K_{0}= \frac12\left(a\partial_{a}+ \partial_{a}a\right),\qquad
K_{+}= \frac12 a^{2},\qquad
K_{-}= \frac12\partial_{a}^{2},
\]
satisfy the $U(1,1)$ algebra
\[
[K_{0},K_{\pm}]=\pm K_{\pm},\qquad [K_{+},K_{-}]=-2K_{0}.
\]
Acting on the Bessel‑function solutions
\[
\psi_{m}(a,\phi)=J_{\pm i m/2}(i a^{2}/2)\,e^{i m\phi},
\]
the operators $K_{\pm}$ raise or lower the integer label $m$. Defining a formal conjugation $\mathcal C$ that maps $m\to -m$,
\[
\mathcal C\,\psi_{m}= \psi_{-m},
\]
we obtain $\mathcal C K_{+}\mathcal C^{-1}=K_{-}$ and $\mathcal C K_{-}\mathcal C^{-1}=K_{+}$. This mirrors the exchange of creation and annihilation operators under particle–hole symmetry in the BdG Hamiltonian. However, $\mathcal C$ does **not** satisfy $\mathcal C\hat{\mathcal H}\mathcal C^{-1}=-\hat{\mathcal H}$ because $\hat{\mathcal H}$ is identically zero on the solution space; the involution is a symmetry of the *solution basis* rather than a genuine anti‑unitary that flips the sign of the Hamiltonian. Consequently, the $U(1,1)$ symmetry implements a particle‑hole‑like involution but does not provide the BdG particle–hole operator required for a Majorana zero mode.

### 4.2 Fredholm index of the WDW operator
Consider the WDW operator $\hat{\mathcal H}$ acting on the Hilbert space $\mathcal H=L^{2}(\mathbb R^{+}\times S^{1},a\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}\phi)$. On any domain $\mathcal D$ that renders $\hat{\mathcal H}$ self‑adjoint (e.g., smooth functions of compact support), the operator is Fredholm with kernel spanned by the Bessel solutions $\{\psi_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb Z}$. The dimension of the kernel is countably infinite, and the cokernel is empty because $\hat{\mathcal H}$ is surjective onto its range. Therefore the Fredholm index
\[
\operatorname{ind}(\hat{\mathcal H})=\dim\ker\hat{\mathcal H}-\dim\operatorname{coker}\hat{\mathcal H}= \infty-0,
\]
which, after regularisation, is interpreted as **zero** because each positive $m$ mode is paired with a negative $m$ mode under the involution $\mathcal C$. In contrast, the Dirac operators that host Majorana modes possess a non‑zero chiral index $n_{R}-n_{L}=Q\neq0$ (the index property is exemplified by the zero‑mode flow in Refs. [3,4] and by the topological invariant discussed in Ref. [7]). Thus the WDW operator lacks a non‑zero topological index, a key ingredient of Majorana protection.

### 4.3 Spectral isolation and exponential protection
A Majorana zero mode is protected when $E=0$ is an isolated eigenvalue separated from the continuum by a gap that remains open under symmetry‑preserving deformations. For the WDW operator (1), the spectrum is continuous for $a\to\infty$ because the solutions behave as
\[
\psi_{m}(a,\phi)\sim \exp\!\left(\frac{a^{2}}{2}\right)\times(\text{oscillatory factor}),
\]
which diverges exponentially and is non‑normalisable. Hence $E=0$ is not an isolated point; any infinitesimal deformation of the lapse function $N(a,\phi)$ can shift the asymptotic behaviour and produce a continuous band that includes zero energy.

To illustrate the magnitude of protection that would be required, we compute the benchmark exponential splitting for a domain‑wall configuration,
\[
\Delta E \sim e^{-L/\xi}.
\]
Setting $L/\xi=10$ gives
\[
\Delta E = e^{-10}=4.539992976\times10^{-5}\approx4.54\times10^{-5}.
\]
This benchmark reproduces the exponential scaling that defines Majorana protection in Ref. [7].

## 5. Results
1. **Particle–hole‑like involution.** The $U(1,1)$ generators implement a basis involution $\mathcal C$ that exchanges $K_{+}$ and $K_{-}$, but $\mathcal C$ does not satisfy the BdG particle–hole condition $\mathcal C\hat{\mathcal H}\mathcal C^{-1}=-\hat{\mathcal H}$. Hence the symmetry is analogous but not equivalent to Majorana particle–hole symmetry.

2. **Fredholm index.** The WDW operator has index 0, whereas Dirac operators that protect Majorana modes possess a non‑zero chiral index (as exemplified by the zero‑mode flow in Refs. [3,4] and the topological invariant in Ref. [7]). The absence of a topological index removes a cornerstone of Majorana protection.

3. **Spectral isolation.** The zero‑energy point is embedded in a continuous, non‑normalisable spectrum; no gap separates it from excited states. The benchmark exponential splitting $e^{-L/\xi}=4.54\times10^{-5}$ at $L/\xi=10$ illustrates the exponential suppression required for robust protection, which the WDW zero‑mode does not achieve.

Collectively, these results falsify the conjecture that the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode is a Majorana‑type topological zero mode.

## 6. Discussion
### 6.1 Limitations
- **L1.** The symmetry analysis is confined to the Hawking–Page wormhole minisuperspace; other minisuperspace models may possess different hidden symmetries.
- **L2.** The Fredholm index calculation assumes standard self‑adjoint domains; exotic boundary conditions could modify the index but are not explored here.
- **L3.** The spectral isolation argument relies on the asymptotic behaviour of Bessel solutions; alternative factorisations of the constraint could, in principle, produce isolated modes.
- **L4.** The exponential‑splitting benchmark is a reference calculation; it does not represent a physical gap in the WDW spectrum.

### 6.2 Failure modes
If a deformation of the lapse function $N(a,\phi)\to N(a,\phi)+\delta N$ preserves the global constraint (1) yet yields a non‑zero eigenvalue for the former zero‑mode, the particle–hole analogy would be further invalidated. Conversely, discovering a factorisation of the WDW operator into off‑diagonal Dirac‑type components with a non‑zero index would overturn our index argument.

### 6.3 Falsifiability
A direct falsification would occur if a numerical diagonalisation of the deformed WDW operator (with $\delta N\neq0$) shows that the eigenvalue formerly at $E=0$ remains exactly zero for all symmetry‑preserving deformations. Observation of any shift would confirm the failure of the Majorana analogy.

### 6.4 Open questions
- Can a different minisuperspace truncation admit a genuine particle–hole antiunitary that squares to $+1$ or $-1$?
- Is there a formulation of quantum gravity where the constraint operator naturally decomposes into a chiral Dirac pair with a non‑zero index?
- What physical observable, if any, could encode a topological invariant derived from the WDW kernel?

## 7. Practical Implications and Engineering Pathways
**(a) Potential users and design impact.** The result is primarily foundational: it informs quantum‑gravity theorists and topological‑quantum‑computing researchers that the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode does **not** provide an intrinsic decoherence‑free subspace. Consequently, engineering efforts should not allocate resources to hardware designs that rely on this specific gravitational mode for protection.

**(b) Technology readiness.** The work is purely analytical and has not undergone peer review; according to NASA TRL definitions it resides at TRL 1–2.

**(c) Smallest next experiment.** A concrete test would be a numerical diagonalisation of the Hawking–Page WDW operator with a controlled deformation of the lapse function $N\to N+\delta N$. The measurement would be the eigenvalue of the would‑be zero‑mode; any non‑zero result would falsify the Majorana analogy.

**(d) Engineering constraints.** The primary constraint is the need for a discretised minisuperspace representation that faithfully preserves the $U(1,1)$ algebra. No additional material or fabrication constraints arise because the proposal concerns a theoretical test rather than a physical device.

**(e) Projected performance bounds.** No performance bounds are claimed because the WDW zero‑mode lacks the exponential protection that underlies fault‑tolerance in Majorana platforms (Ref. [7]).

**(f) Risks and misuse.** Misinterpreting the WDW zero‑mode as a protected qubit could lead to wasted experimental effort and the deployment of quantum‑hardware designs that lack genuine error‑suppression mechanisms, potentially exposing systems to uncontrolled decoherence channels.

## 8. Conclusion
We have subjected the conjecture that the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode constitutes a Majorana‑type topological zero mode to a rigorous structural test. The hidden $U(1,1)$ symmetry yields a particle‑hole‑like involution but fails to satisfy the Bogoliubov–de Gennes particle–hole condition. The WDW operator possesses Fredholm index 0, in stark contrast to the non‑zero chiral index that underpins protection in Dirac‑type Majorana systems. Spectral analysis shows that $E=0$ is not isolated, and the benchmark exponential splitting $e^{-L/\xi}=4.54\times10^{-5}$ illustrates the magnitude of protection that is absent. Consequently, the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode does **not** inherit the defining hallmarks of a Majorana zero mode, and proposals to exploit it as a decoherence‑free resource lack a solid theoretical foundation.
