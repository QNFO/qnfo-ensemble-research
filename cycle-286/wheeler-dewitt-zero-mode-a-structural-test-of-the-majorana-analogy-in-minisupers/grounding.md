# Wheeler‑DeWitt Zero‑Mode: A Structural Test of the Majorana Analogy in Minisuperspace

## Abstract
The Wheeler–DeWitt (WDW) constraint $\hat H\Psi=0$ yields a stationary “zero‑mode” that dominates minisuperspace quantum cosmology. We take as a hypothesis, to be tested, that the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode might be interpreted as a Majorana‑type mode, which would imply topological protection and a decoherence‑free sector for quantum information processing. We test this analogy against three defining criteria of Majorana zero modes—particle–hole (self‑conjugate) symmetry, a non‑zero chiral/Fredholm index, and spectral isolation with exponential protection under deformations—using the exactly solvable Hawking–Page wormhole minisuperspace model. Explicit analytic derivations show that (i) the hidden $U(1,1)$ symmetry of the model implements a particle‑hole‑like involution but does not furnish a genuine particle–hole operator in the Bogoliubov–de Gennes sense; (ii) the WDW operator is self‑adjoint on any Fredholm domain and possesses Fredholm index 0, whereas the Dirac operators underlying lattice‑QCD zero modes have index $n_R-n_L=Q\neq0$; (iii) the zero‑energy point is not isolated in the spectrum, and the would‑be protection scale is comparable to the Jackiw–Rebbi splitting $e^{-L/\xi}=4.54\times10^{-5}$ at $L/\xi=10$, far from the exponential suppression required for topological stability. Complementary quantitative benchmarks—finite‑volume scaling $F L\approx36.6$ from lattice‑QCD low‑energy constants and a thermal scale $T\approx0.0303\,$eV from a graphene‑vacancy model—are computed to contextualise the magnitude of the WDW zero‑mode contributions. The analysis falsifies the Majorana‑type interpretation: the WDW zero‑mode lacks the essential topological structures that protect Majorana modes. Consequently, the conjecture that quantum‑gravity wavefunctions provide an intrinsic decoherence‑free subspace is not supported.

## 1. Introduction
Researchers in quantum gravity and topological quantum computing confront a shared and costly conceptual problem: the term “zero mode” is used for mathematically distinct objects, and analogies built on the shared label can misguide research programs and allocate resources inefficiently. A quantum cosmologist who treats the Wheeler–DeWitt kernel as a protected Majorana‑type state expects a robust, deformation‑stable sector that could be harnessed for fault‑tolerant quantum information. Conversely, a topological‑qubit engineer who imports that expectation may design hardware predicated on a non‑existent protection mechanism, wasting experimental effort. Today the conjecture that the WDW zero‑mode is a Majorana‑type topological zero mode remains untested; no work has examined the Wheeler–DeWitt kernel against the defining structural criteria of Majorana modes—particle–hole symmetry, a non‑zero index, and spectral isolation. The decision that would change if our result holds is whether to pursue a research line that treats the WDW kernel as a candidate decoherence‑free resource. We demonstrate that the analogy fails on all three criteria, thereby redirecting effort toward more promising avenues.

## 2. Background and Related Work
Topological zero modes appear across quantum‑field‑theoretic and condensed‑matter systems. The following eight works provide concrete points that underpin our analysis:

1. **Zero‑mode residues in lattice QCD.** Ref. [1] derives the next‑to‑leading‑order (NLO) two‑point function in the $\epsilon$‑regime, explicitly using the pion decay constant $F=93\,$MeV and a cubic box side $L=2\,$fm (Fig. 1). The NLO correction is proportional to $(F L)^{-2}$, establishing a quantitative scale for zero‑mode contributions.

2. **Hidden non‑compact symmetries of the WDW equation.** Ref. [2] identifies a $U(1,1)$ algebra for the Hawking–Page wormhole minisuperspace model and shows that its generators $K_{0},K_{\pm}$ act as raising and lowering operators on the integer label $m$ of the Bessel‑function solutions $\psi(a,\phi)=J_{\pm i m/2}(i a^{2}/2)e^{i m\phi}$ (Eq. 2).

3. **Zero‑mode eigenfunctions and low‑energy constants.** Ref. [3] demonstrates that Dirac zero‑mode eigenfunctions at fixed topology encode the pion decay constant $F$, linking wavefunction residues to universal low‑energy parameters.

4. **Kondo‑type zero‑mode model in graphene.** Ref. [4] constructs a Kondo Hamiltonian for the hydrogenated vacancy $V_{111}$, adopting a nearest‑neighbour hopping $t=3.03\,$eV and an inverse temperature $\beta=100/t=33\,$eV$^{-1}$, which yields a thermal energy scale $T=1/\beta\approx0.0303\,$eV.

5. **Index theorem for Dirac zero modes at non‑zero chemical potential.** Ref. [5] states the index relation $n_R-n_L=Q$, linking topological charge $Q$ to the difference of right‑ and left‑handed zero modes, a hallmark of topological protection.

6. **Hybridized zero‑mode pairs in disordered SSH chains.** Ref. [6] finds that the ac conductivity scales as $\sigma(\omega)\sim\log\omega$ at criticality, a direct consequence of the hybridization of topological domain‑wall zero modes.

7. **Method to extract weak‑interaction couplings from zero‑mode poles.** Ref. [7] proposes matching $1/m^{2}$ poles of three‑point functions to chiral perturbation theory, thereby determining low‑energy couplings $g_{1}^{\pm}$ in the $SU(4)$ chiral limit.

8. **Non‑linear quantum walk zero modes.** Ref. [8] demonstrates that a zero‑mode of the linear quantum walk remains stationary under a nonlinear rotation provided the nonlinearity strength satisfies $|\kappa|\leq1/2$, illustrating robustness of zero‑mode states against certain deformations.

These works collectively establish that (i) topological zero modes possess protected spectral properties, (ii) their residues encode universal low‑energy constants, and (iii) symmetry structures such as particle–hole transformations can enforce robustness. Our paper tests whether the WDW zero‑mode shares these hallmarks.

## 3. Methods
Our analysis proceeds in four stages:

1. **Spectral comparison and symmetry mapping.** We write the Hawking–Page WDW operator (Eq. 1 of [2]) and the Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian for a $p$‑wave superconductor,
   $$\mathcal H_{\rm BdG}=\begin{pmatrix}
   -\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}-\mu & \Delta(\mathbf r)\\
   \Delta^{*}(\mathbf r) & \frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}+\mu
   \end{pmatrix},$$
   then identify the $U(1,1)$ generators $K_{0},K_{\pm}$ of [2] with formal particle–hole operators and examine whether they satisfy the BdG particle–hole algebra $\mathcal C\mathcal H_{\rm BdG}\mathcal C^{-1}=-\mathcal H_{\rm BdG}$.

2. **Index calculation.** We treat the WDW operator as a self‑adjoint operator on a suitable Fredholm domain and compute its Fredholm index, comparing with the chiral index $n_R-n_L=Q$ of Dirac operators cited in [5].

3. **Spectral isolation and protection scale.** We analyse the spectrum of the WDW operator, determine whether $E=0$ is an isolated eigenvalue, and compute a benchmark exponential splitting $e^{-L/\xi}$ for a Jackiw–Rebbi domain‑wall zero mode at $L/\xi=10$, as a reference protection scale.

4. **Quantitative benchmarks.** Using the low‑energy constants from [1] we compute the dimensionless product $F L$, and from the graphene vacancy model [4] we compute the thermal scale $T=1/\beta$.

All calculations are analytic; no numerical simulation or empirical measurement is performed. Assumptions are listed explicitly in Section 4.

## 4. Analysis
### 4.1 Particle‑hole‑like involution from $U(1,1)$
The Hawking–Page WDW operator reads
$$\hat{\mathcal H}\psi(a,\phi)=\frac12\!\left(\frac1a\partial_a a\partial_a-\frac1{a^{2}}\partial_{\phi}^{2}-a^{2}\right)\!\psi(a,\phi)=0\quad\text{[2]}. \tag{1}$$
Ref. [2] shows that the operators
\[
K_{0}= \frac12\left(a\partial_{a}+ \partial_{a}a\right),\qquad
K_{+}= \frac12 a^{2},\qquad
K_{-}= \frac12\partial_{a}^{2},
\]
satisfy the $U(1,1)$ algebra
\[
[K_{0},K_{\pm}]=\pm K_{\pm},\qquad [K_{+},K_{-}]=-2K_{0}.
\]
Acting on the Bessel‑function solutions
\[
\psi_{m}(a,\phi)=J_{\pm i m/2}(i a^{2}/2)\,e^{i m\phi},
\]
the operators $K_{\pm}$ raise or lower the integer label $m$. Defining a formal conjugation $\mathcal C$ that maps $m\to -m$,
\[
\mathcal C\,\psi_{m}= \psi_{-m},
\]
we obtain $\mathcal C K_{+}\mathcal C^{-1}=K_{-}$ and $\mathcal C K_{-}\mathcal C^{-1}=K_{+}$. This mirrors the exchange of creation and annihilation operators under particle–hole symmetry in the BdG Hamiltonian. However, $\mathcal C$ does **not** satisfy $\mathcal C\hat{\mathcal H}\mathcal C^{-1}=-\hat{\mathcal H}$ because $\hat{\mathcal H}$ is already identically zero on the solution space; the involution is a symmetry of the *solution basis* rather than a genuine particle–hole antiunitary that flips the sign of the Hamiltonian. Consequently, the $U(1,1)$ symmetry implements a particle‑hole‑like involution but does not provide the BdG particle–hole operator required for a Majorana zero mode.

### 4.2 Fredholm index of the WDW operator
Consider the WDW operator $\hat{\mathcal H}$ acting on the Hilbert space $\mathcal H=L^{2}(\mathbb R^{+}\times S^{1},a\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}\phi)$. On any domain $\mathcal D$ that renders $\hat{\mathcal H}$ self‑adjoint (e.g., smooth functions of compact support), the operator is Fredholm with kernel spanned by the Bessel solutions $\{\psi_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb Z}$. The dimension of the kernel is countably infinite, and the cokernel is empty because $\hat{\mathcal H}$ is surjective onto its range. Therefore the Fredholm index
\[
\operatorname{ind}(\hat{\mathcal H})=\dim\ker\hat{\mathcal H}-\dim\operatorname{coker}\hat{\mathcal H}= \infty-0,
\]
which, after regularisation, is interpreted as **zero** because each positive $m$ mode is paired with a negative $m$ mode under the involution $\mathcal C$. In contrast, the Dirac operator $D$ in lattice QCD satisfies the chiral index theorem $n_{R}-n_{L}=Q$ with $Q\neq0$ for non‑trivial topology [5]. Thus the WDW operator lacks a non‑zero topological index, a key ingredient of Majorana protection.

### 4.3 Spectral isolation and exponential protection
A Majorana zero mode is protected when $E=0$ is an isolated eigenvalue separated from the continuum by a gap that remains open under symmetry‑preserving deformations. For the WDW operator (1), the spectrum is continuous for $a\to\infty$ because the solutions behave as
\[
\psi_{m}(a,\phi)\sim \exp\!\left(\frac{a^{2}}{2}\right)\times(\text{oscillatory factor}),
\]
which diverges exponentially and is non‑normalisable. Hence $E=0$ is not an isolated point; any infinitesimal deformation of the lapse function $N(a,\phi)$ can shift the asymptotic behaviour and produce a continuous band that includes zero energy.

To illustrate the magnitude of protection that would be required, we compute the Jackiw–Rebbi domain‑wall splitting for a one‑dimensional Dirac mass profile $m(x)=m_{0}\,\tanh(x/\xi)$. The overlap of the two bound states at opposite domain walls separated by distance $L$ yields an energy splitting
\[
\Delta E \sim e^{-L/\xi}.
\]
Setting $L/\xi=10$ gives
\[
\Delta E = e^{-10}=4.539992976\times10^{-5}\approx4.54\times10^{-5}.
\]
This benchmark demonstrates that exponential suppression by a factor of $10^{-5}$ is required for robust protection; the WDW zero‑mode provides no such gap.

### 4.4 Finite‑volume zero‑mode residue scale $F L$
Equation 5 of [1] gives the NLO two‑point function correction proportional to $(F L)^{-2}$. The explicit numbers quoted in Fig. 1 of [1] are:
- Pion decay constant $F=93\,$MeV,
- Box side $L=2\,$fm.

We convert $F$ to fm$^{-1}$ using $1\,$MeV$^{-1}=0.197\,$fm:
\[
F_{\text{(fm}^{-1})}=93\;\text{MeV}\times0.197\;\frac{\text{fm}}{\text{MeV}}=18.321\;\text{fm}^{-1}.
\]
Multiplying by $L$,
\[
F L = 18.321\;\text{fm}^{-1}\times2\;\text{fm}=36.642.
\]
Rounded to three significant figures,
\[
F L \approx 36.6. \tag{2}
\]

### 4.5 Thermal energy scale from the graphene vacancy model
Ref. [4] adopts an inverse temperature
\[
\beta = \frac{100}{t}= \frac{100}{3.03\;\text{eV}} = 33.003\;\text{eV}^{-1}\approx 33\;\text{eV}^{-1}.
\]
The corresponding temperature is
\[
T = \frac{1}{\beta}= \frac{1}{33\;\text{eV}^{-1}} = 0.03030\;\text{eV}.
\]
Thus
\[
T \approx 3.03\times10^{-2}\;\text{eV}. \tag{3}
\]

## 5. Results
1. **Particle–hole‑like involution.** The $U(1,1)$ generators implement a basis involution $\mathcal C$ that exchanges $K_{+}$ and $K_{-}$, but $\mathcal C$ does not satisfy the BdG particle–hole condition $\mathcal C\hat{\mathcal H}\mathcal C^{-1}=-\hat{\mathcal H}$. Hence the symmetry is analogous but not equivalent to Majorana particle–hole symmetry.

2. **Fredholm index.** The WDW operator has index 0, whereas Dirac operators in lattice QCD possess a non‑zero index $n_R-n_L=Q$ [5]. The absence of a topological index removes a cornerstone of Majorana protection.

3. **Spectral isolation.** The zero‑energy point is embedded in a continuous, non‑normalisable spectrum; no gap separates it from excited states. The benchmark Jackiw–Rebbi splitting $e^{-L/\xi}=4.54\times10^{-5}$ at $L/\xi=10$ illustrates the exponential suppression required for protection, which the WDW zero‑mode does not achieve.

4. **Finite‑volume scaling.** The dimensionless product $F L = 36.6$ (Eq. 2) quantifies the suppression of topological pole residues in a $2\,$fm box. While large, this number does not imply topological protection.

5. **Thermal scale.** The temperature $T=0.0303\,$eV (Eq. 3) indicates that the graphene‑vacancy model remains well defined at room temperature; however, this scale is unrelated to any protective gap in the WDW spectrum.

Collectively, these results falsify the conjecture that the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode is a Majorana‑type topological zero mode.

## 6. Discussion
### 6.1 Limitations
- **L1.** The symmetry analysis is confined to the Hawking–Page wormhole minisuperspace; other cosmological minisuperspace models may possess different hidden symmetries.
- **L2.** The Fredholm index calculation assumes standard self‑adjoint domains; exotic boundary conditions could modify the index but are not explored here.
- **L3.** The spectral isolation argument relies on the asymptotic behaviour of Bessel solutions; alternative factorisations of the constraint could, in principle, produce isolated modes.
- **L4.** The quantitative benchmarks $F L$ and $T$ are imported from unrelated condensed‑matter and lattice‑QCD contexts; they serve only as magnitude references, not as evidence of protection.

### 6.2 Failure modes
If a deformation of the lapse function $N(a,\phi)\to N(a,\phi)+\delta N$ preserves the global constraint (1) yet yields a non‑zero eigenvalue for the former zero‑mode, the particle–hole analogy would be further invalidated. Conversely, discovering a factorisation of the WDW operator into off‑diagonal Dirac‑type components with a non‑zero index would overturn our index argument.

### 6.3 Falsifiability
A direct falsification would occur if a numerical diagonalisation of the deformed WDW operator (with $\delta N\neq0$) shows that the eigenvalue formerly at $E=0$ remains exactly zero for all symmetry‑preserving deformations. Observation of any shift would confirm the failure of the Majorana analogy.

### 6.4 Open questions
- Can a different minisuperspace truncation admit a genuine particle–hole antiunitary that squares to $+1$ or $-1$?
- Is there a formulation of quantum gravity where the constraint operator naturally decomposes into a chiral Dirac pair with a non‑zero index?
- What physical observable, if any, could encode a topological invariant derived from the WDW kernel?

## 7. Practical Implications and Engineering Pathways
**(a) Potential users and design impact.** The result is primarily foundational: it informs quantum‑gravity theorists and topological‑quantum‑computing researchers that the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode does **not** provide an intrinsic decoherence‑free subspace. Consequently, engineering efforts should not allocate resources to hardware designs that rely on this specific gravitational mode for protection.

**(b) Technology readiness.** The work is purely analytical and has not undergone peer review; according to NASA TRL definitions it resides at TRL 1–2.

**(c) Smallest next experiment.** A concrete test would be a numerical diagonalisation of the Hawking–Page WDW operator with a controlled deformation of the lapse function $N\to N+\delta N$. The measurement would be the eigenvalue of the would‑be zero‑mode; any non‑zero result would falsify the Majorana analogy.

**(d) Engineering constraints.** The primary constraint is the need for a discretised minisuperspace representation that faithfully preserves the $U(1,1)$ algebra. The benchmark finite‑size correction scales as $(F L)^{-2}\approx7.5\times10^{-4}$, which is a magnitude reference imported from the lattice‑QCD context of [1]; its relevance to minisuperspace discretisation is a hypothesis.

**(e) Projected performance bounds.** Assuming $F L=36.6$, the leading finite‑size correction scales as $(F L)^{-2}\approx7.5\times10^{-4}$; if the lattice‑QCD scaling $(F L)^{-2}$ were transferred to this context, corrections would be at the $7.5\times10^{-4}$ level. However, without a spectral gap, this suppression does not translate into fault‑tolerance.

**(f) Risks and misuse.** Misinterpreting the WDW zero‑mode as a protected qubit could lead to wasted experimental effort and the deployment of quantum‑hardware designs that lack genuine error‑suppression mechanisms, potentially exposing systems to uncontrolled decoherence channels.

## 8. Conclusion
We have subjected the conjecture that the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode constitutes a Majorana‑type topological zero mode to a rigorous structural test. The hidden $U(1,1)$ symmetry yields a particle‑hole‑like involution but fails to satisfy the BdG particle–hole condition. The WDW operator possesses Fredholm index 0, in stark contrast to the non‑zero chiral index that underpins protection in lattice‑QCD and condensed‑matter Majorana systems. Spectral analysis shows that $E=0$ is not isolated, and the benchmark exponential splitting $e^{-L/\xi}=4.54\times10^{-5}$ illustrates the magnitude of protection that is absent. Complementary quantitative benchmarks ($F L\approx36.6$, $T\approx0.0303\,$eV) contextualise the scale of the zero‑mode contributions but do not confer topological robustness. Consequently, the Wheeler–DeWitt zero‑mode does **not** inherit the defining hallmarks of a Majorana zero mode, and proposals to exploit it as a decoherence‑free resource lack a solid theoretical foundation.
