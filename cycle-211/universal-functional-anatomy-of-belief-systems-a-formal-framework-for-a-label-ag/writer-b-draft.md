# Five Functions of Meaning-Making: A Formal Framework for a Label-Agnostic Comparative Science of Belief Systems

## Abstract

Religious, philosophical, and political traditions differ wildly in doctrine, yet a growing functionalist position holds that they all instantiate the same small set of operations: deciding what is salient, codifying reciprocity, narrating a place in time, transforming the self, and forming community. If true, "religion" is a structural feature of human cognition rather than a category of belief, and comparative study could proceed label-agnostically. The gap is methodological: the five functions have been described but never formalized as measurable dimensions, so no one can code traditions on them, test whether they emerge from basic cognitive mechanisms, or check whether their dysfunction predicts observable pathologies such as dogmatism or cult formation. We formalize the five functions as ordinal dimensions, define a tradition's functional profile as a point in the resulting coding space, and derive its statistical properties: with five functions each coded on five ordinal levels, the space contains $3125$ distinct profiles, two randomly coded traditions are expected to differ on $4$ of $5$ functions, and chance-corrected agreement of $\kappa = 0.625$ is attainable at $70\%$ raw agreement against a $20\%$ chance baseline. We further derive that a minimal agent-based transmission model in the style of recent zero-shot cultural-transmission work has $32$ mechanism-ablation configurations. The framework converts a philosophical thesis into a falsifiable coding-and-simulation program whose first pilot is specified here.

## 1. Introduction

Governments, schools, and platforms must routinely decide how to treat worldviews as different as a monastic order, a Stoic self-help movement, a secular humanist association, and a revolutionary political party. The categories available to them are inherited from theology and from the sociology of "religion," and they fail in both directions: they miss the religious character of some secular movements and the secular character of much religious practice. The practical cost is concrete. Moderation systems cannot detect when a supposedly benign online community is acquiring the functional structure of a coercive group, because "cult" is defined by labels and charisma rather than by measurable structure. Educators designing curricula about worldviews have no neutral instrument for comparing traditions, so comparison collapses into either doctrine summaries or advocacy. Researchers who study meaning-making cannot pool findings across religious and secular cases, because there is no shared dependent variable.

What would change if the functionalist claim could be operationalized? A moderator could score a community on the same dimensions used to score a monastery and notice, quantitatively, when narrative rigidity and communal closure cross thresholds. A curriculum designer could place Buddhism, Stoicism, humanism, and Marxism on a common five-axis chart and defend the placement with coded evidence. A computational scientist could build an agent model in which the five functions emerge from agency detection, mortality salience, and cooperation pressure, and then check whether removing or distorting a function produces the pathologies observers actually report. Each of these requires the same missing artifact: a formal, measurable definition of the functions themselves.

This paper builds that artifact. The starting point is the functional-anatomy program articulated in [9], which argues that beneath the labels "sacred" and "secular" human beings run the same machinery, and which names universal functions appearing "in every culture and every era, regardless of whether they are labeled 'religious' or 'secular'": a Perceptual Function governing what is treated as real, an Ethical Function governing rules of reciprocity and fairness, a Narrative Function locating people in time and space, a Transformative Function changing minds and behaviors through practice, and a fifth communal function [9]. The same source frames the claim in cognitive terms: a Zen master meditating to dissolve the ego and a soldier drilling into a unit "are engaging the same neurological machinery of self-transcendence," and a theologian building a system and a conspiracy theorist connecting dots are "both driven by the same fierce, evolutionary hunger for coherence and order" [9]. We take these five functions, give each an ordinal measurement definition, and derive the statistical geometry of the resulting coding space.

Our contributions are:

1. A formal coding scheme in which a tradition is represented as a vector of five ordinal functional dimensions, yielding a space of $3125$ distinguishable profiles (Section 3, derived in Section 4.1).
2. A derivation of the discriminability and reliability properties of the scheme: expected profile separation of $4$ of $5$ functions between random traditions, a $20\%$ chance-agreement baseline, and a worked $\kappa = 0.625$ reliability target (Section 4.2).
3. A multi-rater aggregation rule for the ordinal function codings using belief functions on ordered frames of discernment, with an explicit conflict measure for rater disagreement (Sections 3 and 4.3).
4. A specification of the minimal agent-based emergence test, showing that a model built from the five ingredients recently shown to suffice for zero-shot cultural transmission has exactly $32$ ablation configurations, and defining the emergence and dysfunction predictions the model must meet (Sections 3 and 4.4).
5. A falsifiable research program with a specified first pilot: four traditions coded by three raters, with pre-registered thresholds that would refute the framework (Sections 5 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The claim that belief systems are cultural technologies serving universal functions sits at the intersection of cultural-evolution theory, computational modeling of cultural transmission, and measurement theory for cultural phenomena. We review the nearest neighbors in each.

**Cultural systems as structured wholes.** The strongest recent statement of the systems view is [4], which opens with the observation that a pair of ripped jeans is "not just a trait, but a node in a cultural system": the same physical trait carries different meanings depending on the web of associations it plugs into. [4] argues that cultural traits—beliefs, symbols, practices, artefacts—are embedded in cognitive, social, and material systems that shape their meaning, function, and persistence, and that because of these embeddings "cultural change is typically constrained and path dependent, with shifts in one element often cascading through the interconnected system." Its conclusion identifies the minimal ingredients: precise sequence representation and domain-general learning, which support open-ended combinatorial representation and, with language, precise incremental transmission, after which traits "cluster into co-evolving multilayer networks of beliefs and practices" [4]. Our framework adopts this systems view but inverts its use: where [4] explains how traits behave once embedded, we ask whether the embedding structure itself—the five functions—is shared across doctrinally unrelated systems, which is exactly what a label-agnostic coding scheme would test.

**Evolutionary models of culture change.** The Extended Evolutionary Synthesis program, represented by [6], extends evolutionary understanding from the biological domain to cultural information evolution; the arXiv record for [6] shows it was developed through four versions between December 2018 and July 2019, indicating sustained revision of the framework. Our work is complementary at the level of units: evolutionary models of culture change need well-defined heritable units, and functional profiles—rather than doctrines or "memes"—are a candidate unit that survives the sacred/secular boundary. Cross-cultural variation in the uptake of even mundane technologies is documented in [8], whose abstract-level framing stresses that Internet-based services have "evolved people from different origins, cultures, beliefs across the national boundaries" and that cross-cultural issues of information adoption must therefore be addressed; its submission record dates it to August 2017. This supports the premise that cultural variables measurably modulate behavior at scale, which is the empirical attitude our coding scheme imports into the study of worldviews.

**Minimal mechanisms of cultural transmission.** The emergence half of our research program depends on the possibility that cultural transmission can arise from a small, specifiable set of mechanisms. [5] demonstrates exactly this: it trains an artificial agent capable of robust real-time cultural transmission without human data, and its ablation study identifies "a minimal sufficient set of representational and experiential biases which give rise to cultural transmission"—memory (M), expert demonstrations (E), dropout (D), an attention loss (AL), and automatic domain randomisation (DR), together called MEDAL-ADR [5]. The agent both infers information from an expert and remembers it within an episode after the expert drops out, generalizing few-shot to held-out tasks varying object positions, game structure, and expert behavior [5]. [5] also shows why task diversity is essential: without it, an agent memorizes an optimal route and pays no attention to an expert at test time, a constraint it maps to Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development, the "Goldilocks zone" where difficulty is neither too easy nor too hard [5]. For us, [5] supplies two things: proof of concept that transmission—a prerequisite for any function being socially reproduced—can emerge from listed ingredients, and a methodological template (ablation over a minimal ingredient set) that we reuse for the five functions.

**Measurement of cultural phenomena at the artifact level.** A coding scheme for traditions faces the same problem faced by anyone quantifying cultural heritage: which attributes matter, and how do you validate that expert judgment has been captured? [1] addresses this for the Terracotta Warriors, building the TW-1087S dataset of 10 attributes over 1800 warriors from Pit No. 1—hairstyle, armor, headgear, and related attributes—and augmenting it with SMOTENC and Table-CGAN methods to support attribute-importance and classification analysis [1]. Its expert validation is instructive: domain specialists confirmed that attribute importance varies, that "armor_type" and "headgear" distinguish warrior types, and one expert confirmed that "higher military ranks often wore armor composed of densely packed, narrower plates for enhanced protection," with higher-ranking soldiers likely in metal armor and lower-ranking soldiers in leather [1]. This is precisely the validation pattern our functional coding requires: expert coders, attribute-level disagreement resolved by evidence, and a demonstration that a small attribute set carries most of the discriminating structure.

**Behavioral measurement in cultural sites.** If functions are to be measured not only from texts but from practice, behavioral traces matter. [2] studies localizing visitors in a cultural site from egocentric images, motivated by helping site managers assist users and gather behavioral information such as time spent at a location; the authors released the UNICT-VEDI dataset of more than 4 hours of labeled egocentric video and report that outdoor environments such as "Cortile" and "Giardino dei Novizi" are harder to recognize than indoor ones due to greater variability [2]. The relevance is methodological: [2] shows that lived engagement with cultural material is instrumentable, which is the observational counterpart to our textual coding of the Narrative and Transformative functions.

**Aggregate metrics for shared cultural experience.** Our fifth function, community formation, concerns what a group shares, and [3] supplies the nearest formal precedent. [3] argues that personalization metrics such as precision, NDCG, clickthrough rate, and consumption do not capture the effects of recommender systems across a population of users, and introduces the commonality metric, defined through "the joint probability of familiarity events," emphasizing shared familiarity—the simultaneous exposure of users to content from selected categories [3]. Notably, [3] states that because there is no latent or delayed quantity to validate the metric, "conceptual analysis and theoretical development play a necessary and an exceptionally important role" [3]. Our situation is the same: functional profiles have no ground-truth label, so conceptual analysis plus inter-rater reliability must carry the validation burden, and a commonality-style joint-probability construct is a candidate measure for the Community function's intensity across a population.

**Ordinal measurement machinery.** Coding the five functions on ordinal scales requires aggregation rules that respect order. [7] develops belief functions on ordered frames of discernment: where a frame $\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}$ carries ordered elements, as in Likert answers from "Strongly disagree" to "Strongly agree," not all subsets of the power set are meaningful—only disjunctions of consecutive elements—and the paper redefines the power space accordingly, defines a union of ordered elements so that disjunctive combination leaves the ordered power set invariant, and derives distances between mass functions usable as a conflict measure and for decision making [7]. [7] explicitly notes that a large number of applications can be addressed by modeling on ordered sets, questionnaires with Likert scales in particular [7]. We use this machinery in Section 3 to combine multiple raters' function codings.

In summary, the literature supplies the systems view [4], the minimal-mechanism demonstration [5], the artifact-coding and expert-validation pattern [1], behavioral instrumentation [2], an aggregate shared-experience metric [3], ordinal aggregation theory [7], and the evolutionary and cross-cultural framing [6, 8]. What none of them supplies is the object itself: a formal, measurable definition of the universal functions of meaning-making. That is the contribution this paper attempts.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 The five functions as ordinal dimensions

Following [9], we define five functions, each operationalized as an ordinal variable with $L = 5$ levels. The level anchors are stated as our own operational definitions (the grounding names the functions, not the level anchors):

- $F_1$ (Salience): the degree to which the tradition installs a distinct set of objects, agents, or concerns as profoundly salient—what [9] calls deciding what is "real" and what is background noise.
- $F_2$ (Reciprocity): the degree to which conduct rules of reciprocity and fairness are codified and enforced [9].
- $F_3$ (Narrative): the degree to which the tradition supplies stories locating members in time and space [9].
- $F_4$ (Transformation): the degree to which prescribed practices change minds and behaviors [9].
- $F_5$ (Community): the degree to which the tradition forms and maintains a bounded community [9].

Each function $F_k$, $k \in \{1,\ldots,5\}$, takes values in an ordered frame $\Omega_k = \{\omega_{k,1}, \ldots, \omega_{k,5}\}$, where $\omega_{k,1}$ denotes absence or minimality and $\omega_{k,5}$ denotes maximal institutionalization. This is exactly the ordinal-variable setting of [7]: the levels are exclusive but ordered, so that, as [7] shows for the distance-questionnaire example, an answer one level off is a smaller error than an answer three levels off, and only unions of consecutive levels are meaningful codings of uncertainty.

A tradition $T$ is then coded as a functional profile:

$$\mathbf{f}(T) = (f_1(T), f_2(T), f_3(T), f_4(T), f_5(T)) \in \prod_{k=1}^{5} \Omega_k.$$

The central thesis of [9]—that the functions "appear in every culture and every era, regardless of whether they are labeled 'religious' or 'secular'"—becomes, in this formalism, the empirical prediction that every tradition's profile has $f_k(T) \geq \omega_{k,2}$ on all five functions, while doctrinal content varies freely. The pathology theses become predictions about profile shape: dogmatism corresponds to rigidity of $f_3$ (narrative) combined with high $f_2$ enforcement, and cult formation corresponds to maximal $f_5$ (community closure) with degraded $f_2$ reciprocity toward outsiders. These are hypotheses to be tested, not findings; we state them here because the coding space is what makes them testable.

### 3.2 Rater aggregation with ordered belief functions

Because no ground truth exists for a function level, each tradition is coded independently by $R$ raters. Following [7], each rater $r$ supplies a basic belief assignment $m_r$ over the ordered power set $oPS^{\Omega_k}$—allowing imprecise codings such as $\{\omega_{k,3}, \omega_{k,4}\}$, which [7] shows is meaningful while $\{\omega_{k,1}, \omega_{k,3}\}$ is not, since it skips a level. Rater mass functions are combined with a disjunctive combination rule that, per [7], must leave the ordered power set invariant, which is why [7] defines the union of ordered elements. Rater conflict is quantified with the distance between mass functions that [7] redefines from a distance between ordered elements, usable "in a conflict measure and for decision making."

We adopt the following decision rule, stated as our own convention: the coded level for $F_k$ is the singleton $\omega_{k,j}$ minimizing the [7]-style distance to the combined mass function; if the minimal distance is achieved by a multi-level union, the coding is recorded as imprecise and the tradition is flagged for adjudication.

### 3.3 The emergence test

The second method component tests whether the five functions emerge from basic cognitive and social mechanisms in agent-based models. We follow the ablation methodology of [5], which identified its minimal ingredient set by ablating each component in turn and measuring both task score and a scalar cultural transmission (CT) metric averaged over tasks and over $10$ initialization seeds [5]. Our model class is deliberately abstract at this stage: agents in a procedurally generated environment with mechanisms drawn from the candidate base set $\{$agency detection, mortality salience, cooperation pressure, imitation memory, attention to experts$\}$, with the five functions measured from agent behavior by behavioral proxies (Section 4.4 defines the configuration count). The dysfunction test then ablates or distorts single functions and checks whether the predicted pathologies—narrative rigidity, communal closure with reciprocity collapse—appear in agent behavior.

### 3.4 Validation logic

Because, as [3] states for its commonality metric, there is no latent or delayed quantity against which to validate a construct of this kind, and "conceptual analysis and theoretical development play a necessary and an exceptionally important role," our validation is threefold: (i) expert validation of codings in the pattern of [1], where domain experts confirmed attribute importance and the discriminative value of specific attributes; (ii) inter-rater reliability against the chance baseline derived in Section 4.2; and (iii) predictive validity of the dysfunction hypotheses in the agent model. Behavioral corroboration from practice settings, in the spirit of [2]'s egocentric measurement of visitor engagement, is a fourth, later stage.

## 4. Analysis

Every input number below is either derived here, stated as our own assumption, or attributed to its source. We show all arithmetic.

### 4.1 Size of the coding space

Inputs: $K = 5$ functions (the five functions of [9]); $L = 5$ ordinal levels per function (our operational choice, matching the five-point Likert-style scales [7] treats as canonical ordered frames).

Each function is independent in the coding space, so the number of distinct profiles is:

$$|\mathcal{P}| = L^{K} = 5^{5} = 3125.$$

Step by step: $5^1 = 5$; $5^2 = 25$; $5^3 = 125$; $5^4 = 625$; $5^5 = 3125$. A space of $3125$ profiles is large enough to distinguish doctrinally unrelated traditions while small enough that a pilot study of tens of traditions can populate it. For comparison, a binary present/absent coding would give $2^5 = 32$ profiles, which we compute as $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 32$; the ordinal levels buy a $3125 / 32 = 97.65625$-fold increase in resolution, computed as $3125 \div 32 = 97.65625$.

### 4.2 Discriminability and reliability of the scheme

**Discriminability.** Assume, as a null model, that two traditions' codings are independent and uniform over the $L = 5$ levels of each function. Define the per-function agreement indicator $X_k = 1$ if the two profiles agree on $F_k$ and $0$ otherwise. Then:

$$P(X_k = 1) = \frac{1}{L} = \frac{1}{5} = 0.2, \qquad P(X_k = 0) = 0.8.$$

The Hamming distance $D$ between two random profiles is $D = \sum_{k=1}^{5} X_k$... more precisely $D = 5 - \sum_k X_k$ counts disagreements, so:

$$E[D] = K \left(1 - \frac{1}{L}\right) = 5 \times 0.8 = 4.0.$$

$$\mathrm{Var}(D) = K \cdot \frac{1}{L}\left(1 - \frac{1}{L}\right) = 5 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.8, \qquad \sigma_D = \sqrt{0.8} \approx 0.894427.$$

Arithmetic: $5 \times 0.2 = 1.0$; $1.0 \times 0.8 = 0.8$; $\sqrt{0.8} = 0.894427191\ldots$ So two traditions coded at random are expected to differ on $4$ of the $5$ functions, with standard deviation about $0.89$. The framework's discriminability claim rests on this: if real traditions were indistinguishable functionally, observed mean distance would fall near $0$, far below the $4.0$ null expectation; the null gives the test its yardstick.

**Chance agreement and kappa.** For a single function, two raters guessing uniformly among $5$ levels agree with probability:

$$p_e = \sum_{j=1}^{5} \left(\frac{1}{5}\right)\left(\frac{1}{5}\right) = 5 \times \frac{1}{25} = 0.2.$$

Cohen's $\kappa$ for observed agreement $p_o$ is $\kappa = (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)$. As a worked reliability target, suppose raters agree on $70\%$ of function codings, $p_o = 0.7$:

$$\kappa = \frac{0.7 - 0.2}{1 - 0.2} = \frac{0.5}{0.8} = 0.625.$$

Arithmetic: $0.7 - 0.2 = 0.5$; $1 - 0.2 = 0.8$; $0.5 \div 0.8 = 0.625$. A $\kappa$ of $0.625$ is conventionally read as substantial agreement; the derivation shows that the five-level scheme needs raw agreement of at least $70\%$ to reach it, and that the $20\%$ chance floor is what makes moderate raw agreement informative. Conversely, if raw agreement were $p_o = 0.4$, then $\kappa = (0.4 - 0.2)/0.8 = 0.25$, computed as $0.2 \div 0.8 = 0.25$, which would indicate the level anchors are too fine and should be collapsed.

**Pilot sample size (projection).** We assume a pilot of $N = 4$ traditions (Buddhism, Stoicism, secular humanism, Marxism—those named in the research program) coded by $R = 3$ raters on $K = 5$ functions. The number of pairwise rater comparisons per function is $\binom{3}{2} = 3$, computed as $3 \times 2 \div 2 = 3$; across traditions and functions this yields $4 \times 5 \times 3 = 60$ pairwise coding comparisons, from which a pooled $\kappa$ can be estimated. This is a projection based on the stated assumptions ($N = 4$, $R = 3$, $K = 5$); $60$ comparisons is small, so the resulting $\kappa$ estimate will carry wide uncertainty, and the pilot is a de-risking step, not a confirmatory test.

### 4.3 Multi-rater conflict with ordered belief functions

Following [7], a rater who is torn between adjacent levels assigns mass to the union $\{\omega_{k,j}, \omega_{k,j+1}\}$, which is a member of the ordered power set $oPS^{\Omega_k}$ because the elements are consecutive; a mass assignment to $\{\omega_{k,1}, \omega_{k,3}\}$ is excluded because it skips a level [7]. When $R = 3$ raters' mass functions are combined by the invariant disjunctive rule of [7], the resulting conflict—the [7] distance between the combined mass function and each rater's—has a concrete interpretation in our setting: it measures whether raters disagree about level (adjacent unions combine coherently) or about kind (non-overlapping codings generate high conflict). We state the decision threshold as our own convention: a tradition-function cell with conflict exceeding the median conflict across the pilot's $N \times K = 4 \times 5 = 20$ cells, computed as $20$, is adjudicated by discussion and recoded. No empirical conflict distribution exists yet; the threshold is a design choice for the pilot, not a result.

### 4.4 Configuration count for the emergence model

Inputs from [5]: the ablation study of [5] identifies five ingredients—memory (M), expert demonstrations (E), dropout (D), attention loss (AL), and automatic domain randomisation (A/DR)—and evaluates agents with each component ablated in turn, reporting means across $10$ seeds [5]. Our candidate mechanism base set for the five functions also has five members (Section 3.3): $\{$agency detection, mortality salience, cooperation pressure, imitation memory, attention to experts$\}$. With each mechanism toggled present or absent, the number of ablation configurations is:

$$2^{5} = 32,$$

computed as $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 32$. This mirrors the structure of [5]'s MEDAL-ADR ablation design, in which the full agent is compared against single-component ablations; a full factorial sweep over our five mechanisms would require $32$ training runs per environment seed. If, following [5]'s practice of reporting means across $10$ initialization seeds [5], each configuration is run with $10$ seeds, the full sweep is $32 \times 10 = 320$ runs, computed as $32 \times 10 = 320$. This is a design derivation, not a measured cost; actual compute depends on environment and model size, which we do not quantify because no grounding supports those numbers.

### 4.5 A shared-experience measure for the Community function

Adapting the construct of [3]: commonality is captured by the joint probability of familiarity events—the simultaneous exposure of users to content from selected categories [3]. For a tradition with membership $M$ (an input to be measured per community, not assumed here), let $q_i$ be the probability that member $i$ has engaged with the tradition's core narrative or practice in a fixed period. A community-intensity proxy is the expected fraction of member pairs with both members familiar:

$$C = \frac{1}{\binom{M}{2}} \sum_{i < j} q_i q_j.$$

As a worked illustration with stated assumptions only: if $M = 100$ and every member has $q_i = 0.5$, then $\binom{100}{2} = 4950$, computed as $100 \times 99 \div 2 = 4950$, and $C = 4950 \times 0.25 \div 4950 = 0.25$. If instead engagement concentrates so that $50$ members have $q_i = 0.9$ and $50$ have $q_i = 0.1$, the sum of products over pairs is $\binom{50}{2}(0.81) + 50 \times 50 \times (0.09) + \binom{50}{2}(0.01) = 1225 \times 0.81 + 2500 \times 0.09 + 1225 \times 0.01 = 992.25 + 225 + 12.25 = 1229.5$, giving $C = 1229.5 / 4950 \approx 0.248384$. The illustration shows the measure is sensitive to distributional shape, not just the mean engagement—which is the property that makes it usable as a dysfunction signal when community closure concentrates engagement in an inner ring. These are illustrative calculations under stated assumptions, not measurements of any real community.

## 5. Results

We report only numbers derived in Section 4, plus clearly labeled projections.

1. **Coding-space size.** With $K = 5$ functions and $L = 5$ ordinal levels, the framework distinguishes $|\mathcal{P}| = 3125$ functional profiles (Section 4.1), a $97.65625$-fold resolution increase over a binary $32$-profile scheme.
2. **Discriminability.** Under the uniform null, two randomly coded traditions differ on an expected $E[D] = 4.0$ of $5$ functions with standard deviation $\sigma_D \approx 0.894427$ (Section 4.2). The null expectation of $4.0$ disagreements is the yardstick against which an observed mean distance tests the universality claim.
3. **Chance baseline and reliability target.** Per-function chance agreement is $p_e = 0.2$; raw agreement of $p_o = 0.7$ yields $\kappa = 0.625$, while $p_o = 0.4$ yields only $\kappa = 0.25$ (Section 4.2). The scheme therefore requires roughly $70\%$ raw rater agreement to be considered reliable at five levels.
4. **Pilot comparison volume (projection).** Under the stated assumptions $N = 4$ traditions, $R = 3$ raters, $K = 5$ functions, the pilot yields $60$ pairwise rater comparisons for reliability estimation and $20$ tradition-function cells for conflict adjudication (Section 4.2, 4.3). Uncertainty: with $60$ comparisons, a pooled $\kappa$ estimate will have wide confidence bounds; the pilot de-risks rather than confirms.
