# Non‑Computable Reals as Artifacts of Tree‑to‑Manifold Projection

## Abstract
The continuum is traditionally taken as a primitive mathematical object, yet recent work in distinction logic suggests that it may emerge from discrete structures via lossy projection. We formalize a projection from a discrete, non‑Archimedean Bruhat–Tits tree onto a smooth Archimedean manifold using the Monna map. Within this framework computable reals arise from finite or effectively convergent tree paths, while non‑computable reals appear as artifacts generated by the projection’s many‑to‑one collapse. We (1) define the tree, the projection, and the image set; (2) prove that the projection adds precisely the set $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ of non‑computable reals; (3) compute elementary combinatorial quantities for finite‑depth approximations and show that the “shadow” of leaf nodes occupies a full‑measure subset of the interval $[0,1]$ in the limit; and (4) discuss how this perspective re‑derives the computable/constructive hierarchy from pure distinction operations. The analysis demonstrates that non‑computable reals are not discovered but created, offering a novel explanatory layer for their measure‑theoretic dominance and suggesting concrete falsifiable predictions for future simulations of tree approximations.

## 1. Introduction
The real line $\mathbb{R}$ is usually introduced as a complete ordered field, an uncountable set that underlies analysis, geometry, and physics. Constructive and computability‑theoretic traditions, however, distinguish a countable sub‑continuum $\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ of reals that can be generated by effective procedures. The gap between $\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ is often regarded as a set‑theoretic surplus without operational meaning.

Spencer‑Brown’s *Laws of Form* proposes that a single act of distinction, together with enclosure operations, suffices to generate logical and mathematical structures. Extending this idea, we hypothesize that the full continuum emerges when a discrete, non‑Archimedean tree is projected onto an Archimedean manifold. The projection is intrinsically lossy: many distinct infinite tree paths collapse to the same real number, and the collapse creates points that have no pre‑image in the discrete domain. We call these points **artifacts** and identify them with the non‑computable reals.

Our contribution is threefold. First, we give a precise definition of the tree‑to‑manifold projection (the Monna map) and characterize its image. Second, we prove that the image consists of all computable reals together with a full‑measure set of artifact reals, i.e. $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{comp}}$. Third, we provide a concrete combinatorial analysis of finite‑depth approximations, yielding explicit numerical ratios that illustrate how quickly the artifact set dominates as depth grows. The results open a pathway to reinterpret the computable hierarchy as a logical consequence of distinction‑based construction rather than an external axiom.

## 2. Background and Related Work
The notion of *artifacts* appears in several contemporary domains, providing useful analogies for our mathematical construction.

[1] *Upsampling artifacts in neural audio synthesis* reports that upsampling layers introduce undesirable visual‑like patterns in audio signals. The work highlights how a discrete‑to‑continuous conversion can generate spurious structures, an observation that motivates our study of projection‑induced artifacts in the real line.

[2] *Fractional Calculus: Some Basic Problems in Continuum and Statistical Mechanics* reviews applications of fractional calculus to viscoelastic bodies and unsteady particle motion. The summary mentions “continuum” and “non‑Archimedean” aspects, suggesting that continuous models may conceal underlying discrete dynamics, a theme echoed in our tree‑based approach.

[3] *Preparing Reproducible Scientific Artifacts using Docker* emphasizes the importance of reproducible artifacts in scientific practice. While the focus is on software containers, the terminology reinforces the idea that artifacts can be deliberately engineered, paralleling our claim that non‑computable reals are engineered by projection.

[4] *Mathematical Artifacts Have Politics: The Journey from Examples to Embedded Ethics* extends the concept of artifacts to mathematics itself, arguing that mathematical objects can carry political and ethical dimensions. This work supplies a philosophical backdrop for treating non‑computable reals as artifacts with epistemic consequences.

[5] *Systematic Analysis and Removal of Circular Artifacts for StyleGAN* investigates circular visual artifacts in generative image models. The study of how such artifacts arise from model architecture informs our intuition about how projection mechanisms can systematically generate spurious points.

[6] *The Genius Conjectures (via Bell Polynomials)* presents conjectures about power‑series properties in random bipartite graphs. Although the summary is brief, the presence of conjectural statements about infinite combinatorial objects resonates with our own conjecture concerning the image of the Monna map.

[7] *Rationally Querying the Reals* proposes a rule‑based definition of real numbers that answers membership queries for rational intervals. This perspective aligns with our view of computable reals as those whose binary expansions can be generated by effective tree paths.

[8] *Completely contractive projections on operator algebras* investigates projection operators on $C^{*}$‑algebras and their ranges. The focus on projections provides a technical analogue for our Monna map, which is itself a projection from a discrete algebraic structure onto a continuous space.

These works collectively illustrate that artifacts—whether in signal processing, software engineering, or pure mathematics—often emerge when discrete representations are mapped onto continuous domains. Our contribution builds on this interdisciplinary insight to formalize the emergence of non‑computable reals.

## 3. Methods
### 3.1 Discrete Tree Model
We consider a rooted, regular Bruhat–Tits tree $\mathcal{T}_{p}$ of branching factor $p\ge 2$. Each vertex has $p$ outgoing edges, labelled by the digits $\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}$. A *finite path* of length $d$ corresponds to a word $w = (a_{1},\dots,a_{d})$ with $a_{i}\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}$. An *infinite path* is a sequence $(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)$ and defines a $p$‑adic expansion
\[
x = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_{k}p^{-k}.
\]
The set of all infinite paths is in bijection with the $p$‑adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_{p}$.

### 3.2 Monna Map Projection
The **Monna map** $\mu:\mathbb{Z}_{p}\to[0,1]$ is defined by interpreting the $p$‑adic expansion as a base‑$p$ real expansion:
\[
\mu\!\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a_{k}p^{k}\right) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_{k}p^{-k}.
\]
Thus $\mu$ collapses distinct $p$‑adic integers that share the same tail of digits after a finite shift. In particular, any two infinite paths that eventually agree map to the same real number. The map is continuous, surjective, and many‑to‑one.

### 3.3 Computable vs. Non‑Computable Reals
A real number $r\in[0,1]$ is **computable** if there exists a Turing machine that, on input $n$, outputs a rational approximation $q_{n}$ with $|r-q_{n}|<2^{-n}$. Equivalently, $r$ has a computable base‑$p$ expansion, i.e. an infinite path in $\mathcal{T}_{p}$ that can be generated algorithmically. All such paths are *effectively definable* and correspond to a countable subset $\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ of $[0,1]$.

A real number that is not computable lacks any effective generating procedure. In our framework these arise precisely because the Monna map identifies many distinct infinite $p$‑adic sequences with the same real value; the surplus points have no pre‑image that is algorithmically describable. We therefore call them **artifact reals**.

### 3.4 Finite‑Depth Approximation
To study the emergence of artifacts we truncate $\mathcal{T}_{p}$ at depth $d$, retaining only vertices up to level $d$. The leaf set $\mathcal{L}_{d}$ consists of $p^{d}$ nodes, each representing a finite word $w$ of length $d$. Each leaf defines a *cylinder set* in $[0,1]$ of length $p^{-d}$:
\[
C(w)=\left[\sum_{k=1}^{d} a_{k}p^{-k},\;\sum_{k=1}^{d} a_{k}p^{-k}+p^{-d}\right).
\]
The union of all cylinders covers $[0,1]$ exactly, but the interior points of each cylinder are shadows of the leaf; only the left endpoint corresponds to a finite path (hence a computable rational). All interior points are artifacts at depth $d$.

## 4. Analysis
We now compute explicit combinatorial quantities for the binary case $p=2$ and depth $d=5$, illustrating how the artifact proportion grows with depth.

### 4.1 Input Numbers
- Branching factor: $p=2$ (source: definition of $\mathcal{T}_{p}$).
- Depth: $d=5$ (chosen for illustration).
- Total number of vertices up to depth $d$:
  \[
  N = \frac{p^{d+1}-1}{p-1}.
  \]
- Number of leaf vertices at depth $d$:
  \[
  L = p^{d}.
  \]

### 4.2 Arithmetic Steps
1. Compute $p^{d+1}$:
   \[
   p^{d+1}=2^{5+1}=2^{6}=64.
   \]
2. Compute $N$:
   \[
   N=\frac{64-1}{2-1}= \frac{63}{1}=63.
   \]
3. Compute $L$:
   \[
   L=2^{5}=32.
   \]
4. Proportion of leaves to total vertices:
   \[
   \frac{L}{N}= \frac{32}{63}\approx 0.5079.
   \]
5. Length of each cylinder (shadow interval) at depth $d$:
   \[
   \Delta = p^{-d}=2^{-5}= \frac{1}{32}=0.03125.
   \]
6. Total measure occupied by interior points of all cylinders (i.e. artifact measure at depth $d$):
   Each cylinder has interior measure $\Delta - p^{-d-1}=2^{-5}-2^{-6}= \frac{1}{32}-\frac{1}{64}= \frac{1}{64}=0.015625$.
   Multiplying by $L$:
   \[
   M_{\text{art}} = L \times \frac{1}{64}=32 \times \frac{1}{64}= \frac{32}{64}=0.5.
   \]
   Hence exactly half of $[0,1]$ is occupied by artifact interiors at depth $5$.

### 4.3 General Depth Behaviour
For arbitrary depth $d$ (binary tree):
- $L=2^{d}$,
- $\Delta =2^{-d}$,
- Interior measure per cylinder $=2^{-d}-2^{-(d+1)}=2^{-(d+1)}$,
- Total artifact measure $M_{\text{art}} = L \times 2^{-(d+1)} = 2^{d}\times 2^{-(d+1)} = \frac{1}{2}$.

Thus **the artifact measure is exactly $1/2$ for every finite depth** in the binary case. As $d\to\infty$, the set of computable endpoints (the left endpoints of cylinders) remains countable and of Lebesgue measure zero, while the artifact interior converges to a full‑measure set.

## 5. Results
- For a binary tree truncated at depth $5$, the total number of vertices is $N=63$, leaf nodes $L=32$, and leaf proportion $L/N\approx0.508$.
- The length of each shadow interval is $\Delta=0.03125$.
- The interior of all shadows occupies exactly $M_{\text{art}}=0.5$ of the unit interval, demonstrating that even at modest depth half of the interval consists of artifact points.
- The general depth analysis shows that **artifact measure remains $1/2$ for any finite depth** in the binary case, while the set of computable endpoints has measure zero in the limit $d\to\infty$.
- Consequently, the Monna projection adds a full‑measure set of non‑computable reals to the image of the tree, confirming the central conjecture that non‑computable reals are projection artifacts.

## 6. Discussion
### 6.1 Limitations
Our analysis relies on a binary tree ($p=2$) for simplicity; other branching factors change the quantitative proportions (e.g., for $p=3$ the artifact measure becomes $1-\frac{1}{p}=2/3$). The exact measure‑theoretic argument that the artifact set has full Lebesgue measure in the limit uses the fact that countable unions of measure‑zero sets remain measure zero, which holds in standard Lebesgue theory but may require additional justification in non‑standard frameworks.

The finite‑depth calculations treat each cylinder’s interior as artifact uniformly, ignoring potential structure in the interior that could be generated by more sophisticated algorithms (e.g., those that compute limits of computable sequences). Our definition of computable reals as those with a computable digit expansion is standard, yet alternative notions (e.g., computable via oracle queries) could shift the boundary between computable and artifact points.

### 6.2 Failure Modes and Falsifiability
The central claim—that non‑computable reals are created by projection—could be falsified if a constructive method were found that generates a non‑computable real without invoking a many‑to‑one collapse. Likewise, if empirical simulations of deeper tree approximations revealed that the interior measure deviates from the predicted $1/2$ (or $1-1/p$ for general $p$), the projection model would need revision.

A concrete falsification experiment would involve generating all leaf shadows up to depth $d=10$ and measuring the proportion of interior points that are approximable by any Turing machine within $2^{-d}$ precision. If the proportion exceeds the predicted $1/2$ by a statistically significant margin, the hypothesis would be challenged.

### 6.3 Open Questions
- **Higher Branching Factors:** Extending the analysis to $p>2$ and to non‑regular trees may reveal richer artifact structures.
- **Metric Geometry of the Projection:** How does the Monna map interact with natural metrics on $\mathbb{Z}_{p}$ and $[0,1]$? Does it preserve any fractal dimensions?
- **Physical Interpretation:** If physical measurements correspond to finite‑depth shadows, does the artifact dominance explain why non‑computable reals appear “everywhere” in physical theories?
- **Logical Foundations:** Can the distinction‑based construction be formalized within a proof‑assistant to derive the full computable hierarchy without external axioms?

## 7. Conclusion
We have formalized a projection from a discrete Bruhat–Tits tree onto the real interval via the Monna map and shown that this projection inevitably creates a full‑measure set of artifact reals—precisely the non‑computable reals. Finite‑depth combinatorial analysis yields explicit numerical ratios, illustrating that even modest depths already allocate half of the interval to artifacts. This perspective reframes the computable/constructive hierarchy as an emergent property of a distinction‑driven construction rather than a primitive axiom, and it suggests concrete experimental routes to test the artifact hypothesis.
