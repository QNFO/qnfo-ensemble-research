# Quantized Ringdown Echoes from Discrete Clockwork Spacetime: A Comb Signature in Black Hole Ringdowns and Its Detectability

## Abstract

If spacetime is generated by discrete computational steps on a topological substrate rather than a continuous manifold, the gravitational-wave ringdown of a black-hole merger should carry a periodic fine structure: a comb of spectral lines with spacing set by the round-trip time of radiation between the photon sphere and a reflecting substrate boundary near the would-be horizon. No existing analysis derives the spacing of such a comb from a specific discrete-substrate dynamics and checks it against the ringdown linewidth. We construct this derivation by identifying the substrate boundary's compactness parameter with the exponential hierarchy generated by a clockwork chain of $N+1$ coupled sites with link coupling $\epsilon$, so that the echo spacing is $T_{\rm echo} = \left[2(3-2\ln 2) + 4N\ln(1/\epsilon)\right]GM_f/c^3$. For a fiducial remnant of $M_f = 60\,M_\odot$ (assumed), $\epsilon = 0.3$ and $N = 10$, we obtain $T_{\rm echo} = 15.19$ ms, a comb spacing $\Delta f = 65.85$ Hz, and up to 32 resolvable echoes in a 0.5 s post-merger window; the spacing exceeds the quadrupolar quasinormal-mode linewidth of 48.42 Hz, so the comb is spectrally resolvable. A non-detection in existing interferometer data would exclude $N\ln(1/\epsilon) \in [5.96,\,20.34]$ for such remnants, a range containing the fiducial value. The result converts a metaphysical claim about discrete spacetime into a bounded, falsifiable search.

## 1. Introduction

Gravitational-wave observatories face a specific and currently unresolvable question: does the remnant of a binary black-hole merger have a surface, or an event horizon? The distinction matters because a horizonless remnant with a reflective boundary near the horizon radius traps radiation temporarily and returns it as delayed "echoes," whereas a true horizon absorbs everything after the prompt quasinormal-mode ringdown. The LIGO and Virgo interferometers have detected binary black-hole coalescences, which opened a new window to probe gravity physics in the strong-field regime [5], and the ringdown regime can be understood analytically in terms of quasinormal modes consisting of exponentially damped oscillations with a discrete spectrum of complex frequencies [6]. Today, however, analysts searching for horizon-scale structure must assume a phenomenological echo template — typically equal-interval pulses — whose spacing and amplitude are free parameters not derived from any underlying theory. That assumption is itself known to be fragile: if the post-merger object is a wormhole that slowly pinches off before collapsing, the intervals between neighboring echoes increase with time, and the usual equal-interval assumption is not always applicable [5]. Without a derived spacing, a null search constrains little, and a marginal detection cannot be checked for internal consistency.

The cost of this gap is concrete. Every published or future echo search on LIGO, Virgo, and KAGRA data, and on the next generation of detectors, converts its null result into a bound on an uninterpreted parameter (the reflector's distance from the horizon) rather than on parameters of a physical model. If a specific discrete-substrate model predicted both the spacing and the amplitude pattern of the echo train, a null search would instead exclude a region of that model's parameter space, and a detection would come with a falsifiable internal consistency check — the spacing, the linewidth resolution, and the amplitude decay must all follow from the same two numbers. That is the decision this paper bears on: how to template, and what to conclude from, ringdown searches for structure at the horizon scale.
