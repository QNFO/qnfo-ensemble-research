# Compression Versus Consilience in Recursive Model-Generated Corpora: A Break-Even Analysis

## Abstract

Training a machine-learning model on data generated by another model is naturally modeled as lossy compression of high-dimensional real-world structure: each recursive generation should discard tail events and shrink effective dimensionality, making inference shallower. Against this compression conjecture stands the observation that very large synthetic corpora can exhibit novel cross-disciplinary associations (consilience) that human-scale efforts would not assemble. We formalize the tension with a recursive compression-channel model in which tail entropy and effective dimensionality decay geometrically with retention factor $\rho = 1-\varepsilon$, while consilience is modeled as the creation of novel cross-domain links from surviving bridge vocabulary, anchored by an audit figure that $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline. Our central result is a generation-independent break-even condition: because compression loss and consilience gain both scale as $(1-\varepsilon)^{g}$, compensation holds at every generation if and only if a dimensionless coupling constant $\kappa$ exceeds $\kappa^{\ast} = \varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)/(\beta V_0 p_{\mathrm{bridge}}) \approx 64.29$ under our stated normalization ($\varepsilon = 0.15$, $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) = 12$ bits, $\beta V_0 = 1$). We derive the one-bit usefulness threshold at generation $g = 16$, show that re-anchoring every $5$ generations sustains $8.90$ bits average tail entropy (a $67.1\%$ uplift), and state falsifiable predictions. No simulations or empirical measurements were performed; all numbers are derived from stated assumptions.

## 1. Introduction

A growing fraction of the text, code, and structured data used to train machine-learning systems is itself produced by machine-learning systems. When a model is fine-tuned on the output of another model, and that output-model was in turn trained on model output, the resulting corpus is the product of a recursive, model-on-model generative chain. Engineering teams that adopt such chains do so because fresh human-curated data is expensive and slow to produce, while synthetic generation is cheap and fast. The open engineering question they face is whether a recursive synthetic-data pipeline can be run for many generations without quietly destroying the rare-but-valuable structure that makes the resulting model useful beyond its training distribution — and if not, how often real data must be re-injected to prevent that destruction. Today this decision is made by intuition and anecdote; there is no quantitative rule that tells a pipeline designer when degradation will outpace any compensating benefit.

A natural worry is that a recursive chain is a lossy compression process: each generation discards some of the high-dimensional structure of the real-world data that seeded the chain, so that tail events — rare but informative configurations far from the corpus mode — are progressively flattened, and inference becomes "shallower." Call this the **compression conjecture**. It predicts monotone geometric decay of tail entropy $H_{\mathrm{tail}}$ and effective dimensionality $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ across generations.

Against this stands an **emergent-consilience conjecture**: very large synthetic corpora can exhibit novel cross-disciplinary consilience — unexpected, productive associations between domains that no human-scale research effort would assemble, because no human reads across disciplinary silos at machine scale and speed. The precondition for such associations is shared vocabulary across disciplines, and this precondition is scarce: an audit of cross-domain vocabulary found that $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline. If bridge terms are that rare, synthetic corpora that recombine content at machine scale might surface bridges that human literature never formed — precisely because machines can enumerate combinations humans do not attempt.

The research question is therefore precise: **can emergent cross-domain link structure compensate for per-sample compression, or does compression inevitably dominate?** We answer with a framework rather than an experiment. If the framework's break-even condition holds for a given pipeline, a designer can run the recursion indefinitely without re-anchoring; if it fails, the designer knows that periodic re-anchoring is not optional but structurally required, and can compute how often. The contributions are:

1. **A channel model of recursive training**, in which one generation of model-on-model training is a compression operator retaining a fraction $1-\varepsilon$ of tail-entropy mass, in the spirit of Bayesian data compression [1] (Section 3.1–3.2).
2. **Closed-form decay laws** for tail entropy and effective dimensionality under constant $\varepsilon$, with the one-bit usefulness threshold derived at $g = 16$ (Sections 3.2 and 4).
3. **A consilience metric and a break-even theorem**: the compensation condition is generation-independent and reduces to a single inequality on a dimensionless coupling constant $\kappa$, calibrated by the $97.2\%$ single-discipline audit figure; a complementary pair-counting crossover condition is stated as a labeled secondary model (Sections 3.3–3.5 and 4).
4. **A taxonomy of interventions**: diversity enforcement, formal verification, and periodic re-anchoring each map to a specific term of the break-even inequality, with the re-anchoring uplift computed at $67.1\%$ (Sections 3.6, 4, and 7).

The paper is explicit about what is derived and what is assumed. Every number in Section 4 is computed from stated inputs with shown arithmetic; no simulations or empirical measurements were performed, and Section 5 reports only derived quantities plus clearly labeled projections.

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Bayesian data compression.** The closest methodological antecedent to our compression channel is Bayesian data compression (BDC), which derives an algorithm that adapts to the specific measurement situation in the context of signal reconstruction, compressing a dataset under conservation of its posterior structure with minimal information loss given prior knowledge [1]. The BDC derivation minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the original posterior and the posterior of a virtual, computationally cheaper likelihood, and the resulting criterion leads to a generalized eigenvalue problem [1]. Two features of that work transfer directly to our setting. First, the information-loss measure is a divergence between full and compressed posteriors, which is the single-shot analogue of our per-generation entropy loss $\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}$. Second, BDC reports a concrete compression figure: in an illustrative one-dimensional synthetic linear scenario, 40 data points could be compressed to four data points with less than 20% loss of information [1]. That single-shot loss of under 20% is exactly the order of magnitude we assume for the per-generation loss $\varepsilon = 0.15$ in a well-behaved compression step; the recursive setting compounds what BDC controls once.

**Compression for robust control.** In data-driven robust control, an optimal-transport-based method compresses a large dataset of input/output pairs into a smaller synthetic dataset of representative behaviours, aiming to alleviate computational burden while retaining controller utility [2]. The method uses the Wasserstein metric to compress the informative content of a large dataset into a smaller one, and the authors evaluate the robust controller obtained from the synthetic dataset against the one obtained from the original [2]. This is an instructive precedent for our hybrid-pipeline question: compression there is judged by whether the compressed synthetic dataset preserves *control-relevant* behaviour, i.e., utility is task-conditioned, not distribution-wide. Our tail-entropy metric is the distribution-wide counterpart, and the gap between the two motivates our distinction between mode utility (which survives compression well) and tail generalization (which does not). The same work also notes that allowing variable weights in the discrete measure would make the compression curve monotone, since an additional synthetic point could always be placed with zero mass [2] — a reminder that more synthetic samples do not automatically add information, which is the core intuition behind our decay law.

**Adaptive compression in databases.** Modern in-memory databases must be optimized for small memory footprint and high query speed at the same time, and data compression reduces memory requirements but often reduces query speed too; an adaptive compressor based on generalized deduplication offers a new trade-off point between these dimensions [3]. The evaluation compares GD Segment variants against uncompressed, dictionary-encoded, frame-of-reference-encoded and LZ4-compressed segments, both standalone and integrated into the Hyrise database on the TPC-H and Join Order benchmarks, and concludes that the proposed segments perform comparably to state-of-the-art encoders while avoiding full-segment decompression [3]. The trade-off structure — compression buys storage efficiency at a performance cost, and the operating point must be chosen adaptively — is the same structure we formalize as $\varepsilon$ versus consilience gain, in a completely different substrate. The database case also supplies a design lesson we adopt in Section 7: the trade-off parameter (there, the deviation size in bits; here, the re-anchoring period) should be selected from measurements of the actual workload, not fixed a priori.

**Landmark encoding of time series.** Continuous glucose monitoring generates dense time-series data, and a study of novel encoding strategies reduces CGM profiles to a compact set of landmark points while maintaining fidelity in reconstructed signals and derived glycemic metrics, evaluated on two complementary CGM datasets [4]. This is a concrete instance of tail-selective compression: landmarks are, in our vocabulary, retained tail events, and the stated concern for derived downstream metrics parallels our concern for downstream task generalization. The design pattern — keep the extremes, compress the middle — is precisely what a diversity-enforcing generator would do to protect tail mass in a synthetic corpus.

**Dataset distillation as compression.** A rate–utility perspective on dataset distillation argues that distillation compresses an original dataset into a small set of synthetic samples while preserving its full utility, and criticizes existing methods for either maximizing performance under fixed storage or otherwise treating the rate–utility trade-off incompletely [5]. The work formulates a differentiable objective that incorporates the bitrate of latent codes, class labels, and decoder parameters alongside a task utility loss, and reports state-of-the-art trade-offs on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet subsets, with up to $170\times$ compression relative to vanilla distillation while maintaining or improving test accuracy under tight storage budgets [5]. This is the most direct precedent for our framing: a distilled dataset is a synthetic corpus, and the rate–utility axis is our $(\varepsilon, \text{consilience})$ axis. A companion theoretical study of the mechanisms by which gradient-based learning extracts task-relevant information and encodes it efficiently into synthetic data points states that progress in dataset distillation remains largely empirical, and proves theorems bounding the sufficient size of distilled datasets for retraining to preserve generalization [8]. Its conclusion that the necessary distilled size should scale with task complexity rather than ambient dimension alone [8] supports our decision to supply a theory-first framework rather than another empirical method, and suggests that our $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)$ should be read as a task-complexity quantity, not an ambient-dimension one.

**Compression as structure discovery.** Regionalization methods typically require user input such as the number of regions or a similarity measure; a network regionalization algorithm based on the minimum description length principle instead learns natural clusters that result in a maximally compressed representation, requiring no user input, recovering synthetically planted clusters in noisy spatial data, and partitioning ethnoracial census data in U.S. metropolitan areas [6]. The optimization is a greedy procedure that merges the pair of adjacent clusters whose aggregation yields the largest decrease in description length [6]. This matters for our consilience metric, which requires a notion of "domain" in semantic space: [6] shows that information compression can define such structure intrinsically rather than by user input, suggesting an unsupervised route to the domain partition our metric needs.

**Latent representations under resource constraints.** A major challenge in quantum computing is its application to large real-world datasets due to scarce quantum hardware resources; the $\zeta$-QVAE addresses this by finding low-dimensional representations that preserve essential information for downstream analysis, using regularized mixed-state latent representations in a quantum variational autoencoder, with a latent Hilbert space over $N_Z \leq N_X$ qubits and trash qubits discarded by partial trace [7]. The structural parallel is exact: compression is justified by downstream utility under a resource constraint, which is our setting with "quantum hardware" replaced by "the model's context and capacity."

**Tensor compression.** Tensor canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition formulated as a nonlinear least-squares problem and solved with a modified Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm provides another instance of low-rank, effective-dimensionality-reducing compression, with emphasis on image compression and reconstruction [11]. The work defines compression percentage as the ratio $R(I+J+K)/(I \times J \times K)$ for a third-order tensor of size $I \times J \times K$ and rank $R$, and reports that the modified LM achieved compression with the same order of accuracy as the standard LM at significantly lower CPU times [11]. Our $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ decay is the training-loop analogue of the rank reduction that ratio measures.

**Synthetic data and its risks.** A survey of privacy measurement in tabular synthetic data reports that there is no standard for quantifying the degree of privacy protection that synthetic data afford, codifies common technical assessment frameworks, and discusses proposed quantification approaches to support modeling and evaluation decisions [10]. The survey defines synthetic data as data generated using a purpose-built mathematical model with the aim of solving data science tasks — a definition that covers our recursive corpora — and identifies failure modes such as improperly trained generators that overfit and reproduce fixed patterns rather than inferring stochastically [10]. That failure mode is the mechanism behind our compression conjecture. Although privacy is not our focus, the survey's central diagnosis — synthetic-data evaluation lacks agreed metrics — applies verbatim to consilience: our Section 3 metric is a proposal in a space without standards, and we flag that as a limitation. As a cautionary calibration point from empirical science, constraints on dark-energy parameters derived from 13 H II starburst galaxy apparent magnitude measurements were found to be largely consistent with those from other datasets but not as restrictive as the constraints derived from SNeIa, BAO, and CMB anisotropy data [9]. We use [9] as an example of the general pattern that a single, possibly biased data source yields consistent but weaker constraints — the exact failure mode we predict for deeply recursive synthetic corpora, whose constraints on reality weaken with each generation.

**Consilience measurement and the prior version.** The $97.2\%$ single-discipline vocabulary figure used in Section 4 is an empirical input carried from a prior audit of cross-domain vocabulary; the prior version of this analysis [12] built the same break-even argument on that figure and reported the identical derived quantities — the one-bit threshold at $g = 16$, the break-even coupling $\kappa^{\ast} \approx 64.29$, and the $67.1\%$ re-anchoring uplift [12]. This paper restates and tightens that derivation, adds the sensitivity analysis of Section 4, and develops the engineering pathway of Section 7, which the prior version left implicit. Two further works in the same corpus bear on the conceptual background. The Relational Patternist Synthesis argues that words and coordinate systems are arbitrary transformations, but the structural relationships they describe — formalized as cross-ratios invariant under projective transformations — are not, and that the shared task of physics, economics, and cognitive science is finding such invariant cross-ratios beneath coordinate noise [13]. This supplies a candidate formalization of what a "consilient" cross-domain link preserves: an invariant relation, not a surface co-occurrence. The projective-geometric analysis of semantic structures argues that semantic memory relies on ultrametric topologies, that statistical approximation alone fails to capture deep semantic structure, and that semantic space is an ultrametric projective geometry [14]. If semantic structure is ultrametric, tail events in semantic space are hierarchically nested rather than isotropic, which bears directly on the isotropic-loss assumption our $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ decay law makes; we return to this in Section 6.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 The recursive compression channel

Let $\mathcal{C}_g$ denote the corpus at generation $g$, with $\mathcal{C}_0$ a real-world-seeded corpus. One generation of model-on-model training maps $\mathcal{C}_g \to \mathcal{C}_{g+1}$, where a model trained on $\mathcal{C}_g$ generates $\mathcal{C}_{g+1}$. We model this map as a compression operator $T_{\varepsilon}$ with a single scalar parameter: the per-generation retained tail-entropy fraction $1-\varepsilon$, with $0 < \varepsilon < 1$. The modeling assumption is that $T_{\varepsilon}$ preserves task-relevant structure in the mode of the distribution (as BDC preserves posterior structure [1]) while losing tail mass at rate $\varepsilon$ per generation.

### 3.2 Tail entropy and effective dimensionality

Define the tail entropy $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(\mathcal{C})$ as the Shannon entropy mass carried by samples outside the corpus's high-probability mode region, and the effective dimensionality $d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{C})$ as the participation ratio of the corpus embedding covariance,

$$d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{C}) = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D} \lambda_i\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{D} \lambda_i^{2}},$$

where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of the embedding covariance matrix in ambient dimension $D$. Under the constant-$\varepsilon$ model,

$$H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) = H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)\,(1-\varepsilon)^{g}, \qquad d_{\mathrm{eff}}(g) = d_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)\,(1-\varepsilon)^{g}.$$

Both follow by induction: $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g+1) = (1-\varepsilon) H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g)$ by definition of $T_{\varepsilon}$, and the participation ratio scales identically if compression removes variance mass proportionally across retained directions (the isotropic-loss assumption; we revisit it in Section 6, noting that the ultrametric structure argued for in [14] would make loss non-isotropic).

### 3.3 Consilience metric

Let the corpus be partitioned into domains, with the partition either given by metadata or learned intrinsically by a compression-based clustering criterion in the spirit of the minimum-description-length regionalization of [6]. Define the cross-domain link density

$$\rho_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathcal{C}) = \frac{\#\{\text{links } (a,b) : \mathrm{dom}(a) \neq \mathrm{dom}(b)\}}{\#\{\text{links}\}},$$

where links are citation edges, co-occurrence edges in embedding $k$-nearest-neighbor graphs, or co-membership in verification-checked equivalence classes. The empirical input carried from the prior audit is that $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline, so the baseline probability that a vocabulary item is shared across disciplines is

$$p_{\mathrm{bridge}} = 1 - 0.972 = 0.028.$$

We define the consilience gain of generation $g \to g+1$ as the number of *novel* cross-domain links created, modeled as

$$\Delta C_g = \kappa \, p_{\mathrm{bridge}} \, V_g,$$

where $V_g$ is the surviving technical-vocabulary mass at generation $g$ and $\kappa \geq 0$ is a dimensionless coupling constant measuring how strongly the generative model exploits available bridge vocabulary. We assume $V_g = V_0 (1-\varepsilon)^{g}$: vocabulary decays at the same rate as tail entropy, since rare vocabulary is tail mass.

### 3.4 The break-even condition

Compression loss per generation in entropy units is $\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g)$. Consilience gain, converted to the same units by a proportionality constant $\beta$ (bits of tail-structure value per novel cross-domain link), is $\beta \kappa p_{\mathrm{bridge}} V_0 (1-\varepsilon)^{g}$. Compensation requires

$$\beta \kappa p_{\mathrm{bridge}} V_0 (1-\varepsilon)^{g} \geq \varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) (1-\varepsilon)^{g}.$$

The factor $(1-\varepsilon)^{g}$ cancels: the condition is **generation-independent**. Dividing both sides by $(1-\varepsilon)^{g} \beta V_0$,

$$\kappa \geq \kappa^{\ast} \equiv \frac{\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)}{\beta V_0 p_{\mathrm{bridge}}}.$$

This is the paper's central structural result: recursive compression does not progressively lose the race against consilience — either the coupling constant clears a fixed threshold and compensation holds at every generation, or it does not and compression dominates at every generation. Interventions act on specific quantities: diversity enforcement raises $\kappa$; formal verification raises $\beta$ by making links trustworthy; re-anchoring with real data resets $\varepsilon$ downward and injects fresh $V_0$, $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)$.

### 3.5 Complementary pair-counting model (labeled secondary model)

An alternative, stronger growth model treats a cross-domain link as an independent pair event. If corpus size grows as $N_g = N_0 \beta_{\mathrm{c}}^{g}$ and per-pair link probability decays as $\pi_0 \rho^{\gamma g}$ (with $\rho = 1-\varepsilon$ and tail-concentration exponent $\gamma \geq 0$), the expected link count obeys $E_g \approx E_0 (\beta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\rho^{\gamma})^{g}$, with $E_0 = \binom{N_0}{2}\pi_0$. Consilience then grows iff

$$\beta_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\rho^{\gamma} > 1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \rho > \beta_{\mathrm{c}}^{-2/\gamma}.$$

We report this model separately in Section 5 as a labeled projection under its own declared parameters, because it assumes link independence — an assumption the break-even model does not require.

### 3.6 Hybrid re-anchoring

In a hybrid pipeline, real data re-enters every $m$ generations, resetting $H_{\mathrm{tail}}$ to a fraction $r$ of its generation-0 value ($0 < r \leq 1$; $r=1$ is a full reset). Between anchors the geometric law holds, so the long-run average tail entropy over one cycle is

$$\bar{H} = \frac{H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)\, r}{m} \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} (1-\varepsilon)^{j} = \frac{H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)\, r}{m} \cdot \frac{1 - (1-\varepsilon)^{m}}{\varepsilon}.$$

All symbols: $g$ generation index; $\varepsilon$ per-generation compression fraction; $\rho = 1-\varepsilon$ retention factor; $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)$ initial tail entropy; $d_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)$ initial effective dimensionality; $p_{\mathrm{bridge}}$ bridge-vocabulary proportion; $V_0$ initial vocabulary mass; $\kappa$ consilience coupling; $\beta$ link-value proportionality; $\beta_{\mathrm{c}}$ corpus growth factor (secondary model); $\gamma$ tail-concentration exponent; $m$ re-anchoring period; $r$ reset fraction.

## 4. Analysis

Every input number is stated here with its source; every arithmetic step is shown.

**Input 1 (empirical input, carried from the prior audit and prior version [12]):** $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline. Hence

$$p_{\mathrm{bridge}} = 1 - 0.972 = 0.028.$$

**Input 2 (assumption, labeled):** per-generation compression fraction $\varepsilon = 0.15$, i.e., each recursive generation retains $85\%$ of tail-entropy mass. This is a modeling assumption, not a measurement; its order of magnitude is motivated by the single-shot BDC result that compressing 40 points to 4 points costs less than 20% information loss [1], and Section 6 discusses its status.

**Input 3 (assumption, labeled):** initial tail entropy $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) = 12$ bits, a representative order of magnitude for a corpus whose tail spans several independent rare-event axes.

**Input 4 (assumption, labeled):** $\beta V_0 = 1$ bit-value unit, fixing the normalization of the consilience coupling; $\kappa$ is then measured in units of this normalization.

**Derivation 1 — tail-entropy trajectory.** With $\varepsilon = 0.15$, the retention factor is $1-\varepsilon = 0.85$. Then

$$H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) = 12 \times 0.85^{g} \text{ bits}.$$

Compute powers of $0.85$:

$$0.85^{1} = 0.85,\quad 0.85^{2} = 0.7225,\quad 0.85^{3} = 0.614125,$$
$$0.85^{4} = 0.52200625,\quad 0.85^{5} = 0.4437053125.$$

So $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(5) = 12 \times 0.4437053125 = 5.32446375$ bits. Continuing,

$$0.85^{10} = (0.85^{5})^{2} = 0.4437053125^{2} = 0.19687440434,$$
$$H_{\mathrm{tail}}(10) = 12 \times 0.19687440434 = 2.36249285 \text{ bits}.$$

**Derivation 2 — generations to the one-bit threshold.** We ask when $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) < 1$ bit:

$$12 \times 0.85^{g} < 1 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; 0.85^{g} < \frac{1}{12} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; g > \frac{\ln(12)}{\ln(1/0.85)}.$$

With $\ln(12) = 2.48490665$ and $\ln(1/0.85) = 0.16251893$,

$$g > \frac{2.48490665}{0.16251893} = 15.2906.$$

Since $g$ is an integer, tail entropy drops below one bit at generation $g = 16$. Check: $0.85^{16} = 0.85^{10} \times 0.85^{5} \times 0.85 = 0.19687440434 \times 0.4437053125 \times 0.85 = 0.07427498$; $12 \times 0.07427498 = 0.89129976 < 1$. At $g = 15$: $0.89129976 / 0.85 = 1.04858795 > 1$. Confirmed.

**Derivation 3 — break-even coupling.** From Section 3.4 with $\beta V_0 = 1$:

$$\kappa^{\ast} = \frac{\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)}{\beta V_0 p_{\mathrm{bridge}}} = \frac{0.15 \times 12}{1 \times 0.028} = \frac{1.8}{0.028} = 64.285714\ldots \approx 64.29.$$

The generative process must create about $64$ times more novel cross-domain links per unit of surviving vocabulary than a baseline that samples bridge vocabulary uniformly at rate $p_{\mathrm{bridge}} = 0.028$. Equivalently, the consilience gain per generation at break-even is

$$\Delta C^{\ast} = \kappa^{\ast} p_{\mathrm{bridge}} V_0 = 64.285714 \times 0.028 \times 1 = 1.8 \text{ link-value units},$$

which matches the compression loss $\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) = 0.15 \times 12 = 1.8$, as required by construction.

**Derivation 4 — sensitivity of the threshold to $\varepsilon$.** Since $\kappa^{\ast}$ is linear in $\varepsilon$,

$$\kappa^{\ast}(\varepsilon) = \frac{12\,\varepsilon}{0.028} = 428.571429\,\varepsilon.$$

For a well-regularized pipeline with $\varepsilon = 0.05$: $\kappa^{\ast} = 428.571429 \times 0.05 = 21.43$. For an aggressive pipeline with $\varepsilon = 0.30$: $\kappa^{\ast} = 428.571429 \times 0.30 = 128.57$. Reducing $\varepsilon$ by a factor of $6$ (from $0.30$ to $0.05$) reduces the required coupling by the same factor, since $128.57 / 21.43 = 6.000$.
