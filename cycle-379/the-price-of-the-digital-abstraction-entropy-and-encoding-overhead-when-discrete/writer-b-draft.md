# The Price of the Digital Abstraction: Entropy and Encoding Overhead When Discrete Error Models Meet Continuous Quantum Hardware

## Abstract

Quantum error correction (QEC) theory assumes that noise on each physical device can be represented by a discrete set of Pauli errors acting on two-level systems, but superconducting transmons are anharmonic oscillators whose native dynamics are continuous and better described as qudits of dimension $d\approx12$. This work quantifies the information-theoretic overhead incurred when the continuous error model of such a device is forced into a discrete Pauli framework. Using analytic derivations with fully shown arithmetic, we compute (i) the classical register entropy required to label a digitized bosonic mode, $H_{\mathrm{reg}}=\log_2 12=3.584963$ bits; (ii) the binary-encoding dimension overhead $4/3$ with encoding efficiency $\eta=0.896241$; (iii) the entropy distortion introduced by Pauli twirling of an amplitude-damping channel at $\gamma=10^{-3}$, $\Delta H=1.5\times10^{-3}$ bits (ratio $1.131489$), and we prove by exact algebra that $\Delta H=3\gamma/2$ holds for all $\gamma$, with no higher-order remainder; and (iv) the discarded Hilbert-space weight for a cold mode with $\bar n=0.1$, $\approx3.19\times10^{-13}$. The twirled channel's own entropy scales as $\gamma\log_2(1/\gamma)$ and is dominated by its error-species terms ($88.8\%$ at $\gamma=10^{-3}$), while the excess $\Delta H$ is exactly linear in $\gamma$. These numbers establish a measurable price for the digital abstraction and clarify that most of the cost is bookkeeping rather than physical leakage. The analysis informs hardware designers and QEC architects about trade-offs between radix choice, encoding efficiency, and entropy attribution.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum error correction is deployed on superconducting processors that consist of transmons—anharmonic oscillators with many energy levels—yet QEC theory treats each device as a qubit subject to a discrete Pauli error set $\{I,X,Y,Z\}$. Designers of large-scale quantum processors must therefore map continuous bosonic dynamics onto a discrete logical layer, incurring hidden costs in discarded Hilbert-space weight, extra encoding qubits, and entropy bookkeeping that does not appear in standard fault-tolerance thresholds. If these hidden costs are large, they could increase the physical qubit budget, measurement bandwidth, or energy consumption required to achieve a target logical error rate, thereby affecting the economic viability of quantum computers. If they are small, the digital abstraction is cheap and the engineering consensus in favor of qubit-based QEC is vindicated. Which of these two situations holds is a decision-relevant question, and today it is answered mostly by intuition rather than by calculation.

This paper quantifies a slice of that hidden cost with explicit arithmetic. We treat the transmon as a qudit of dimension $d=12$ and compare the continuous amplitude-damping channel to its Pauli-twirled surrogate, deriving entropy and dimension overheads per mode and per correction cycle. The locale-framework analysis of quantum computing claims treats "a transmon is a qubit" as one of eight widely repeated statements that hold only within a specified domain and fail at a documented boundary [8]; the present paper supplies one quantitative face of that boundary. The prior version of this paper established the basic quantities; this revision strengthens the derivations, most notably by giving a complete exact proof that the twirling excess $\Delta H$ is exactly linear in the damping probability with no remainder term, by correcting the scaling statement for the twirled channel's own entropy, and by recomputing the distortion ratio at a second damping point.

**Contributions.**
1. Register entropy of digitization – derivation of $H_{\mathrm{reg}}(d)=\log_2 d$ for $d=12$, yielding $3.584963$ bits per mode per use, together with the correction that this floor depends on the chosen cutoff and encoding and is therefore not independent of code choice (Section 4.1).
2. Binary encoding overhead – computation of qubit count $m=\lceil\log_2 d\rceil$, encoding efficiency $\eta$, and dimension overhead $4/3$, with the power-of-two counterexample $d=16$ at which the overhead vanishes (Sections 4.2 and 5.2).
3. Entropy distortion under Pauli twirling – exact calculation of environment entropy for amplitude damping and its Pauli surrogate at $\gamma=10^{-3}$, giving $\Delta H=1.5\times10^{-3}$ bits and ratio $1.131489$, together with the closed-form proof that $\Delta H=3\gamma/2$ exactly for all $\gamma$ (Sections 4.3–4.4).
4. Scaling classification – demonstration that the twirled channel's own entropy scales as $\gamma\log_2(1/\gamma)$ and is dominated by its three error-species terms, while only the excess $\Delta H$ is linear in $\gamma$ (Section 4.4).
5. Discarded Hilbert-space weight – analytic expression for thermal leakage with $\bar n=0.1$ and $d=12$, evaluated also at colder and larger-cutoff operating points (Sections 4.5 and 5.4).

## 2. Background and Related Work

Digitization of continuous or heterogeneous source material into discrete representations is a documented engineering pattern with measurable costs, and the present analysis imports that pattern into quantum hardware. The following works supply the concrete observations we build on.

[1] reports that the digital-twin model emerged from the interaction of cyber-physical systems with sensors, industry 4.0, the internet of things, machine learning and artificial intelligence, and that the model has since been used across many areas with significant effect. A digital twin is precisely a discrete digital representation operated against a continuous physical process, which is the same map-territory structure we quantify for qubits.

[2] studies fashion-trend analysis from the humanities perspective and shows that existing digital fashion archives, built mainly to enhance museum collections or to predict short-term commercial trends, are not suitable for analyzing everyday fashion trends; the authors therefore built the CAT STREET archive of street-level fashion images covering 1970–2017. The archive had to be constructed because the available discrete representations did not match the phenomenon, an instance of representation mismatch with an observable remediation cost.

[3] examines XML-based approaches to representing compound digital assets, listing the Metadata Encoding and Transmission Standard (METS), the IMS Content Packaging XML binding, and the XML Formatted Data Units (XFDU) developed by CCSDS Panel 2, and positions the MPEG-21 Digital Item Declaration among them. Choosing one discrete container format for continuous, compound assets is a radix choice in the classical domain, directly analogous to our choice of encoding radix in Section 4.2.
