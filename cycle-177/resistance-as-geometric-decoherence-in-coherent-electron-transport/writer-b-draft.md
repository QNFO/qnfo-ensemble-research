# A Coherence-Budget Model of Electrical Resistance in Mesoscopic Conductors

## Abstract

Engineers designing cryogenic interconnects, single-electron devices and mesoscopic sensors must decide, at layout time, whether a given conductor segment will transport charge coherently; today that judgement requires device-specific transport simulation or empirical iteration, because resistance and decoherence are computed in separate formalisms. The specific gap is the absence of a single closed-form expression that converts bias, temperature and geometry into a coherent-transmission probability and hence into a resistance estimate. We construct such a coherence-budget model: a conductor of length $L$ with $N$ transmission channels of ballistic transmission $T$ carries an effective transmission $T_{\mathrm{eff}} = T\exp(-\Gamma_\phi L/v_F)$, where $\Gamma_\phi = \max(k_B T, eV)/\hbar$ is a phenomenological dephasing-rate bound and $v_F$ the Fermi velocity. The model reproduces the ballistic conductance quantum $G_0 = 2e^2/h = 77.48\ \mu\mathrm{S}$ ($R_Q = 12.906\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$) and yields, for a 1 µm, 10-channel contact with $T = 0.9$ at 4 K and 100 µV bias, a resistance of $2.42\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ against a ballistic value of $1.43\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, with a thermal coherence length $L_\phi = 1.91\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ at 4 K falling to $25.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ at 300 K. The result gives interconnect designers an order-of-magnitude sizing rule and a falsifiable scaling law, $R \propto \exp(\Gamma_\phi L/v_F)$, testable with standard four-terminal measurements.

## 1. Introduction

A conductor that carries charge coherently behaves differently from one that does not: it can split electron waves, sustain interference-based sensors, and connect qubits without dephasing them. The people who face this decision concretely are designers of cryogenic computing hardware, of mesoscopic sensors and of single-electron devices, who must choose conductor lengths, materials and operating bias before fabrication. The cost of guessing wrong is a fabricated device whose interference contrast, shot-noise floor or qubit coupling is degraded below specification, discovered only after a full cool-down and measurement campaign, each of which is slow and expensive. At present there is no closed-form rule that tells a designer, from bias, temperature and geometry alone, what fraction of a conductor's transmission remains phase coherent; the quantity must be extracted per device from weak-localization corrections, universal-conductance-fluctuation amplitudes or full transport simulation.

A usable rule would change the design process in a specific way: conductor length could be budgeted against a computed coherence length at the schematic stage, the way timing budgets are computed before place-and-route in digital design. If the rule holds, a designer who knows the operating temperature, the maximum bias across a segment and the Fermi velocity of the metallization can decide in one line of arithmetic whether the segment sits in the coherent regime ($L \ll L_\phi$), the crossover ($L \sim L_\phi$) or the classical regime ($L \gg L_\phi$), and can size the segment accordingly before committing to fabrication.

This paper builds that rule as an explicitly phenomenological model, derives all of its numbers from stated inputs, and states the conditions under which measurement would refute it. The model is deliberately simple: it treats dephasing as a Poisson process along the trajectory and bounds the dephasing rate by the larger of the thermal and bias energy scales divided by $\hbar$. Its value is not microscopic accuracy but a computable, falsifiable first-order budget that connects the resistance quantum, which is exact, to the dephasing physics, which is not.

The contributions are:

- A closed-form coherence-budget expression $T_{\mathrm{eff}} = T\exp(-\Gamma_\phi L/v_F)$ with an explicitly stated dephasing-rate bound $\Gamma_\phi = \max(k_B T, eV)/\hbar$, derived in Section 3 and evaluated numerically in Section 4.
- A full numerical evaluation of the model for a reference device (10 channels, $T = 0.9$, $L = 1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$), giving $R = 2.42\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ at 4 K and 100 µV against a ballistic value of $1.43\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, with all arithmetic shown in Section 4 and summarized in Section 5.
- A falsifiability analysis: the specific measurement outcome (resistance versus length and temperature scaling) that would refute the model, given in Section 6.
- A practical pathway for interconnect and mesoscopic-device designers, including the smallest de-risking experiment and the technology-readiness assessment, given in Section 7.
