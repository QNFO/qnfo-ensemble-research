# Reflection Closure of Line Sets: A Minimal Generative Route to Dihedral Symmetry

## Abstract

Physical symmetries are usually posited rather than derived: model builders select a group such as $\mathrm{O(3)}$ or a gauge group by hand, and mis-selection degrades models and obscures observables. This paper tests a minimal generative alternative in two dimensions: begin from a finite set of lines (acts of distinction) and close the set under reflection across each of its members. We prove that if the initial lines pass through a common point, the closure is always exactly the reflection system of a dihedral group $\mathrm{I_2}(n)$, with $n$ equal to the least common multiple of the pairwise rotation orders, and that the closure consists of $n$ lines at angles $\pi j/n$. We derive the closed-form frequency distribution of the emergent group on a rational angle grid of resolution $N$, $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{I_2}(q)) = \varphi(q)/N$ for $q \mid N$, and compute the full distribution for $N = 12$: $\mathrm{I_2}(12)$ arises with probability $1/3$. We prove a dichotomy: under continuously distributed angles the closure is finite with probability $0$, while any rational grid guarantees finiteness. We further prove that non-concurrent initial lines generate translations and hence infinite groups. The results give the proposed closure rule exact mathematical substance in two dimensions, yield a falsifiable computational protocol, and frame the extension to hyperplane arrangements and the finite Coxeter classification as the next testable step.

## 1. Introduction

Anyone who builds quantitative models of physical systems must decide, before any fitting begins, which symmetry group the model respects. This decision falls on computational physicists constructing atomistic simulators, on machine-learning researchers designing architectures for physical prediction, and on field theorists choosing gauges in which observables remain interpretable. The cost of the decision is concrete. A neural network without the correct rotational constraint can regress data that violate the very invariance the underlying physics demands, and practitioners respond by embedding constraints such as even/odd symmetry directly into network layers to keep predictions physically admissible [3]. In gauge theories the problem is sharper still: the choice of gauge, which addresses unphysical redundancies, can interfere with how physical symmetries constrain observables, so that symmetry constraints are explicit in some gauges and hidden in others [1]. In cosmological models, a symmetry manifest in a gauge-fixed description need not be a symmetry of the physical dynamics at all, and disentangling the two requires case-by-case analysis [8]. In every one of these settings, the working symmetry group is an input that must be chosen correctly, and a wrong or hidden choice blocks interpretation, costs retraining, or produces order parameters that fail to diagnose the physics.

What would change if symmetry groups could instead be derived? If a single minimal rule — draw a distinction, then close under reflections across what has been drawn — reliably generated the classified finite reflection groups, with the specific group determined only by the initial configuration, then the choice of symmetry would reduce to a choice of initial conditions, and the space of possible symmetries would be enumerable in advance. This is the motivation supplied by the syntactic-generation program, in which a basic syntactic token, the drawing of a boundary separating inside from outside, is posited as the single foundational element of a relational ontology [14], with discrete relational boundaries replacing continuous substance-based backgrounds [13] and closure structures organized in a unified computational framework [12]. The present paper does not attempt to establish that grand claim; it establishes the first precisely checkable instance of it, in the plane.

The setting is two-dimensional. A finite set of lines through a common point is iterated under the operation "adjoin the mirror image of every line across every line already in the set." We ask: which finite groups of plane isometries can the closure generate? The answer, proved here with explicit matrix arithmetic, is: exactly the dihedral groups $\mathrm{I_2}(n)$, and nothing else, with $n$ computable in closed form from the initial pairwise angles. We also compute exactly which groups emerge, and with what frequencies, when initial angles are drawn from a rational grid — answering the "with what frequencies" part of the motivating conjecture with exact enumeration rather than simulation. The complementary result is a measure-zero dichotomy: with continuously drawn angles, finiteness has probability exactly $0$.

The contributions are:

- A proof, by explicit $2 \times 2$ matrix computation, that reflection closure of any finite set of concurrent lines yields precisely the $n$-line reflection system of the dihedral group $\mathrm{I_2}(n)$, with $n = \operatorname{lcm}$ of the pairwise rotation orders (Section 4).
- Worked closure computations for the cases $\theta = \pi/5$ (five lines, group order $10$), the $\mathrm{A_2}$ configuration at $60^\circ$ (three lines, group order $6$), and the mixed configuration at $\pi/3$ and $\pi/4$ (twelve lines, group order $24$) (Section 4).
- An exact derivation of the emergence frequencies on a rational grid: $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{I_2}(q)) = \varphi(q)/N$ for each divisor $q$ of the grid denominator $N$, with the complete table computed for $N = 12$ (Section 4).
- A proof of the finite/infinite dichotomy: continuously distributed initial angles give finite closure with probability $0$, while any rational grid guarantees finiteness with $n \le N$; and a proof that non-concurrent initial lines generate translations, hence infinite groups (Section 4).
- A falsifiable computational protocol for the closure algorithm, and a statement of the conjectural extension to hyperplane arrangements in $n$ dimensions and the finite Coxeter classification, with the explicit observation that would falsify the program (Sections 3 and 6).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The physical background is that symmetries are treated as the most basic properties of physical systems, so that a sound formulation should make them as explicit as possible; [1] analyzes how gauge fixing, which handles unphysical gauge symmetries, can interfere with this explicitness in two ways — symmetry constraints on observables are not explicit in all gauges in the presence of approximations, and physical symmetries need not be explicit in gauge-fixed correlation functions — and introduces symmetric gauge fixings for which these problems are absent, using center symmetry in finite-temperature Yang–Mills theory as the working example. This matters here because it documents the practical cost of treating a symmetry as an externally chosen, representation-dependent input rather than an intrinsic output; the closure program proposes the opposite orientation, in which the group is derived and only the initial configuration is chosen.

The same orientation problem appears in quantum mechanics. [11] studies the Landau problem, where the widespread viewpoint ties the symmetric gauge to Laguerre-type eigenfunctions respecting rotational symmetry and the Landau gauges to Hermite-type eigenfunctions respecting translational symmetry; [11] shows instead that three conserved quantities — two pseudomomenta and one conserved orbital angular momentum — exist independently of the gauge potential, with covariant transformation properties under arbitrary gauge transformations, so that eigenfunctions can be written without committing to a gauge. The lesson taken here is that symmetry content is a property of the system's structure, not of the representational choice; a generative rule that produces the group from initial conditions would make this independence structural rather than incidental.

Symmetries can also be emergent rather than fundamental. [6] addresses how gauge symmetries, which indicate redundancies of description, can appear in systems containing no trace of them at a more fundamental level, and demonstrates with a complex Klein–Gordon field example that local symmetry does not imply gauge symmetry; [6] further shows that in its analyzed example a stress-energy tensor not invariant under a set of local symmetries ends up invariant under emergent gauge symmetries, while cautioning that which quantities retain operational meaning must be determined case by case. This supports the logical possibility the closure conjecture requires: a symmetry present at the effective level need not be posited at the foundational level, because a generative mechanism can produce it.

Discrete foundations give a second point of support. [10] presents a procedure for obtaining a "minimal" discretization of a partial differential equation — one scalar function of two variables replaced by a partial difference scheme involving $N = 3$ difference equations — that preserves all of the PDE's Lie point symmetries by construction, and applies it to the linear heat equation while noting that if the world is discrete at the microscopic level, the fundamental equations are finite difference ones and differential equations are approximations. [10] demonstrates that symmetry can be carried exactly through a discrete, combinatorial construction rather than recovered only in a continuum limit; the closure rule studied here is the group-theoretic analogue, a discrete combinatorial operation whose output is an exact symmetry group.

The machine-learning literature quantifies both sides of the posited-versus-learned distinction. [7] studies unconstrained models operating on decorated point clouds, where the relevant group for atomistic surrogates is the Euclidean group $\mathrm{E(3)}$, and develops metrics quantifying how accurately models without hard-coded equivariance constraints come to satisfy transformation laws, noting that the mounting evidence that symmetry-adapted architectures are not necessary sits uneasily with the foundational role of symmetry enshrined in Noether's theorem. [3] takes the enforcement route, designing a "hub layer" that, used as the last hidden layer of a multilayer perceptron, guarantees the regression is even or odd as desired, and reports that networks with embedded physics are faster to train and exactly satisfy known constraints such as conservation laws. Both works presuppose that the target group is known in advance; a closure rule that enumerates the candidate groups from initial conditions would supply the missing prior.

Complete classification results in combinatorics provide the methodological template. [2] gives, for the $\mathsf{NP}$-hard Edge Dominating Set problem, a complete classification of kernelization behavior parameterized by the size of a modulator to $\mathcal{H}$-component graphs for all finite sets $\mathcal{H}$, with polynomial kernelizations available exactly under stated conditions and lower bounds ruling out polynomial kernels otherwise unless $\mathsf{NP} \subseteq \mathsf{coNP/poly}$; [2] also records that EDS admits a $2$-approximation by any maximal matching and an $\mathcal{O}^{*}(2.2351^{k})$ algorithm. The structural parallel is direct: a closure-type operation (kernelization reduces an instance while preserving the answer) admits a clean dichotomy across an entire parameterized family, and the present paper aims for the same style of exhaustive statement for reflection closure.

Structural coincidence theorems of the form "a derived set is contained in a canonical set" recur in analysis. [5] proves, for solutions $u$ of total variation and higher-order regularisation problems, the jump set containment $\mathcal{H}^{m-1}(J_u \setminus J_f) = 0$ — the jump set of the solution is contained in the jump set of the data — using a double-Lipschitz comparability condition on the regularisation functional and Lipschitz shift transformations, extending the technique from total variation through Huber regularisation to variants of $\mathrm{TGV}^2$. The proof shape — show that an object produced by an iterative or variational process cannot escape a canonically classified set — is the shape of the closure proofs in Section 4.

Probabilistic enumeration of rare structural outcomes has a developed toolkit. [9] studies the Smith set under impartial culture with $m = 2\ell - 1$ voters and $n$ alternatives, and proves the upper bound $\max\{\mathbb{P}(|S_{m,n}| = s), \mathbb{P}(|S_{m,n}| = n - s)\} \le \lambda_{\ell}^{s} (s/n)^{s(\ell - 1)/\ell}$ via the constants $c_{\ell} = \int_{[0,\infty)^{m}} e^{-\sigma_{\ell}(\mathbf{w})}\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}$, resolving the conjectured sharp decay rate $\mathbb{P}(|S_{m,n}| = s) = \Theta_{\ell,s}(n^{-s(\ell-1)/\ell})$. The present paper's frequency result, $\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{I_2}(q)) = \varphi(q)/N$, is the exactly solvable cousin: instead of bounding a probability asymptotically, the group outcome distribution over a finite grid is computed in closed form.

Finally, the framing of the program rests on the syntactic-generation framework itself. [14] posits the basic act of making a distinction — a syntactic token whose entire existence is the separation of an inside from an outside, possessing no intrinsic mass, extension, or duration — together with a syntactic void acting as an identity element and strictly recursive rules of combination from which complexity is built from a single starting point. [13] develops this into the Syntactic Token Calculus, arguing that continuous metric spaces are epistemic artifacts of lossy compression by a bounded observer and that geometry is dynamically derived rather than statically assumed. [12] supplies the meta-framework, constructing a universal computational topos that classifies computational closure structures and unifying object, model, and meta computation. The closure rule analyzed here is a deliberately minimal, independently checkable fragment of this agenda: one operation (reflection), one substrate (lines), one output class (dihedral groups), all of it provable and falsifiable without adopting the wider framework.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Objects and closure operator

Work in the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. A line through the origin is identified by its direction angle $\alpha \in [0, \pi)$, since a line is unoriented. Let $L = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m\}$ be a finite set of such angles, all lines concurrent at the origin. Define the reflection operator $\rho_{\beta}$ on angle sets by

$$\rho_{\beta}(\alpha) = 2\beta - \alpha \pmod{\pi}.$$

This is the mirror image of the line at angle $\alpha$ across the line at angle $\beta$; the reduction modulo $\pi$ reflects the unorientedness of lines. The reflection closure of $L$ is the smallest set $C(L) \subseteq [0, \pi)$ containing $L$ with $\rho_{\beta}(\alpha) \in C(L)$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in C(L)$. It exists because $[0,\pi)$ is finite when angles are restricted to a finite grid and, in the general case, because the group generated is at most countable; concretely, the operator is monotone on the lattice of angle sets and the iteration

$$L_0 = L, \qquad L_{t+1} = L_t \cup \{\,2\beta - \alpha \bmod \pi : \alpha, \beta \in L_t\,\}$$

is increasing, $L_t \subseteq L_{t+1}$, so it stabilizes whenever a bound on $|L_t|$ is known. Section 4 supplies the bound $|C(L)| = n \le$ (group order), proving termination.

The group generated by $L$ is $G_L = \langle s_{\alpha_1}, \dots, s_{\alpha_m} \rangle$, where $s_{\alpha}$ is the orthogonal reflection of $\mathbb{R}^2$ across the line at angle $\alpha$. The closure set $C(L)$ is exactly the set of reflection axes of $G_L$: every element of $G_L$ is a product of generators, and the reflections in a group generated by reflections are precisely the conjugates of the generators, whose axes are the images of the original lines under the group — which are exactly the lines produced by the closure iteration.

### 3.2 Algorithm

The computational protocol, stated here and not yet executed as a large-scale simulation (all quantitative results below are derived analytically in Section 4), is:

1. Represent angles exactly as rationals in units of $\pi$ (pairs of integers), or as arbitrary-precision reals with a rationality test; never as machine floats, since the finite/infinite dichotomy of Section 4.5 is destroyed by rounding.
2. Given initial angles $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m\}$, compute all pairwise differences $\alpha_i - \alpha_j$, reduce to lowest terms $p_{ij}/q_{ij}$ (in units of $\pi$), and set $n = \operatorname{lcm}_{i,j} q_{ij}$.
3. Predicted output: the $n$ angles $\{\pi j/n : j = 0, \dots, n-1\}$ and the group $\mathrm{I_2}(n)$ of order $2n$.
4. Verify by explicit iteration of $L_{t+1}$ from $L_0$ until stabilization, and check $|C(L)| = n$ and angle set equality.
5. Record the emergent group label $\mathrm{I_2}(n)$; aggregate over many random initial configurations to obtain emergence frequencies, to be compared against the closed-form prediction of Section 4.6.

For non-concurrent initial lines (Step 2 fails to apply), the protocol instead tests for translation components, per Section 4.7.

### 3.3 What is claimed and what is conjectured

Proven in Section 4: the two-line case, the general concurrent case, termination, the grid frequency law, the measure-zero dichotomy, and the parallel-line obstruction. Conjectured, not proven: that hyperplane arrangements in $n$ dimensions close into the full finite Coxeter classification, and that the rule extends to physical symmetry generation beyond the planar reflection setting. These are carried as explicit conjectures with falsification criteria in Section 6.
