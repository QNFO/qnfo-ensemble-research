# Ultrametric Confinement as Passive Quantum Error Correction: Energy Barriers, Code Parameters, and Cooling Budgets on Bruhat–Tits Trees

## Abstract

Active quantum error correction dominates scalable architectures, but its repeated syndrome extraction, real-time decoding, and ancilla overhead consume a substantial fraction of hardware resources, and any architectural alternative that removes the correction cycle would change the scaling of fault-tolerant systems. The specific gap addressed here is the absence of a quantitative, fully derived account of how non-Archimedean (ultrametric) geometry could confine errors passively: prior proposals assert an exponential energy barrier on the Bruhat–Tits tree $T_{p+1}$ without computing code parameters, failure probabilities, and cooling budgets in one consistent framework. This paper formalizes logical qudits on balls of $T_{p+1}$, derives the exact ball sizes $n_t = 1 + (p+1)(p^{t}-1)/(p-1)$ (for $p=3$: $n_1=5$, $n_2=17$, $n_3=53$), the barrier $B(d)=\lceil p/2\rceil^{d}$, and the passive failure probabilities under independent depolarizing noise at $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$: $P_{\mathrm{pass}}\approx 9.98\times10^{-6}$ per epoch for the flagship $p=3$, $t=1$ block (a suppression factor of $100.2$ over an unencoded qudit) and $P_{\mathrm{pass}}^{(2)}\approx 6.73\times10^{-7}$ for $t=2$. Concatenated eight-fold majority voting at physical error rate $0.40$ suppresses failure through $0.1736704$, $5.549\times10^{-3}$, and $2.905\times10^{-10}$ over three levels, consistent with the barrier bound $p_L(d)\le\varepsilon^{B(d)}$. The thermal-activation reading gives $p_L(d)\le e^{-B(d)}$ at $k_{\mathrm{B}}T=1$, and the cooling-budget analysis shows a depth-4 barrier requires a per-site gap $\Delta\ge1.727\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ for a $10^{-12}$ logical error target. The results convert the ultrametric passive-QEC conjecture into explicit, falsifiable numbers and identify the symmetric-encoder construction as the binding open task.

## 1. Introduction

Scalable quantum computing is presently gated by the cost of active error correction. Stabilizer-code architectures such as the surface code protect logical qubits only by running an unending cycle of syndrome measurement, classical decoding, and feedback; every cycle consumes ancilla qubits, measurement hardware, low-latency classical compute, and electrical power, and the overhead grows with the code distance [13], [14]. The groups facing this problem concretely are the builders of superconducting, trapped-ion, and neutral-atom processors, for whom the correction layer — not the raw qubit count — is increasingly the binding constraint on scaling, and the designers of quantum memories, for whom a stored state that decays between active cycles is a standing liability. If a storage geometry existed that suppressed logical errors without any measurement cycle at all, the correction layer for quantum memories could be removed from the real-time control stack and replaced by a static energy landscape and a cold bath; the resources now spent on decoders and ancilla refresh would be redirected to the much smaller problem of maintaining a temperature far below an energy gap.

Passive protection is not an empty category. The classical ferromagnet is the canonical example: a low-temperature Ising system retains its magnetization — a stored bit — for a time exponentially long in system size, with the environment itself performing the correction [2]. Bosonic cat and kitten codes reach continuous photon-loss correction at breakeven with drives rather than measurement cycles [1], a non-reciprocal superconducting circuit can be designed so that its ground space consists of approximate Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill codewords, protected by an energy gap without active intervention [5], and subsystem (gauge) degrees of freedom provide passive error suppression inside otherwise active codes [6]. What none of these supply is a geometry in which the *propagation* of errors between logical alternatives is blocked by the metric structure itself, so that confinement follows from an inequality rather than from an engineered dissipator or a gap that must be checked case by case.

The candidate geometry is the ultrametric one. On the $p$-adic numbers $\mathbb{Q}_p$ the norm satisfies the strong triangle inequality $\|x+y\|_p\le\max(\|x\|_p,\|y\|_p)$, which forces balls to be either nested or disjoint; the combinatorial skeleton of this geometry is the Bruhat–Tits tree $T_{p+1}$, the homogeneous tree in which every vertex has exactly $p+1$ neighbors [12], [13]. The claim under examination, stated precisely in [13] and [14], is that this nesting structure forces any logical error to climb a hierarchical barrier of height $B(d)=\lceil p/2\rceil^{d}$ in tree depth $d$, so that at fixed temperature the logical error rate is thermally suppressed as $e^{-B(d)}$ — an exponentially improving protection bought with depth rather than with active cycles. If this holds, the decision it changes is architectural: memory and idling-qubit protection could move from the active control stack to a passive energy landscape, and the comparison between correction schemes would be a cooling-budget comparison rather than a decoder-latency comparison.

This paper derives the quantitative core of that claim and states plainly what remains conditional. The contributions are:

- The exact ball-code parameters $n_t = 1 + (p+1)(p^{t}-1)/(p-1)$ for $p=2$ and $p=3$, with the conditional distance $d=2t+1$, derived in Section 4.1.
- The barrier recursion $B(d)=\lceil p/2\rceil^{d}$ with explicit values $B(3)=8$ and $B(4)=16$, and the resulting logical-error bound $p_L(d)\le\varepsilon^{B(d)}$, giving $p_L(3)\le6.5536\times10^{-4}$ and $p_L(4)\le4.295\times10^{-7}$ at $\varepsilon=0.40$, derived in Section 4.2.
- Exact concatenated majority-vote failure probabilities $0.1736704$, $5.549\times10^{-3}$, and $2.905\times10^{-10}$ at physical error rate $0.40$, derived in Section 4.3.
- Passive failure probabilities for the depolarizing ball code, $P_{\mathrm{pass}}\approx9.98\times10^{-6}$ ($p=3$, $t=1$) and $P_{\mathrm{pass}}^{(2)}\approx6.73\times10^{-7}$ ($t=2$) at $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$, with suppression factors $100.2$ and $1.49\times10^{3}$, derived in Section 4.4.
- Thermal-activation bounds $p_L(3)\le3.355\times10^{-4}$, $p_L(5)\le1.266\times10^{-14}$, and $p_L(10)\le10^{-444.7}$ at $k_{\mathrm{B}}T=1$, and the cooling-budget requirement $\Delta\ge1.727\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ per site for a $10^{-12}$ target at depth 4, derived in Section 4.5.
- A falsification protocol, adapted from the neutral-atom proposal of [13], specified in Section 7.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The standard theory of quantum error correction encodes $k$ logical qubits into $n$ physical qubits and corrects a measured error set by syndrome extraction and recovery. Shor's nine-qubit code first protected one qubit against general single-qubit errors, Steane's seven-qubit code did the same more economically, and the stabilizer formalism of Gottesman generated the modern code families; general noise on qubits digitizes into Pauli errors, so correcting a finite error set suffices [10]. The pedagogical through-line from classical codes is redundancy: a binary symmetric channel with flip probability $p$ is beaten by majority-protected encodings, and quantum codes inherit both the syndrome-measurement structure and the correctability conditions from their classical ancestors [9]. These foundations matter here because the ultrametric proposal is, at the classical layer, a hierarchical majority scheme; its quantum claim is that the same hierarchy confines *coherent* error paths, which is exactly the part the classical theory does not settle [10], [9].

Two extensions of the stabilizer paradigm reorganize *which resources* do the protecting. Entanglement-assisted quantum error-correcting codes (EAQECCs) allow the sender and receiver to spend pre-shared entanglement (ebits) in addition to ancillas, which removes the dual-containing constraint on the underlying classical codes and can raise both rate and correctable-error count [8]. A resource classification sorts techniques by redundancy type: ancilla qubits support active and passive capability, ebits support active capability, and gauge qubits — qubits deliberately left noisy — support passive capability within subsystem (operator) codes; the same framework extends to continuous variables, where displacement operators $X(x)=\exp\{-i\pi x\hat{p}\}$ and $Z(p)=\exp\{i\pi p\,\hat{x}\}$ generate the error algebra and entanglement-assisted schemes remain vulnerable to finite squeezing and detector inefficiency [6]. The ultrametric proposal occupies the passive column of this classification but replaces the redundancy resource entirely: no ancillas, ebits, or gauge qubits are spent, and the geometry itself is the redundancy [12], [13].

A distinct line of work makes passivity *dynamical* rather than structural. Continuous-time quantum error correction treats both noise and correction as processes continuous in time, using continuous weak measurement and feedback; its analysis via the subsystem principle shows that correctable information always lives in a subsystem that is unitarily recoverable after the noise map, and the code-space fidelity reaches a quasi-equilibrium value that serves as the performance metric [7]. Error suppression in adiabatic quantum computing unifies energy-gap protection, dynamical decoupling, and local-cooling error correction in one master equation for stabilizer-encoded adiabatic evolution, and calculates explicit leakage rates from the codespace; the same dynamical model applies wherever stabilizer encoding protects evolving ground states [11]. Both works demonstrate that protection can run without discrete measurement cycles, but both still require an active agent — a feedback controller in [7], a cooling or driving mechanism in [11]. The ultrametric scheme aims one step further down: no controller at all, only a landscape.
