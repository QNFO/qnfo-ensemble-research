# The Brain as a Filter: A Channel-Capacity Model of Phenomenological Invariants in Altered States of Consciousness

## Abstract

Clinicians and trial designers working with altered states of consciousness — psychedelic-assisted therapy, meditation-based interventions, intensive care — currently lack a quantitative framework that links measurable neural gating mechanisms to the cross-culturally recurring phenomenological features (timelessness, unity, presence) reported in those states. Existing production-based theories treat such features as brain-generated and offer no principled account of why the same features recur across cultures. This paper formalizes the opposing filter hypothesis, in which the brain attenuates rather than generates a grounding informational layer, as an information-theoretic channel-capacity constraint. We model the filter as a transmittance parameter $\lambda$ acting on a noisy ground signal, derive the cross-cultural correlation $\rho(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}/(\lambda^{2}+(1-\lambda)^{2})$ for reported invariants, and show that a correlation of $0.9$ requires $\lambda \geq 0.75$, while a waking-state transmittance of $\lambda \approx 0.0307$ follows if the invariant component is three orders of magnitude fainter than idiosyncratic content. Filtered channel capacity rises from $0.4307$ to $1.2792$ bits/s when transmittance triples. The model yields a falsifiable scaling law connecting gating-suppression measures to invariant-report statistics, and a design calculation showing that roughly 13 independent cultural samples suffice to test it.

## 1. Introduction

A patient entering a psychedelic-assisted therapy trial, a meditator in a deep-retreat study, and a cardiac-arrest survivor reporting a near-death experience all describe a strikingly similar short list of features: timelessness, a sense of unity, and an intensified sense of presence. The researchers who run these studies — psychiatrists selecting dose schedules, neuroscientists choosing imaging endpoints, cross-cultural psychologists coding interview corpora — face the same gap: they can measure neural changes such as thalamocortical gating reduction or default-mode-network suppression, and they can collect phenomenological reports, but no existing quantitative model predicts which phenomenological features should co-occur with which neural changes, or why the same features recur in culturally isolated populations. Today this gap is bridged only by narrative appeals to "correlates," which blocks the design of mechanistically grounded outcome measures and leaves trial endpoints vulnerable to post-hoc selection. If a formal filter model of the kind developed here holds, a measurable gating index would become a quantitative predictor of invariant-report strength, giving trial designers an a priori endpoint and giving cross-cultural surveys a sample-size target.

The standard explanatory arrow in consciousness science runs from brain activity to experience: neural dynamics generate conscious content, and altered states are reconfigurations of that generation process. The filter hypothesis inverts this arrow. It holds that the brain does not generate consciousness but filters and localizes a pre-existing, non-spatiotemporal informational ground — call it $L_{3}$ — into an embodied perspective — call it $L_{2}$. On this reading, states that reduce filtering (deep meditation, psychedelic states, near-death experiences) partially reveal the structure of $L_{3}$, which explains why they exhibit cross-culturally invariant phenomenological features: the invariants are properties of the shared ground, not of the filtering brain. The hypothesis is not merely metaphysical posturing; it makes a quantitative prediction. If filtering is a channel with transmittance $\lambda$, then the measurable cross-cultural correlation of invariant reports should be a specific function of $\lambda$, and $\lambda$ should track neural gating measures. That prediction can be tested with existing neuroimaging and survey methods, and it can be falsified: if gating suppression grows while invariant-report correlation stays flat or falls in the manner the model rules out, the filter account loses its explanatory advantage over production-based theories.

This paper does not claim that the filter hypothesis is true. It claims that the hypothesis, unlike most alternatives to production theories, can be written as a constrained information channel whose observable consequences are computed before any data are collected, and that those consequences are sharp enough to be wrong. The contributions are:

- A formalization of the brain-as-filter hypothesis as a channel-capacity constraint on a grounding informational layer, with the transmittance parameter $\lambda$ defined and tied to neural gating measures (Section 3).
- A derivation of the cross-cultural invariance function $\rho(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}/(\lambda^{2}+(1-\lambda)^{2})$ under two attenuation sub-models, with the result that $\rho = 0.9$ requires $\lambda \geq 0.75$ (Section 4).
- A numerical capacity analysis showing that tripling transmittance from $\lambda = 0.01$ to $\lambda = 0.03$ raises filtered capacity from $0.4307$ to $1.2792$ bits/s, a factor of $2.97$, with sensitivity $42.02$ bits/s per unit $\lambda$ at $\lambda = 0.03$ (Section 4).
- A transmittance estimate $\lambda_{\text{wake}} \approx 0.0307$ derived from the assumption that the invariant component is $10^{3}$ times fainter than idiosyncratic content, and the resulting tension that even a doubled transmittance yields only $\rho \approx 0.0042$ under uniform attenuation — a constraint that forces the filter to be feature-selective if strong invariance is real (Section 4).
- A design calculation showing that distinguishing $\rho = 0.9$ from a null of $\rho = 0.5$ requires approximately 13 independent cultural samples (Section 4).
- An assessment of how the model compares with bottom-up whole-brain approaches to altered states, what would falsify it, and what the smallest decisive test would be (Sections 5–7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The scientific study of altered states of consciousness has a well-established empirical base. Work on whole-brain models argues that altered states are a prime opportunity to study how global changes in brain activity relate to varieties of subjective experience, and it builds mechanistic accounts of global signatures of altered consciousness from the bottom up, explicitly aiming to converge with high-level theories such as Global Neuronal Workspace and Integrated Information Theory rather than replace them, in deliberate analogy with how statistical thermodynamics bridges microscopic rules and phenomenological principles [8]. That work also carries the standard distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness, and the framing of neural correlates of consciousness as minimal neural events systematically associated with subjective experience [8]. Our filter model occupies the same methodological slot — a mechanistic bridge between neural signatures and phenomenology — but reverses the direction of explanation: instead of generating global signatures from local neural dynamics, it treats the signatures as leakage through an attenuating channel and derives what the leakage should look like.

The most direct methodological precedent for reading a "filter" out of neurophysiology comes from brain-grounded analysis of large language models. That work constructs a word-level brain atlas of phase-locking value (PLV) connectivity in the theta band (4–8 Hz) over 2.0 s sliding windows with 0.5 s step, compresses the edge vector by PCA to 128 dimensions, and uses the SMN4Lang MEG/fMRI dataset of naturalistic story listening (12 subjects, 60 stories) to build the atlas via ridge regression [1]. Its key comparative result is that steering a model along a brain-derived axis shifts log-frequency in the intended direction with lower perplexity cost ($d = 0.9246$; PPL $d = -0.2183$), while a text-only log-frequency probe shifts in the opposite direction and increases perplexity ($d = -0.3021$; PPL $d = 0.4549$), leading its authors to conclude that mapping through neurophysiology can act as a functional filter isolating causal structure that a direct text probe does not capture [1]. We borrow two things from this result: the demonstration that neurophysiological measurements define usable coordinate axes for an informational system, and the explicit use of "filter" as a functional, information-processing term rather than a metaphor.

The term "filter" itself has at least three established uses that must be kept distinct.
