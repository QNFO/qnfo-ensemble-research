# Cross-Ratio Invariants of Braid Networks as Candidate Fault-Tolerance Mechanisms for Topological Quantum Computation

## Abstract

Large-scale quantum computation is blocked by the overhead of quantum error correction: protecting one logical qubit with surface codes demands physical-to-logical qubit ratios of roughly 1000:1, while experimental arrays remain near ten functional qubits. Stabilizer measurement and decoding dominate this overhead, so any encoding whose logical information is carried by a quantity that local errors cannot alter without macroscopic disturbance would change the fault-tolerance cost structure. We formalize a conjecture that logical information in a topological system can be stored in cross-ratio invariants of anyon worldline configurations: projective coordinates $\chi$ that are exactly invariant under global reparametrizations and whose sensitivity to a local displacement of one anchor is linear in the displacement-to-system-size ratio. We prove projective invariance by explicit computation, show that $n$ anchored worldlines carry $n-3$ independent complex invariants, and derive a local-error sensitivity $|\Delta\chi|/\chi \approx \delta/L$ with a worked example giving $2.5\times 10^{-3}$ for a displacement $2.5\times 10^{-3}$ of the system size. We argue gate universality via braid-group action on these invariants, importing the genericity result that almost every two-qubit gate is universal. The formulation is analytical and unreviewed (TRL 1–2); its testable content is a simulation protocol comparing invariant-based and stabilizer decoding, and its falsifier is any demonstration that cross-ratio sensitivity under realistic error models exceeds stabilizer-logical error rates.

## 1. Introduction

Building a useful quantum computer today means building an error-corrected one, and the error correction is the bottleneck. Fault-tolerant, error-corrected quantum computing underpins a significant worldwide effort to construct viable commercial systems, yet the field has experimentally fabricated arrays of only about ten functional qubits against theoretical architectures that presuppose thousands of physical qubits per logical operation [1]. The resource cost is concrete: surface-code protection requires physical-to-logical qubit ratios of approximately 1000:1, and active correction faces a signal-processing ceiling beyond which classical filtering cannot eliminate fundamental quantum back-action [7]. Every additional logical qubit therefore costs roughly three orders of magnitude in hardware, and the decoding pipeline — repeated stabilizer measurement, classical processing, and feedback — dominates the engineering budget. A formulation of topological quantum error correction in which the logical information is carried by a geometric invariant that local disturbances cannot change except at a cost scaling with system size would attack this bottleneck at its root: it would replace a measurement-and-decode cycle with a passively protected coordinate, potentially changing the required physical-to-logical ratio, the decoding latency, and the classical control stack.

The specific gap this paper addresses is representational. Standard topological quantum computation encodes logical information in fusion spaces or code subspaces and protects it through stabilizer operators; the geometric data of the anyon worldlines — where the strands went, in what projective configuration — is treated as a computational history rather than as the carrier of the logical state. We conjecture the reverse: that the natural encoding of correlation patterns in a braid network is a set of cross-ratio invariants, one per four worldline anchors, and that fault tolerance is explained by the projective invariance of these quantities. The cross-ratio is the unique invariant of four points under the projective group; if logical information lives in macroscopic cross-ratios of a correlation network, then a local error — which moves one anchor by a small amount relative to the system — changes the invariant only proportionally to that small ratio, and changing it appreciably requires a disturbance spanning the system. This is a conjecture about where the logical information sits, not a new code with a proven threshold; the paper's honest claim is that the formulation is well-defined, exactly invariant under a specified group, quantitatively insensitive to local errors at a computable rate, and falsifiable by simulation against standard stabilizer decoding.

The approach is analytical throughout. We define cross-ratio invariants on the endpoint configuration of anyon worldlines (or, equivalently, on the external legs of a tensor-network representation of a surface or color code), prove their invariance under projective transformations by direct computation, count the independent invariants, derive the local-error sensitivity with explicit arithmetic, and construct the gate-universality argument from braid-group representations acting on the invariant coordinates, importing the result that almost every gate acting on two or more qubits is universal [4]. We connect the braid-theoretic side to the p-adic program in which braiding is discrete rather than continuous [5, 6], since a discrete braiding geometry changes what "local error move" means and therefore what the invariance must withstand. We use a structured ignorance audit to organize the open questions, because the audit's design principles — content independence, depth ordering, and action closure — fit a conjecture-stage research program whose main deliverable is a precise list of what must be shown next [8].

The contributions are:

- A formal definition of cross-ratio invariants $\chi$ on anyon worldline endpoint configurations, with a proof of exact invariance under projective transformations verified by explicit numerical computation on three distinct Möbius maps (Section 4.1).
- A counting theorem: $n$ anchored worldlines in the plane carry exactly $n-3$ independent complex cross-ratio invariants, derived from the real dimension count $2n - 6$ (Section 4.2).
- A local-error sensitivity derivation showing $|\Delta\chi|/\chi$ is linear in the relative displacement $\delta/L$, with a worked example yielding $2.5\times 10^{-3}$ relative change for a local displacement of $2.5\times 10^{-3}$ of the system size, and a hit-probability estimate $p_{\text{hit}} = 4\pi a^2/L^2 = 1.257\times 10^{-3}$ for single-lattice-spacing errors on a system of $L = 100a$ (Section 4.3).
- A gate-universality argument for braid actions on cross-ratio coordinates, structured by the genericity theorem that the non-universal gates form a lower-dimensional subset of $\mathrm{U}(4)$ (Section 4.4).
- A falsifiable comparison protocol — invariant-based versus stabilizer decoding under matched noise — and a structured statement of the open questions that must be resolved before the formulation can be judged against standard topological quantum error correction (Sections 5, 6, and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The fault-tolerance framework this paper seeks to re-express is the stabilizer-based one. The standard introduction to fault-tolerant quantum computing establishes why quantum error correction cannot simply copy states: the no-cloning theorem forbids any operation satisfying $U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$ for an unknown $|\psi\rangle$, and measuring an encoded block directly destroys the information it protects, so codes must be designed so that syndrome extraction reveals errors without revealing the logical state [1]. The same source emphasizes the engineering stakes: interactions between qubits during circuit execution copy errors, and if this happens in an uncontrolled manner the correction code is overwhelmed and the computation fails [1]. Our proposal accepts these constraints unchanged — cross-ratio invariants must still be measured without collapsing the encoded state — and changes only the proposed carrier of the logical information.

The most direct statement of what a fault-tolerant scheme must achieve against noise is the quantitative bound of the fault-tolerant input/output program. That work considers circuits with quantum input and output, where noise in the gates of the first and last layers is unavoidable, and proves fault-tolerant realizations such that the noisy circuit realizes the original circuit up to residual channels on the input and output systems, with the detrimental effects controlled by characterizing the residual noise as adversarial and local stochastic channels [2]. Its state-preparation theorem gives the explicit bound $\|[\overline{\Phi}]_{\delta} - \mathcal{W}\circ\Phi\|_{\diamond} \leq O(\theta\sqrt{(c\delta)^{k}})$ for a state-preparation circuit with no quantum input [2]. This bound matters for our program because any invariant-based scheme must eventually be embedded in exactly this kind of statement: the invariant coordinates must survive the noisy realization up to controllable residual channels, and the $\sqrt{(c\delta)^k}$ scaling shows how the tolerated noise contracts with concatenation depth $k$. The same source describes Kitaev's framework as using many-to-one codes in which the representation of a quantum state in the code is not unique [2] — a feature our formulation shares, since the cross-ratio configuration is invariant under the projective group and therefore represents the same logical state by many geometric configurations.

Holonomic quantum computation offers the closest existing analogue to a geometrically protected gate. The fault-tolerant holonomic scheme combines holonomic computation with fault-tolerant quantum error correction on stabilizer codes using Hamiltonians of finite locality, avoiding the unscalable alternative that requires couplings becoming increasingly nonlocal as the code grows [3]. Its mechanism is adiabatic dragging of the code subsystem around loops along which the information remains protected, and its conclusion is instructive for us: implemented with the Bacon-Shor code using 2- and 3-qubit Hamiltonians, the scheme inherits the error threshold of the dynamical scheme on which it is based, but because adiabatic gates are slower and some gates must run serially at the lowest concatenation level, the allowed environmental-noise threshold is lower than in the dynamical scheme [3]. This is the trade-off pattern our conjecture must be tested against: geometric protection can substitute for dynamical correction, but it can also pay for that substitution in speed, and the threshold comparison is empirical, not automatic. The subsystem decomposition $\mathcal{H}^S = \bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{H}^A_i \otimes \mathcal{H}^B_i \oplus \mathcal{K}$ used in that work [3] is also the natural home for our invariants: the cross-ratio configuration is a property of the code subsystem $\mathcal{H}^A$, and the co-subsystem $\mathcal{H}^B$ absorbs the dynamical transformations that local errors induce.

The universality side of the argument rests on the genericity theorem for quantum gates. That work proves that a generic two-qubit gate $U \in \mathrm{U}(4)$ — a 16-dimensional manifold — is universal, in that the set of non-universal gates has lower dimensionality than $\mathrm{U}(4)$ itself, and identifies the excluded cases: no 1-bit gate can be universal because 1-bit gates cannot entangle initially unentangled qubits, and no classical gate can be universal because classical gates map computation-basis states to computation-basis states and never to superpositions [4]. Two further points from that source shape our claims. First, its proof leaves open whether gates failing its genericity conditions might be universal by other schemes, so universality arguments of this type are existence statements with identified exceptional sets, not classifications [4]. Second, its conclusion stresses that the proper definition of universality depends on the laws of physics, and that universality in a strong, robust, practical sense is good evidence-supported but not fully proven as generic across technologies [4]. Our universality claim in Section 4.4 is correspondingly modest: we show braid actions move the cross-ratio coordinates through a rich set of transformations and argue by genericity, without claiming a closed classification.

The braid-theoretic substrate is where the conjecture lives. The classical braid group $B_n$ is generated by $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}$ with the relations $\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_i = \sigma_{i+1}\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1}$ and $\sigma_i\sigma_j = \sigma_j\sigma_i$ for $|i-j|>1$, and is realized as the fundamental group $\pi_1(\mathrm{Conf}_n(\mathbb{R}^2))$ of the configuration space of $n$ distinct points in the plane [6]. The p-adic program shows this substrate is not unique: since $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is totally disconnected, with every point its own connected component, the classical definition of $B_n$ as a fundamental group of continuous loops fails entirely at the p-adic place, and the construction instead defines $B_n(\mathbb{Q}_p)$ on the Bruhat–Tits tree of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Q}_p)$, with the tree's discrete geodesics replacing continuous paths, and verifies that the resulting group satisfies the standard braid relations [6]. The synthesis paper extends this to a program of adelic topological quantum computation in which anyon types are arithmetic objects whose manifestation at each place $v$ is a representation-theoretic object, with candidate physical realizations including Bethe-lattice quantum spin liquids on $(p+1)$-regular tree graphs, ultrametric cold-atom systems with hierarchical tunneling amplitudes, and digital quantum simulators programmed over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ [5]. For our conjecture this matters concretely: if braiding is discrete, then a "local error move" is a discrete jump on a tree rather than a small continuous displacement, and the cross-ratio invariants must be defined on the Bruhat–Tits geometry, where the projective group acting on the boundary of the tree replaces the Möbius group of the plane. The Alpha Pi project supplies the engineering motivation from the other direction, arguing that the continuous real-number field underlying standard quantum mechanics is itself a source of fragility — the smooth Bloch sphere admits infinitesimal thermal drifts that accumulate linearly into significant errors — and cataloguing testable signatures of an ultrametric alternative, including anomalies in few-particle interference patterns in ultracold systems and modified tunneling rates in mesoscopic devices [7].

Finally, because this paper is a conjecture-stage program rather than a completed theory, we adopt the discipline of a structured ignorance instrument. The Universal Ignorance Audit is a fifteen-question, five-phase method whose design principles are content independence (questions apply to any subject without modification), depth ordering (questions progress from surface structure to protected depth), self-applicability, and action closure (the audit terminates in actionable uncertainty rather than abstraction) [8]. Its stance, drawn from the tradition of specified ignorance, is that articulating what a research program does not know and why that boundary matters is a professional instrument rather than a failure [8]. We use it in Section 6 to order the open questions of the cross-ratio program from surface (definitional) to protected (physical), and to ensure each open question terminates in a concrete next action.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Setting and notation

We work in the setting of topological quantum computation, where logical information is encoded nonlocally in a system of anyons — quasiparticles whose exchange statistics are governed by braid-group representations — and gates are effected by braiding the worldlines. We define:

- An **anchor configuration** of order $n$: an ordered $n$-tuple of points $P = (z_1, \ldots, z_n)$, $z_i \in \mathbb{C}$, representing the endpoints (or a time-slice of the strands) of $n$ anyon worldlines projected to the plane. We take the plane $\mathbb{C}$ as the archimedean case; the p-adic analogue on the Bruhat–Tits tree is discussed in Section 6 as an open construction.
- The **projective group** $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{C})$: transformations $z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ with $ad - bc \neq 0$, acting on the anchor configuration pointwise.
- The **cross-ratio** of four anchors:
$$\chi(z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) = \frac{(z_1 - z_3)(z_2 - z_4)}{(z_1 - z_4)(z_2 - z_3)}.$$
- A **local error move**: a displacement of a single anchor $z_i \to z_i + \Delta z_i$ with $|\Delta z_i| = \delta \ll L$, where $L$ is the diameter of the configuration, together with the induced unitary or channel on the underlying Hilbert space.

The physical interpretation is a conjecture, stated plainly: we assume that the logical state of the encoded system is a function of the invariant vector $\vec{\chi} = (\chi^{(1)}, \ldots, \chi^{(n-3)})$ of independent cross-ratios of the anchor configuration, and that stabilizer-like operators of the code act to lock these invariants. This assumption is the paper's central hypothesis, not a derived result; everything downstream (sensitivity estimates, universality, comparison protocol) is conditional on it.

### 3.2 Method of analysis

Because the conjecture concerns invariance and sensitivity of a classical function of the configuration, both properties can be established by direct calculation before any quantum machinery is invoked. Our method is:

1. Verify exact projective invariance of $\chi$ by symbolic structure and by explicit numerical evaluation under three distinct Möbius transformations (Section 4.1).
2. Count independent invariants by dimension: the configuration space of $n$ anchors has $2n$ real degrees of freedom; $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ acts with 6 real parameters; the quotient carries $2n - 6$ real dimensions, i.e. $n - 3$ complex invariants (Section 4.2).
3. Derive the first-order sensitivity of $\chi$ to a local error move by differentiating, and evaluate it numerically on a reference configuration (Section 4.3).
4. Estimate the probability that a spatially local error touches a cross-ratio anchor at all, using a uniform-error assumption on a lattice (Section 4.3).
5. Construct the universality argument: braid generators act on the fusion space, hence on the invariant coordinates; by the genericity theorem for two-qubit gates, the non-universal transformations form a lower-dimensional set, so generic braid words are expected to be universal (Section 4.4). This step is an argument, not a proof; we label its assumptions explicitly.
6. Specify a comparison protocol against stabilizer decoding, with all performance numbers left as open simulation outputs rather than projections (Section 5).

### 3.3 What is not derived

We do not derive an error threshold, a code distance, a decoder, or a physical-to-logical ratio for the cross-ratio scheme. The threshold comparison proposed in Section 5 is a protocol whose outcome is unknown; where we quote resource numbers for context (the 1000:1 surface-code ratio [7]), they are properties of the existing schemes, not of ours. Where Section 7 needs order-of-magnitude figures, every input is either derived in Section 4 or cited, and the figure is labeled a projection with its assumptions.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Exact projective invariance of the cross-ratio

**Claim.** For any Möbius transformation $w = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ with $ad-bc \neq 0$,
$$\chi(w(z_1), w(z_2), w(z_3), w(z_4)) = \chi(z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4).$$

**Symbolic verification.** Write $w(z_i) - w(z_j) = \frac{(az_i+b)(cz_j+d) - (az_j+b)(cz_i+d)}{(cz_i+d)(cz_j+d)} = \frac{(ad-bc)(z_i - z_j)}{(cz_i+d)(cz_j+d)}$. Substituting into the cross-ratio, each $w$-term contributes a factor $(ad-bc)$ to the numerator and denominator alike, and the factors $(cz_i+d)$ cancel pairwise between numerator and denominator, leaving $\chi$ unchanged. $\blacksquare$

**Numerical verification.** Take the reference configuration $z_1 = 0$, $z_2 = 1$, $z_3 = 2$, $z_4 = 4$:
$$\chi = \frac{(0-2)(1-4)}{(0-4)(1-2)} = \frac{(-2)(-3)}{(-4)(-1)} = \frac{6}{4} = 1.5.$$

Apply three distinct Möbius maps.

*Map 1: $w = 2z + 3$.* Then $w_1 = 3$, $w_2 = 5$, $w_3 = 7$, $w_4 = 11$:
$$\chi = \frac{(3-7)(5-11)}{(3-11)(5-7)} = \frac{(-4)(-6)}{(-8)(-2)} = \frac{24}{16} = 1.5. \checkmark$$

*Map 2: $w = \frac{z+1}{z+2}$.* Then $w_1 = \frac{1}{2}$, $w_2 = \frac{2}{3}$, $w_3 = \frac{3}{4}$, $w_4 = \frac{5}{6}$:
$$\chi = \frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{4}\right)\left(\frac{2}{3}-\frac{5}{6}\right)}{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{5}{6}\right)\left(\frac{2}{3}-\frac{3}{4}\right)} = \frac{\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{6}\right)}{\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{12}\right)} = \frac{\frac{1}{24}}{\frac{1}{36}} = \frac{36}{24} = 1.5. \checkmark$$

*Map 3: $w = -z + 10$.* Then $w_1 = 10$, $w_2 = 9$, $w_3 = 8$, $w_4 = 6$:
$$\chi = \frac{(10-8)(9-6)}{(10-6)(9-8)} = \frac{(2)(3)}{(4)(1)} = \frac{6}{4} = 1.5. \checkmark$$

The invariance is exact in all three cases. The physical reading: any global reparametrization of the plane — a change of gauge, frame, or scale applied uniformly to the whole correlation network — leaves the logical coordinate untouched. This is the precise sense in which the encoding is "timeless" in the Wheeler–DeWitt sense of the research idea: the invariant is a property of the configuration as a whole, not of its embedding or evolution.

### 4.2 Counting independent invariants

**Claim.** An ordered configuration of $n$ anchors in the plane carries exactly $n - 3$ independent complex cross-ratio invariants.

**Derivation.** The configuration space has $n$ complex coordinates, hence $2n$ real degrees of freedom. The group $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is the set of matrices $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ modulo scaling; a $2\times 2$ complex matrix has 8 real parameters, and projectivization (overall complex scaling, which does not change the transformation) removes 2, leaving $8 - 2 = 6$ real parameters. The quotient of the configuration by the group action therefore has
$$2n - 6 = 2(n-3)$$
real dimensions, i.e. $n - 3$ complex dimensions. The cross-ratios $\chi(z_1, z_2, z_3, z_i)$ for $i = 4, \ldots, n$ (fixing three anchors as the reference triple) are $n-3$ quantities that are invariant by Section 4.1 and functionally independent, since the configuration can be reconstructed from them up to the group action. Hence they exhaust the invariant content. $\blacksquare$

Concrete cases: $n = 4$ gives $2(4) - 6 = 2$ real dimensions, one complex invariant $\chi$; $n = 5$ gives $4$ real dimensions, two complex invariants; $n = 100$ gives $194$ real dimensions, $97$ complex invariants. A logical qubit requires at least a two-dimensional invariant space, so the minimal anchor set for one logical qubit in this scheme is $n = 4$ anchors carrying a single complex $\chi$ whose two real dimensions (after normalization) can encode the qubit — we assume, but do not prove, that a two-dimensional subspace of the invariant values can be identified with the logical $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ span.

### 4.3 Sensitivity to local error moves

**Claim.** Under a local error move displacing one anchor by $\Delta z$, the relative change in $\chi$ is linear in the relative displacement, with a computable coefficient.

**Derivation.** Differentiate $\chi = \frac{(z_1 - z_3)(z_2 - z_4)}{(z_1 - z_4)(z_2 - z_3)}$ with respect to $z_1$:
$$\frac{\partial \chi}{\partial z_1} = \chi \left( \frac{1}{z_1 - z_3} - \frac{1}{z_1 - z_4} \right).$$
For the reference configuration $z_1 = 0$, $z_2 = 1$, $z_3 = 2$, $z_4 = 4$:
$$\frac{1}{0-2} - \frac{1}{0-4} = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} = -\frac{1}{4},$$
so
$$\frac{\partial \chi}{\partial z_1} = 1.5 \times \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) = -0.375.$$

Now apply a local error move $\Delta z_1 = 0.01$. The configuration diameter is $L = |z_4 - z_1| = 4$, so the relative displacement is $\delta/L = 0.01/4 = 2.5\times 10^{-3}$. First-order:
$$\Delta \chi \approx \frac{\partial \chi}{\partial z_1}\Delta z_1 = (-0.375)(0.01) = -3.75\times 10^{-3},$$
and the relative change is
$$\frac{|\Delta\chi|}{\chi} = \frac{3.75\times 10^{-3}}{1.5} = 2.5\times 10^{-3}.$$

This equals the relative displacement $\delta/L = 2.5\times 10^{-3}$ exactly at this configuration — a coincidence of the chosen geometry, but the structural point is general: the sensitivity coefficient $\left|\frac{1}{z_1-z_3} - \frac{1}{z_1-z_4}\right|$ scales as $1/L$ for anchors separated on the scale $L$, so $|\Delta\chi|/\chi$ scales as $\delta/L$. A local error of fixed physical size produces a logical-coordinate perturbation that vanishes as the system grows.
