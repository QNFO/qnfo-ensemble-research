# Vertex-Anchored Geometry: Ball Trees, Quantized Perspective, and Exact Constraint Redundancy in Polystochastic Polytopes

## Abstract

Non-Archimedean (ultrametric) geometry, governed by the strong triangle inequality $d(x,z) \le \max\{d(x,y),d(y,z)\}$, lacks the smooth infinitesimal structure on which most physical and geometric intuition rests, and workers building $p$-adic and adelic models currently lack a principled answer to which geometric objects should carry their constructions. We develop the thesis that the vertex — the discrete extremal point of a tree, complex, or polytope — is that primitive, in three registers. Geometrically, we derive from the strong triangle inequality alone that ultrametric balls are centered at every interior point and that ball counts in a $q$-homogeneous ultrametric tree grow as $N_k = q^k$, with $N_5 = 243$ for $q=3$ and $N_{20} = 1{,}048{,}576$ for $q=2$. Phenomenologically, apparent size in an ultrametric world is quantized as $s_k = (S/d_0)\,q^k$ with adjacent ratio exactly $q$. Combinatorially, for the polystochastic polytope $\Omega_{n,d}$ we derive the line count $L(n,d) = d\,n^{d-1}$ ($L(4,3)=48$, $L(3,4)=108$) and, using the known dimension formula $\dim \Omega_{n,d} = (n-1)^d$, the exact number of redundant line constraints $R(n,d) = d\,n^{d-1} - n^d + (n-1)^d$, giving $R(4,3)=11$, $R(3,4)=43$, and $R(2,3)=5$, the last confirmed by explicit elimination. Independent recomputation verifies the vertex counts $17$ and $53$ for $p=3$ building balls and the depth-ten counts $V_{10}=3070$, $C_{10}=27{,}651$ on the Bruhat–Tits tree. The results replace a previously conjectured redundancy statement with an exact count and sharpen the observational signature of ultrametric structure.

## 1. Introduction

Physicists seeking a non-perturbative formulation of quantum gravity and adelic duality face a concrete structural obstacle: the established dualities of quantum field theory and string theory are built on the Archimedean completion $\mathbb{R}$ of the rationals, while the adelic structure of $\mathbb{Q}$ predicts infinitely many non-Archimedean completions $\mathbb{Q}_p$; this omission blocks any unified duality that respects the full adelic symmetry and prevents the use of $p$-adic tools such as ultrametric analysis in high-energy physics [11]. A second, more immediate decision point arises in any construction placed on a Bruhat–Tits building or, more generally, on an ultrametric space: the builder must choose an anchoring set for discrete data such as braiding, fusion, or stabilizer arithmetic, and the choice between the full continuum of points and the discrete vertex set changes the resulting model's combinatorics, its symmetry breaking, and its computational cost [9]. A third group, combinatorialists enumerating the vertices of polystochastic polytopes $\Omega_{n,d}$, needs to know how many of the defining line constraints are redundant, because that number controls both the effective dimension of the polytope and the feasibility of exhaustive vertex enumeration [2],[6].

What would change if the results of this paper hold? A builder of $p$-adic models would have an exact, assumption-light argument that the vertex set — not the point continuum — carries the metric information of an ultrametric space, with quantitative ball counts to budget against; an observer-side modeler would have a sharp, in-principle-testable signature (geometric quantization of apparent size with ratio $q$) distinguishing ultrametric from Archimedean distance structure; and an enumerator of $\Omega_{n,d}$ would know exactly how many of the $d\,n^{d-1}$ line equations are dependent, replacing a conjecture with a count.

The ultrametric setting is defined by the strong triangle inequality

$$d(x,z) \le \max\{d(x,y),\, d(y,z)\},$$

under which every triangle is isosceles with the two longest sides equal, every ball is clopen, and the space is naturally a tree. The $p$-adic numbers $\mathbb{Q}_p$ are the canonical example. This paper advances a single organizing thesis: **in the non-Archimedean world, the vertex — not the point, not the ball, not the chart — is the primitive that carries structure.** Three independent bodies of work converge on discrete extremal points and motivate the thesis. First, on Bruhat–Tits buildings, the vertices correspond to parahoric subgroups, and recent constructions anchor $p$-adic braiding data at the vertex set rather than at arbitrary points of the geometric realization [9]; a discrete gauge theory with an exact Wilson/'t Hooft exchange involution has likewise been formulated on the Bruhat–Tits tree, with all of its counting results expressed in terms of vertices at depth $d$ [11]. Second, in the theory of polystochastic polytopes, the extreme points of $\Omega_{n,d}$ are the objects of interest, and their enumeration for $\Omega_4^3$ and $\Omega_3^4$ has been carried out by identifying vertex supports [2], with product constructions and bounds on the total vertex number $V(n,d)$ [6]. Third, in higher-spin field theory the fundamental data of an interaction is the cubic vertex: generating functions of parity-invariant cubic vertices for massive and massless higher-spin fields have been constructed in light-cone gauge [5],[8], and the cubic-vertex list for $\mathcal{N}=1$ supermultiplets determines which field content a consistent theory requires [4]. Even in three-dimensional duality cascades, the vertices of the fundamental domain of the parameter space are the brane configurations with vanishing relative ranks, and the question of whether the domain tiles the parameter space is a question about its vertices [7].

The plan of the paper is as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature. Section 3 sets up definitions. Section 4 carries out the explicit derivations with all arithmetic shown: the ball–tree correspondence, the ball-count formula $N_k = q^k$, the quantization of apparent size, the line counts and the exact redundancy count for $\Omega_{n,d}$ with an explicit elimination check for $(n,d)=(2,3)$, and independent recomputations that verify published vertex and pair counts on the Bruhat–Tits tree. Section 5 collects the results. Section 6 discusses limitations, failure modes, and falsification criteria. Section 7 translates the results into practical pathways, and Section 8 concludes.

The contributions are:

- A derivation of the ball–tree correspondence (Lemma 1: every ultrametric ball is centered at each of its points) and the ball-count formula $N_k = q^k$ with worked values, from the strong triangle inequality alone (Section 4.1–4.2).
- A derivation of the apparent-size spectrum $s_k = (S/d_0)\,q^k$ with adjacent ratio exactly $q$, giving a sharp observational signature of ultrametric distance structure (Section 4.3).
- The line count $L(n,d) = d\,n^{d-1}$ with an incidence check, and the exact redundancy count $R(n,d) = d\,n^{d-1} - n^d + (n-1)^d$ derived from the dimension formula $\dim \Omega_{n,d} = (n-1)^d$, yielding $R(4,3)=11$, $R(3,4)=43$, $R(2,3)=5$, with the $(2,3)$ case confirmed by explicit elimination (Section 4.4).
- Independent recomputation, with shown arithmetic, of the published building-ball vertex counts $V(2)=17$, $V(3)=53$ for $p=3$ [9] and the tree counts $V_{10}=3070$, $C_{10}=27{,}651$, $f_{10}\approx 5.87\times 10^{-3}$ for $p=2$ [11], together with the reconciliation of ball-counting $N_k=q^k$ with depth-counting $(p+1)p^{d-1}$ (Section 4.5).
