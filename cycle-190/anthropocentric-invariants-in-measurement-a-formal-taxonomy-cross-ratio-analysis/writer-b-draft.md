# Units as Anthropocentric Invariants: Cognitive Transformations, Cross-Ratios, and the Uniqueness of Dimensionless Fixed Points in Measurement

## Abstract

Every dimensional measurement convention — the magnitude of a unit, its reference substance, its numerical radix — encodes features of the human observer, yet metrology treats these conventions as neutral bookkeeping. The specific gap is a formal framework that separates which parts of a unit system carry observer-specific content from which parts survive a change of observer. We model a unit convention as a point on a projective line of scales and define a cognitive transformation group acting on it: changes of numerical radix, changes of body scale, and changes of biological reference substance. In analogy with projective geometry, we ask which quantities are invariant when the observer — the hidden fourth point of the cross-ratio — is moved. We prove that dimensionless ratios are the unique fixed points of this group, and we verify the cross-ratio invariance numerically: the cross-ratio of four collinear scale points equals $4/3$ before and after a Möbius rescaling. We quantify the anthropocentric cost of specific conventions: representing the inverse fine-structure constant $\alpha^{-1} = 137.036$ costs $30$ elementary digit-slots in base 10 against a radix-theoretic optimum of $13.37$, an overhead factor of $2.24$; the SI second's $86400$ subdivisions factor as $2^{7}\cdot 3^{3}\cdot 5^{2}$, a fossil of sexagesimal and circadian choices; and a catalog of the seven SI base units classifies all seven as anthropocentric, with six of seven relocated by the 2019 redefinition. The framework gives standards bodies and message designers a testable criterion: a convention is observer-independent if and only if it is dimensionless.

## 1. Introduction

Metrology committees, standards bodies, and anyone who designs numerical representations for science or engineering face a problem that is usually invisible: the measurement conventions they maintain and transmit are not observer-neutral. The 2019 redefinition of the SI relocated the meter, kilogram, second, kelvin, mole, and ampere onto fixed values of physical constants, yet the choice of which constants, which numerical values, and which base-10 mantissas to publish still carries the imprint of a ten-fingered, water-based, circadian observer [9]. The cost of this imprint is concrete. Numeration systems built on base 10 make some representations longer and some error-prone manipulations harder than alternative radices would, a dependence traced to human anatomy rather than mathematical superiority [10]. A scientific culture that inherits decimal notation inherits a specific cognitive artifact and mistakes it for a law of number itself.

Who faces this today? First, the bodies that maintain unit definitions: every redefinition decision must distinguish the physical content of a constant from the human-scale packaging of its numerical value, and no accepted formal criterion currently separates the two. Second, educators and tool builders in numerical computing, whose radix choices determine representation length and rounding behavior for every user. Third, any program that must encode physical knowledge for a non-human reader — a machine or a hypothetical extraterrestrial counterpart — because such a reader shares none of our body scale, radix habits, or biological reference substances. If the paper's central result holds, these groups gain a decision procedure: a convention is observer-independent exactly when it is a dimensionless ratio, and everything else is a cognitive artifact whose degree of anthropocentric bias can be measured.

What would change if the result holds? Unit redefinitions could be audited by an invariance test rather than by intuition; radix choice could be evaluated by an explicit cost function instead of tradition; and the search for observer-independent structure in physics could be restricted, on principled grounds, to dimensionless quantities. The framework also gives a quantitative answer to a question usually left rhetorical: how much anthropocentric bias does a given convention carry?

This paper formalizes the claim that conventional units are anthropocentric invariants — projective-style quantities in which the human observer is the secretly fixed fourth point — while dimensionless constants such as the fine-structure constant $\alpha$ are observer-independent. The contributions are:

- A formal definition of the cognitive transformation group $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cog}}$ acting on unit conventions, with invariance defined by analogy with projective cross-ratio invariance (Section 3).
- A proof, with an explicit numerical verification, that dimensionless ratios are the unique fixed points of $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cog}}$: the cross-ratio of four scale points takes the value $4/3$ both before and after a Möbius transformation, and a dimensional ratio fails invariance under observer rescaling while a dimensionless ratio is unchanged (Sections 3 and 4).
- A radix-economy quantification of decimal bias: representing $\alpha^{-1} = 137.036$ costs $30$ digit-slots in base 10 against a continuous optimum of $13.37$, an overhead factor of $2.24$, with base 3 the best integer radix at $15$ (Section 4).
- A catalog-derived anthropocentric bias index for the SI: all $7$ base units are classified anthropocentric, $6$ of $7$ were relocated by the 2019 redefinition, and the second's subdivision count $86400 = 2^{7}\cdot 3^{3}\cdot 5^{2}$ is shown to be a composite of sexagesimal, circadian, and planetary-rotation choices (Section 4).
- A falsifiable simulation protocol in which model observers with different radices, body scales, and chemistries reconstruct physics, with convergence on $\alpha$ and divergence on derived unit systems as the discriminating outcome (Section 3, with the de-risking test specified in Section 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The mathematical backbone of this paper is invariant theory. The orbit-closure literature gives the modern template for asking which quantities separate observers. For the simultaneous conjugation action of $\mathrm{GL}_n$ on $m$-tuples of matrices, [1] gives a polynomial-time algorithm to decide whether the orbit closures of two points intersect, and proves that when they do not, an explicit separating invariant can be found in polynomial time; the same work bounds the degree of separating invariants by $\beta_{\mathrm{sep}}(S(n,m)) \leq 4n^{2}\log_{2}(n) + 12n^{2} - 4n$, improving to $4n\log_{2}(n) + 12n - 4$ in characteristic zero. We borrow the structural idea — a group action, orbit closure, and explicit separating invariant — and apply it to the action of cognitive transformations on unit conventions, where the "separating invariant" between two observers is precisely a dimensionless ratio.

The cross-ratio is the classical projective invariant, and [11] states and proves the relevant theorem: for any $T \in \mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{F})$ and any four distinct points $A,B,C,D$, the cross-ratio satisfies $\mathrm{CR}(T(A),T(B),T(C),T(D)) = \mathrm{CR}(A,B,C,D)$, with the proof reducing to the cancellation of the determinants $ad - bc$ of the fractional linear transformation $T(t) = (at+b)/(ct+d)$. [11] further argues that the universe "speaks in pure, scale-relative, invariant proportions," with the cross-ratio and its generalizations as the syntactic primitives of a coordinate-free description, and that words and bases are arbitrary transformations while the structural relationships they describe are not. Our contribution is to make precise which transformations count as "cognitive" and to prove that the resulting fixed points coincide with dimensionless physical quantities.

On the physics side, [12] compiles dimensionless reformulations of $53$ fundamental physics equations across ten disciplines, expressing every quantity as a pure-number ratio to its Planck-scale counterpart with $\hbar = c = G = k_B = 1$. Its Dimensional-Dimensionless Correspondence Theorem shows via the Buckingham Pi theorem that any dimensionally homogeneous law among $n$ quantities involving $k$ independent dimensions rewrites as a relation among $n - k$ dimensionless groups, and its conclusion reports that no formula resists reformulation, including Einstein field equations, Planck's law, and Fermi's golden rule. [12] also invokes Ostrowski's theorem — every non-trivial absolute value on $\mathbb{Q}$ is equivalent either to the real absolute value or to a $p$-adic one — to argue that a dimensional formula containing constants such as $\hbar = 1.054571817 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ is well-defined only at the Archimedean completion. Our fixed-point theorem gives the observer-theoretic reading of this compilation: the dimensionless reformulation is exactly the part of physics that survives cognitive transformations.

The anthropocentric content of the SI itself is cataloged in [9], which reverse-engineers each base unit into its original definition, the cognitive features it encodes, a necessity class, and an observer-independent reformulation. Its unit tables attribute the meter to decimal numeracy, body scale, the Earth meridian, and Euclidean triangulation; the kilogram to water as a substance making up roughly $60\%$ of human body mass, Euclidean volume intuition, the $4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ density maximum of terrestrial lakes, and hand-heft comparability; and the second to circadian rhythm, heartbeat, sexagesimal inheritance, and planetary rotation. Its summary table concludes that all seven SI base units are anthropocentric, with the candela alone unchanged by the 2019 redefinition because it encodes human retinal sensitivity directly. [9] supplies the empirical catalog that our bias index in Section 4 quantifies. The radix-specific version of this critique is developed in [10], which argues that base 10 owes its dominance to the ten fingers available for counting rather than to inherent mathematical superiority, and that this perceived inevitability masks a biological contingency. Section 4 turns that qualitative claim into a number by computing radix economy for the representation of $\alpha^{-1}$.

That measurement conventions are cognitive artifacts rather than neutral labels fits a broader theme in the cognitive literature: symbols and representations emerge from an agent's bodily and social situation rather than being given by a designer. [4] surveys symbol emergence in cognitive developmental systems, arguing from Peircean semiotics that symbols are formed, updated, and modulated by humans through social interaction, so that any definition of symbol holding only for artificial systems is insufficient for human–robot interaction, and that the field must treat both societal symbol systems and agent-centric internal representations together. Unit systems are exactly such socially emergent symbol systems applied to quantity; our cognitive transformation group is a way of asking which of their features are conventional in the semiotic sense and which are not. The bodily grounding of cognition is emphasized from a different direction by [8], which distinguishes minimal from radical embodiment and argues that minimal embodiment remains neurocentric with limited potential for understanding the functional contribution of the body, a distinction relevant to how deeply body scale is baked into a convention. [2] contributes the point that a cognitive system's primary tasks are survival and maximization of life-long utility, with emotional control operating as part of the homeostatic system that keeps bodily parameters in their proper range; the homeostatic parameters it lists, such as heart-beat frequency, are the very physiological quantities that [9] identifies as fossilized in the second. [3] adds that the brain's autonomous internal dynamics organizes cognitive processing rather than merely responding to stimuli, which supports our modeling assumption that an observer's internal state — here, its radix and body scale — is an active filter on representation, not a passive channel.

Two applied works show the same anthropomorphic coupling in engineering. [6] reviews exoskeleton technology and concludes that assistive devices must be anthropomorphic, aligned with the user's limbs, and matched to human biomechanics, with cognitive design extending from intent detection to high- and low-level control; the technology remains immature for non-programmed tasks precisely because it is tuned to one body plan. This is the engineering face of body-scale contingency: a device built around human limb characteristics fails for a different observer, exactly as a unit built around human heft fails for a different physiology. [5] demonstrates the constructive alternative: a cognitive diversity framework for radar target classification in which varying the observation perspective improves automatic target recognition, with a median of $3$ perspectives taken by the algorithm and performance better than both single-perspective and fixed-number multi-perspective schemes. Multi-perspective observation is the operational analogue of our alien-observer test: convergent estimates across diverse observers isolate the invariant part of the signal, which in our setting is the dimensionless content of physics.

Finally, [7] situates System-1 and System-2 processing within the Common Model of Cognition and reports that the supposedly distinctive characteristics of the two systems instead form a spectrum of cognitive properties. This supports our treatment of "cognitive parameters" such as radix preference and body scale as continuous, modelable variables rather than binary human/alien switches.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Unit conventions as points on a projective line

Fix a physical quantity type $q$ (length, mass, duration, and so on). A unit convention for $q$ is a pair $u = (s, r)$ where $s > 0$ is the scale — how many units make up a fixed reference quantity — and $r \in \mathbb{N}$, $r \geq 2$, is the numerical radix in which measurements are expressed. We identify the scale $s$ with a point of the projective line $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{R}_{+})$, so that only ratios of scales are meaningful, not absolute magnitudes. This identification is the formal content of the claim that "the meter" has no absolute meaning, only meaning relative to other scales.

A measurement act then involves four points on this line, in structural analogy with the projective cross-ratio:

$$\mathrm{CR}(q,\; u,\; u_{\mathrm{ref}},\; o) \;=\; \frac{(q - u)(u_{\mathrm{ref}} - o)}{(q - u_{\mathrm{ref}})(u - o)},$$

where $o$ denotes the observer's own scale — the body length, heft, or pulse period against which the unit was implicitly calibrated. In ordinary dimensional measurement the fourth point $o$ is hidden: it entered when the unit was defined (a cloth yard, a resting pulse, a hand-heft) and never appears in the published definition. The cross-ratio analogy states that published unit systems report three of the four points and silently fix the fourth at the human value.

### 3.2 The cognitive transformation group

Define the cognitive transformation group $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cog}}$ as the group generated by the following three families of transformations on conventions $(s, r)$ and on measured values:

1. **Radix change** $\rho_{b}$: re-express all numerical values in radix $b$. This leaves every physical ratio unchanged and acts only on the representation layer.
2. **Body-scale change** $\sigma_{\lambda}$: rescale the observer's characteristic lengths by $\lambda > 0$ and the observer's characteristic masses by $\lambda^{3}$, holding density fixed. This acts on the hidden point $o$ and, through historical calibration, on the unit scale $s$.
3. **Substance change** $\chi_{X}$: replace the reference substance (water, a specific wavelength, a specific planet's meridian) by the analogue available to a different chemistry or environment.

An observer is a triple $\omega = (b, \lambda, X)$ of radix, body scale, and reference chemistry; $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cog}}$ is the group of observer substitutions. Two conventions are *cognitively equivalent* if they lie in the same $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cog}}$-orbit; a quantity is *anthropocentrically invariant* if its value is unchanged across the entire orbit of the human observer $\omega_{\mathrm{H}} = (10, 1, \mathrm{H_2O})$.

### 3.3 Invariance criterion and the fixed-point claim

The central definition is:

**Definition 3.1.** A quantity $Q$ is a *fixed point* of $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cog}}$ if for every $(b, \lambda, X)$, the value that observer $\omega = (b, \lambda, X)$ assigns to $Q$, after reconstructing physics from raw observation, equals the value assigned by $\omega_{\mathrm{H}}$.

**Claim 3.2 (Fixed-point theorem).** The fixed points of $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cog}}$ are exactly the dimensionless quantities — ratios $q_{1}/q_{2}$ of quantities of like dimension, including dimensionless combinations of physical constants such as $\alpha$ — and no dimensional quantity or radix-dependent representation is a fixed point.

The proof has three parts, one per generator. (i) Radix change $\rho_b$ changes the digit string of every numerical value but preserves every ratio of two real numbers, since a ratio of reals is radix-independent; hence dimensionless ratios are invariant under $\rho_b$, while any quantity whose *stated value* is a digit string (a unit magnitude such as $299792458$) is not. (ii) Body-scale change $\sigma_\lambda$: a dimensional quantity $q$ expressed in a body-calibrated unit $u$ transforms as $q \mapsto q/\lambda^{d}$ in the new unit, where $d$ is the quantity's mass-length dimension exponent, while a ratio $q_1/q_2$ of like-dimensioned quantities gains a factor $\lambda^{d}/\lambda^{d} = 1$ and is unchanged. (iii) Substance change $\chi_X$ replaces the reference in $u$; a quantity defined *as* a multiple of that reference (the kilogram as water mass, the kelvin as water triple point) changes value, while a ratio of two quantities referred to a common dimensionless standard does not. Conversely, any quantity that is not dimensionless has $d \neq 0$ in at least one generator's action, so it moves; and any radix-dependent representation moves under $\rho_b$. Hence the fixed-point set is exactly the dimensionless quantities. The cross-ratio computation in Section 4.1 verifies the projective mechanism numerically, and the explicit rescaling computation in Section 4.4 verifies part (ii) with numbers.

### 3.4 Quantifying anthropocentric bias

Two metrics are used throughout. The **radix economy** of representing a positive real $N$ in radix $b$ is

$$E(b) \;=\; b \cdot \left\lceil \frac{\ln N}{\ln b} \right\rceil,$$

the product of the radix and the number of digits needed, counting each digit slot as costing $b$ distinguishable symbols. The continuous optimum over all real $b$ is attained at $b = e$, giving $E^{\ast} = e \ln N$. The **catalog bias index** of a unit system is the fraction of its base units whose defining features are classified contingent or species-specific by the cognitive genealogy of [9].

### 3.5 Simulation protocol for non-human observers

The fixed-point theorem makes a discriminating prediction, testable by simulation. Construct model observers $\omega_i = (b_i, \lambda_i, X_i)$ with, for example, $b_i \in \{2, 3, 8, 12, 60\}$, $\lambda_i \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$, and reference chemistries replacing water. Each observer is given the same raw physical observations (dimensionally tagged event data) and must reconstruct: (a) a unit system, (b) the numerical values of dimensional constants in that system, and (c) a set of claimed invariant quantities. The theorem predicts convergence of all observers on the same dimensionless set — $\alpha = 7.29735 \times 10^{-3}$, $m_e/m_p$, and their kin — and divergence on every derived unit system, with the divergence measurable as the spread of radix economies and of unit magnitudes across observers. Convergence of any observer on a *dimensional* constant as observer-independent, or divergence between observers on a dimensionless ratio, would falsify the theorem. The protocol is specified as a design; no simulation was executed for this paper, and all numbers reported in Section 5 are analytic derivations from stated inputs.

### 4.1 Cross-ratio invariance under a Möbius transformation

Take four collinear scale points $A = 0$, $B = 1$, $C = 2$, $D = 3$ on the projective line, representing, say, a quantity and three unit scales in affine coordinates. The cross-ratio is

$$\mathrm{CR}(A,B,C,D) = \frac{(A - C)(B - D)}{(A - D)(B - C)}.$$

Step 1: numerator $(A-C)(B-D) = (0-2)(1-3) = (-2)(-2) = 4$.
Step 2: denominator $(A-D)(B-C) = (0-3)(1-2) = (-3)(-1) = 3$.
Step 3: $\mathrm{CR} = 4/3$.

Now apply the Möbius transformation $T(t) = 2t + 1$ (a projective transformation with $a = 2$, $b = 1$, $c = 0$, $d = 1$, determinant $ad - bc = 2 \neq 0$), which sends the points to $T(A) = 1$, $T(B) = 3$, $T(C) = 5$, $T(D) = 7$.
