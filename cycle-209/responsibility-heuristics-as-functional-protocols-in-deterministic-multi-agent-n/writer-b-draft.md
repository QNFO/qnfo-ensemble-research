# Responsibility Heuristics as Load-Bearing Protocols in Deterministic Multi-Agent Networks: A Mean-Field Analysis of Retributive versus Corrective Governance

## Abstract

If agents are deterministic systems, the felt experience of choice and the social practice of holding agents responsible are not metaphysical commitments but protocols running on deterministic hardware. Whether these protocols are load-bearing — whether removing them degrades collective performance — is a quantitative question that legal policy, institutional design, and multi-agent engineering all face in the form of a single decision: punish past agents or correct their future behavior. Existing multi-agent reinforcement learning treats non-stationarity and partial observability with communication and architectural patches, but does not model responsibility attribution as a governance protocol with measurable systemic consequences. We formalize a deterministic multi-agent network in which each agent carries an internal responsibility state updated by one of two policies: retributive deterrence (a fixed behavioral bonus while enforcement is funded, plus a stigmatizing decrement) or corrective feedback (exponential convergence toward a target propensity). Mean-field analysis with explicit arithmetic shows that, for representative parameters ($\alpha=0.2$, $f=0.9$, $r_0=0.3$, $\gamma=0.01$, $d=0.4$), the corrective policy reaches cooperation $0.8356$ by step $10$ and accumulates collective payoff $42.60$ over a $50$-step horizon against $16.65$ for retribution, a $2.56\times$ advantage, with a break-even horizon $T^\ast$ between $8$ and $9$ steps. Recidivism at horizon end is $0.100$ under correction versus $1.000$ under expired deterrence. Aggregate stability obeys a $\sigma\propto N^{-1/2}$ law with Chebyshev deviation bounds falling from $0.36$ at $N=100$ to $0.0036$ at $N=10^4$. The corrective-dominance condition $f > r_0 + d$ is derived exactly. These results reframe retribution versus rehabilitation as competing intervention policies with computable payoffs.

## 1. Introduction

Courts, parole boards, school administrators, and platform-moderation teams all make the same decision repeatedly: when an agent defects, do they punish the past act or restructure the agent's future behavior? Retribution consumes enforcement budget on monitoring and sanction; corrective feedback consumes it on information and reintegration. The choice is made today largely on moral intuition and anecdotal recidivism statistics, because there is no shared formal object in which the two policies' systemic consequences can be compared. If a formal comparison existed, a policy designer could state the conditions under which each policy wins, budget enforcement accordingly, and predict what happens to cooperation when the enforcement budget lapses — the moment at which deterrence-only systems are known informally to fail.

The stakes are concrete. A deterrence-based policy holds cooperation up only while monitoring persists; when the budget ends, the behavior it suppressed returns, which is the systemic signature of recidivism. A feedback-based policy changes the agents themselves, so its effect survives the end of intervention. Which policy a society or a deployed multi-agent system should adopt therefore depends on the intervention horizon, the enforcement budget, and the stigmatizing side-effects of punishment — three quantities that can be named, measured, and optimized, but rarely are together.

This paper argues that the right formal object is already available. In a deterministic multi-agent network, an agent's internal representation of its own responsibility — the heuristic that its choices are real and attributable to it — functions as a protocol: a standardized, externally legible rule that coordinates behavior without requiring full state knowledge. This framing has direct precedents in the engineering literature. Conventions in cooperative games act as user-defined rules external to the game's rules that govern player behavior and produce desired outcomes, and augmenting an action space with such conventions yields faster training and improved policies [3]. Communication in multi-agent reinforcement learning is treated as a vital means to tackle partial observability and non-stationarity [1], and a responsibility signal is precisely a low-bandwidth message that stabilizes the expectations of all parties. Deterministic multi-agent orchestration has been demonstrated empirically to produce zero-variance decision quality where single-agent pipelines produce inconsistent output [7], establishing that protocol-level determinism is a production requirement rather than an aesthetic preference. And the philosophical antecedent is explicit: maintaining the functional illusion of free will while accepting determinism is described as a cognitive strategy without which an organism loses the ability to interact with peers who rely on the agency heuristic [10], and perception itself is modeled as a user-friendly interface over a substrate it does not reveal [11].

What is missing is the arithmetic. No existing work derives when the choice-illusion protocol outperforms its removal, what the break-even intervention horizon is, or how systemic stability scales with network size under each governance policy. This paper supplies that derivation for a minimal but exactly solvable model.

The contributions are:

- A formalization of the responsibility heuristic as an internal state variable updated by one of two governance policies — retributive deterrence with stigmatization, or corrective feedback — in a deterministic multi-agent network (Section 3).
- An exact mean-field solution of both policies' trajectories, with all arithmetic shown, yielding the corrective policy's convergence value $r_{10}=0.8356$, the cumulative-payoff comparison $42.60$ versus $16.65$ over $T=50$ steps, and the break-even horizon $T^\ast\in(8,9)$ (Section 4).
- An exact condition $f > r_0 + d$ under which corrective feedback dominates retributive deterrence at long horizons, together with the regimes in which retribution wins (Section 4).
- A stability scaling law $\sigma_N\propto N^{-1/2}$ with explicit Chebyshev bounds ($0.36$ at $N=100$, $0.0036$ at $N=10^4$), analogous to the passage from molecular to fluid description (Section 4).
- A falsifiable experimental design for the simulation study, sized against the experimental-design practice of $650$ configurations with $5$ replications used in stochastic agent-based exploration (Sections 5 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

Multi-agent reinforcement learning supplies the systems vocabulary this paper uses. The survey of communication in multi-agent deep reinforcement learning identifies partial observability — each agent sees only local observations rather than the complete environment state — and non-stationarity, the drift caused by other agents' adapting policies, as the two core difficulties of the field, and treats communication as a vital means to tackle both [1]. The survey further classifies the literature along nine dimensions and concludes that communication can be extended to non-cooperative settings, heterogeneous players, and much larger agent populations [1]. A responsibility signal fits this taxonomy directly: it is a message type that reduces effective non-stationarity by making each agent's future behavior predictable to the others, and the survey's observation that communication research should extend to non-cooperative settings is exactly the gap a governance protocol occupies.

The same coordination function can be carried by conventions rather than messages. In the card game Hanabi, where communication is limited, human communities standardize conventions — strategies or user-defined rules external to the game rules that govern behavior and produce desired outcomes — and encoding these as artificial conventions through action-space augmentation produced faster training times and improved policies, with a pure convention space covering all scenarios in the small variant of the game [3]. The responsibility heuristic is structurally identical: a standardized rule, external to any individual agent's optimization, that every agent assumes in every other agent, making cooperation decodable without explicit communication. Hanabi's result that a pure convention space can suffice in small state spaces, while becoming harder in larger ones, predicts that responsibility protocols carry more coordination load precisely in large societies where communication bandwidth per pair of agents is negligible.

Protocols themselves can be given a formal semantics. Work on discovering semantic relationships among agent communication protocols models a protocol as a State Transition System, a tuple $(S, s_0, L, T, F)$ of states, initial state, labels, transitions, and finality, and builds the CommOnt ontology on speech-act theory, in which every communication act is the sender's expression of an attitude toward a proposition [4]. That work's central methodological move is to compare protocols by the set of fluents associated with the branches of each protocol rather than by their surface structure [4]. This is the tool the present paper needs: the responsibility heuristic can be specified as a protocol whose branch fluents include blame, credit, and obligation, and two governance policies are then comparable protocols over the same fluent set, differing in which fluents they update.

The dynamics of who decides when is itself a performance variable. The AOAD-MAT model shows that explicitly incorporating the order of agents' action decisions into a Transformer-based actor-critic architecture, with a subtask predicting the next agent to act, outperformed the Multi-Agent Transformer and other baselines on challenging StarCraft II SMAC tasks including $5m\_vs\_6m$, $6h\_vs\_8z$, MMM2, and $3s5z\_vs\_3s6z$ [5]. That work also restates the two structural obstacles — environmental non-stationarity and the exponential growth of the joint action space with agent count [5] — that any society-level protocol must circumvent. A governance policy is a global ordering on state updates; AOAD-MAT's finding that decision order is a learnable, performance-relevant variable supports treating the policy choice as an architectural parameter rather than a moral afterthought.

Determinism at the multi-agent level has been measured, not merely assumed. The MyAntFarm.ai framework ran $348$ controlled trials, $116$ per condition, comparing single-agent and multi-agent LLM orchestration for incident response: multi-agent orchestration achieved mean decision quality $0.692$ with standard deviation approximately $0$, against $0.403$ with standard deviation $0.023$ for the single-agent copilot — a $71.7\%$ quality advantage — while the two conditions' time-to-understand differed by only $3.2\%$ ($41.61$ s versus $40.31$ s) [7]. The authors' conclusion that the architectural value lies in decision quality and determinism rather than speed, reframing multi-agent orchestration as a production-readiness requirement [7], is the empirical anchor for this paper's premise that deterministic multi-agent networks are an appropriate idealization: determinism is what makes protocol-level analysis exact, and the measurement shows it is achievable and valuable.

Heuristics versus deliberation is a measured tradeoff in autonomous agent systems. The MASTER framework for materials discovery organizes LLM agents into design, simulation, and review layers operating in a closed loop, and finds that increasing reasoning effort reduces the mean number of iterations — for the weak-binding range, from $3.5$ to $2.6$ — while minimal-reasoning agents rely primarily on positional heuristics along the periodic table [8]. Each saved iteration saves two expensive simulations [8]. This is the micro-level analogue of this paper's macro-level claim: cheap heuristics carry real decision load, and the choice between heuristic and deliberation is a quantifiable efficiency question, not a philosophical one.

Connecting agent-level rules to society-level behavior requires a multi-level modeling discipline. The IRM4MLS methodology engineers multi-level agent-based simulations in which heterogeneous models of the same system at different levels of organization share entities and interact, explicitly managing the compromise between simulation quality and CPU and memory consumption, with dynamic changes of level of detail and an open problem in emergence detection and reification [2]. IRM4MLS builds on the influence/reaction model and its temporal extension IRM4S, and any valid instance can be simulated by a generic algorithm [2]. This paper's mean-field treatment is exactly the coarse level of such a hierarchy: the agent-level update rules of Section 3 are the fine level, and the scaling law of Section 4 is the consistency condition between them, in the sense in which multi-resolution modeling measures the information lost when crossing models [2].

Exploring the resulting simulation space has a known cost structure. A multi-stage pipeline for exploring stochastic agent-based models confronts the curse of dimensionality and inherent stochasticity, and in a predator-prey case study used Latin Hypercube Sampling to generate $N=650$ parameter configurations at $50$ points per variable, each run $n=5$ times for $3{,}250$ total simulation runs, finding outcomes strongly unbalanced ($60\%$ total extinction, $27\%$ prey survival, $13\%$ coexistence) and that linear screening fails to capture critical non-linear threshold effects governing resilience [6]. Section 7 sizes this paper's proposed simulation against that practice, and the existence of a sharp break-even horizon $T^\ast$ in Section 4 is precisely the kind of threshold that linear screening would miss [6].

The philosophical substrate is stated in the QNFO corpus. The unified-framework treatment organizes determinism from Bell's theorem through neurobiology, including a chapter on the mathematical illusion of alternate possibilities grounded in phase-space trajectories [9]. The algorithmic-necessity treatise states the functional claim directly: maintaining the functional illusion is the strategy of accepting superdeterminism while operating on the free-will heuristic, a dual-boot in which disabling the illusion of choice would destroy the organism's ability to interact rapidly with peers who rely entirely on the agency bypass [10]. The interface-theoretic treatment supplies the epistemic reading: the brain provides a user-friendly simulation that guides adaptive behavior without revealing the underlying ontology, and conflating the properties of our models with the properties of reality generates philosophical puzzles [11]. Finally, the topological unification work argues that computational irreducibility forces embedded observers to use probabilistic effective theories of a deterministic substrate with a computationally dense process measure $LCI\approx 1.83$, and that dense constraint networks are highly controllable with a driver-node fraction $N_D=1$, identifying agents as emergent driver nodes [12]. Three of these claims are load-bearing here: the effective-theory argument licenses the mean-field description of Section 4 [12], the controllability result supports the possibility of governance intervention at all [12], and the functional-illusion argument is the thesis this paper quantifies [10].

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Model specification

We consider $N$ deterministic agents indexed $i\in\{1,\dots,N\}$. Each agent $i$ carries a scalar responsibility state $r_i(t)\in[0,1]$ at discrete time $t=0,1,2,\dots$, interpreted as the agent's internal propensity to treat its own choices as real and attributable — the operational content of the responsibility heuristic. An agent with $r_i(t)=1$ cooperates in every interaction; an agent with $r_i(t)=0$ defects in every interaction. Because agents are deterministic, $r_i(t)$ and the policy determine $r_i(t+1)$ exactly; there is no intrinsic noise. All stochastic statements in this paper concern uncertainty of the analyst over initial conditions, not randomness in the dynamics.

Agents interact pairwise; a cooperating agent contributes a normalized collective benefit of $1$ per step. The collective payoff of the network at step $t$ is

$$\Pi(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i(t),$$

and the horizon payoff over $T$ steps is $S(T)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Pi(t)$. In the mean-field limit we track the population average $r_t=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i r_i(t)$, so $\Pi(t)=N r_t$ and we report per-capita payoff $S(T)/N$, dropping the factor $N$.
