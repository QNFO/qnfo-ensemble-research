# Re-Entry of Non-Markovian Boson-Mediated Transport into Light-Harvesting Complexes: A Timescale Analysis with FMO-Grounded Parameters

## Abstract

Photosynthetic light-harvesting complexes transfer excitation energy with near-unity efficiency at ambient temperature, and a recent general framework for non-Markovian Hamiltonian dynamics of boson-mediated energy transfer proposes that structured phonon environments actively direct this transport. The open question is whether that framework, built for generic bosonic networks, applies with predictive value to specific biological antennas, or whether cheaper Markovian dephasing models suffice. We formulate this "re-entry" question as a quantitative timescale test: non-Markovian dynamics are required when the bath memory time $\tau_B$ is not small compared with the coherent transfer time $\tau_T$. Using a two-site Haken–Strobl-type model whose parameters are taken from published FMO dimer values (coupling magnitude $V = 50\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, site-energy gap $\Delta = 150\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), we derive an optimal dephasing rate $\Gamma^* = \Delta = 150\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, an optimal hopping rate $k^* = V^2/\Delta = 16.67\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, a transfer time $\tau_T = 0.3185\ \mathrm{ps} = 318.5\ \mathrm{fs}$, and a single-hop trapping-limited efficiency $\eta = 0.999001$. Comparing $\tau_T$ with FMO coherence lifetimes of $300$–$660\ \mathrm{fs}$ reported in the literature yields memory ratios $R = \tau_B/\tau_T \in [0.63,\ 2.07]$, and a corner analysis over the full published parameter ranges ($V \in [50, 75]\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, $\Delta \in [110, 180]\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) keeps $R > 0.5$ throughout. The non-Markovian framework is therefore required across the entire parameter regime supported by published FMO dimer data, and the result gives modelers a concrete, falsifiable criterion for when the expensive non-Markian treatment is mandatory.

## 1. Introduction

Designers of computational models for photosynthetic energy transfer face a costly methodological fork. Exact non-Markovian treatments of a pigment–protein complex — wavepacket propagation, path integrals, hierarchical equations — are computationally expensive, while Markovian dephasing master equations are cheap but may silently discard physics that matters. Choosing wrong in either direction wastes resources: an unnecessary exact calculation delays parameter scans and spectroscopic interpretation, while an unjustified Markovian reduction can produce quantitatively wrong transfer rates and efficiencies. This fork is not academic. Photosynthesis converts approximately $130\ \mathrm{TW}$ of solar energy into chemical free energy annually, and understanding how excitation energy transfer to the reaction center achieves quantum efficiencies approaching unity under low-light conditions has direct implications for the design of artificial light-harvesting devices [7]. Any modeling choice that systematically biases transfer rates — for example, the finding that classically treated vibrations overestimate transfer rates, with a quantum correction that can approach a factor of $2$ [3] — propagates directly into device design and into the interpretation of ultrafast spectroscopy.

The specific decision this paper addresses is: for a given light-harvesting system, when is the non-Markovian boson-mediated framework of [8] — which treats the phonon environment not as disruptive noise but as a structured signal that directs excitons — required, and when does a Markovian dephasing model suffice? A general framework for non-Markovian Hamiltonian dynamics of boson-mediated energy transfer has been developed for generic bosonic transport networks [8], and a first timescale-based applicability analysis has been sketched for light-harvesting complexes using representative rather than system-specific parameters [9]. What is missing, and what this paper provides, is a version of the test grounded in parameters that published FMO studies actually report, together with a sensitivity analysis showing whether the verdict survives the full published uncertainty ranges of those parameters.

The stakes of the test are concrete. If the Markovian reduction were quantitatively adequate, the non-Markovian framework would be unnecessary overhead for this application domain. If it is not — as our analysis concludes — the framework should predict observable corrections that Markovian models cannot produce: transient population backflow between sites, and a shift of the optimal noise level away from the Markovian value. Published evidence already hints in this direction from two independent sides: two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy of FMO shows oscillatory signals lasting $300$–$660\ \mathrm{fs}$ at physiological temperature, now attributed predominantly to vibronic coherence involving underdamped intramolecular modes in the $200$–$500\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ range [7]; and mixed quantum–classical simulations show that the quantum nature of high-frequency chromophore vibrations measurably slows energy transfer in LH2, FMO, and LHCII alike [3]. Both observations point to structured, slowly decorrelating vibrational environments — exactly the regime where memory effects should matter.

Our approach is deliberately minimal and involves no new simulations. We (i) set up a two-site Haken–Strobl-type model with parameters taken from published FMO dimer values, (ii) derive the optimal dephasing rate, hopping rate, transfer time, and single-hop efficiency with full arithmetic, (iii) compare the transfer time against coherence lifetimes reported in the spectroscopic literature to locate the system on a Markovian–non-Markian axis, and (iv) test the robustness of the resulting classification across the full published parameter ranges. The result is a concrete, falsifiable criterion for when the boson-mediated non-Markovian framework [8] re-enters light-harvesting physics.

The contributions of this paper are:

- A re-statement of the re-entry criterion as a memory ratio $R = \tau_B/\tau_T$ with explicit, falsifiable thresholds, with all model inputs grounded in published FMO dimer parameters rather than generic representatives (Sections 3 and 4).
- A full derivation, with every arithmetic step shown, of the optimal dephasing rate $\Gamma^* = \Delta = 150\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, the optimal hopping rate $k^* = 16.67\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, the transfer time $\tau_T = 0.3185\ \mathrm{ps}$, and the single-hop efficiency $\eta = 0.999001$ (Section 4).
- A memory-ratio analysis showing $R \in [0.63, 2.07]$ for FMO, using published coherence lifetimes as bath memory times, and a corner analysis over the full published ranges $V \in [50, 75]\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $\Delta \in [110, 180]\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ showing $R > 0.5$ everywhere in that box (Section 4).
- An explicit falsification protocol and a statement of the observable signature — a memory-scaled shift of the optimal dephasing rate — that would confirm or refute the re-entry verdict (Sections 5 and 6).

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Vibronic dimer parameters for FMO.** The dimer-level parameters used throughout this paper come from the vibronic exciton study of [1]. That work investigates a vibronic heterodimer with parameters chosen to mimic the FMO complex, and reports from the modeling literature a transition energy difference between BChl 3 and 4 of about $110$ to $180\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and an excitonic coupling between these two molecules of about $-50$ to $-75\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$; it further reports that fluorescence line narrowing spectroscopy finds vibrational modes near $180\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ in the lowest-energy FMO BChl with a total Huang–Rhys factor of $0.03$. Two aspects of [1] matter for our purposes. First, its parameter ranges define the physical box within which our re-entry verdict must hold, and we use them directly in Section 4. Second, [1] finds that for negative coupling of the FMO type, most oscillator strength resides in a transition of essentially electronic character, and that Huang–Rhys factors obtained from fluorescence spectroscopy of strongly coupled complexes need corrections that can reach $100\%$ — a caution that the vibronic side of the parameter set carries real uncertainty, which we propagate into our sensitivity analysis.

**System-scale context and timescales.** The atomistic study of energy funneling in green sulfur bacteria [2] frames the full biological chain — chlorosome, baseplate, FMO — in which the FMO dimer sits. [2] builds an atomistic model of $3988$ pigments covering the chlorosome (two double-wall rolls of BChl c), a $64$-site baseplate, and six FMO trimers ($144$ BChl a sites), and evolves exciton populations with a quantum master equation including coherent, dephasing, and relaxation processes. Two of its findings calibrate our timescales: the results are insensitive to the initial excitation scenario within roughly $500\ \mathrm{fs}$, and all scenarios converge within the overall $10\ \mathrm{ps}$ transfer timescale through the full apparatus. The $500\ \mathrm{fs}$ figure independently corroborates that sub-picosecond memory times are physically operative in this system, which is the range we use for $\tau_B$.

**Nuclear quantum effects.** [3] assesses how quantum-mechanical treatment of high-frequency chromophore vibrations affects energy transfer in LH2, FMO, and LHCII at $300\ \mathrm{K}$, using a mixed quantum–classical theory consistent with quantum–classical equilibrium. Its central result is that the difference between quantum and classical vibrational treatment is not the direction of energy flow but the rate: classical treatment overestimates the transfer rate, and a renormalization of the Hamiltonian by a first-order perturbative correction accounts for the effect, which for biologically relevant parameters is bounded by a factor of $2$. This matters for re-entry in two ways. It establishes that the high-frequency vibrational sector — the sector a boson-mediated non-Markovian framework is built to capture — has a demonstrable, bounded dynamical effect, and it supplies a benchmark magnitude (up to a factor of $2$ in rate) against which any non-Markovian correction predicted by [8] can be sanity-checked.

**Single-complex spectroscopy.** [4] demonstrates single-molecule pump–probe (SM2P) spectroscopy of LH2 complexes, resolving ultrafast energy relaxation in individual complexes with femtosecond resolution and showing that the observed relaxation time distributions can be qualitatively explained by the energetic disorder of the LH2 pigments at the levels commonly used in bulk spectroscopy modeling. For our purposes, [4] contributes two points: experimental confirmation that coherent evolution on a $100\ \mathrm{fs}$ timescale is observable in individual complexes, and a demonstration that disorder — one of our key model inputs — has a directly measurable spectroscopic signature at the single-complex level, which is the natural venue for testing the backflow signature our analysis predicts.

**Two-timescale taxonomy.** The methodological review of [5] organizes electronic energy transfer around two timescales: $\tau_{\mathrm{da}}$, the donor–acceptor transfer time, and $\tau_{\mathrm{vr}}$, the intramolecular vibrational relaxation time. This taxonomy is precisely the structure of our re-entry criterion, with $\tau_T$ playing the role of $\tau_{\mathrm{da}}$ and $\tau_B$ the role of a vibrational correlation time. [5] also reports that light-harvesting efficiencies are close to $100\%$ across antenna systems with interchromophore separations from about $1\ \mathrm{nm}$ (chlorophyll-based) to more than $2\ \mathrm{nm}$ (bilin-based), and it documents the Frenkel-exciton assumptions — no inter-pigment electron transfer, two-level pigments, weak inter-pigment electronic–nuclear coupling — under which our two-site model is a controlled approximation.

**Spectral densities as the data bottleneck.** [6] applies a time-dependent atomistic procedure to determine spectral densities for the CP29 complex, framing spectral densities as the key input functions for modeling excitation energy transfer dynamics and spectroscopic properties. Its decomposition of the experimental spectral density into a continuous low-frequency exciton–phonon part $J^{exp}_0(\omega)$ (one over-damped Brownian oscillator, representing protein and solvent coupling) and a high-frequency part $J^{exp}_{vib}(\omega)$ of discrete intramolecular vibrations — with $48$ vibrational frequencies extracted for chlorophyll molecules in the PSII system — is the practical realization of the fast/slow bath split our criterion assumes. [6] also notes that long-lived oscillations in light-harvesting spectroscopy likely originate from impulsively excited vibrations and are too short-lived to have functional significance, a caution we address in Section 6 when distinguishing memory effects from mere spectroscopic artifacts.

**Coherence lifetimes as memory times.** The review of quantum coherence in Photosystem II [7] supplies the empirical memory times we use for $\tau_B$. It reports that two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy of FMO consistently reveals oscillatory cross-peak signals lasting $300$–$660\ \mathrm{fs}$ at physiological temperatures, that underdamped intramolecular modes in the $200$–$500\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ range can produce such signatures, and that the current consensus attributes the dominant room-temperature observable to vibronic (mixed electronic–vibrational) coherence; it further reports coherence lifetimes of about $200\ \mathrm{fs}$ in LHCII at $293\ \mathrm{K}$. It also gives the thermal scale $k_B T \approx 200\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ at room temperature, against which we compare our site-energy gap in Section 4. These lifetimes are the most direct empirical estimates available for how long the vibrational environment retains phase information in exactly the complexes we model.

**The framework under test.** The boson-mediated framework itself is presented in [8], which proposes that bosonic fields (phonons) act as structured control signals directing fermionic excitons through the energy landscape, formalized as a non-Markovian Hamiltonian dynamics that goes beyond static Hilbert-space representations. [8] reports that its simulations confirm the phonon environment acts as a structured signal guiding excitons with efficiencies above $95\%$, and frames the "warm quantum paradox" — near-unity transfer efficiency at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ in complexes such as FMO — as the anomaly the framework resolves. Our paper does not re-derive [8]; it tests whether the regime where [8] is necessary is actually realized in FMO-scale systems, using independently published parameters.
