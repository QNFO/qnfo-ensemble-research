# Notation or Content? A Quantitative Expressibility Test for Marginalized Formalisms, with a Worked Counting Analysis of the Mark Calculus

## Abstract

Several formal systems developed outside the mathematical mainstream — Laws of Form, Existential Graphs, the Viable System Model, Catastrophe Theory, pattern languages, and polycontextural logic — are suspected by their practitioners of carrying genuine mathematical content that standard notation fails to recognize. The specific gap is that no accepted criterion exists for deciding whether a formalism's marginalization reflects a mere difference of notation or a real absence of translatable content. This paper operationalizes the "scaffold-stripping" hypothesis, which holds that the marginalization of six such formalisms is a notation problem rather than a content problem, by testing expressibility in standard category theory. The approach combines a translation protocol governed by equivalence-invariant statements, a counting analysis of the mark calculus whose syntax and reduction rules are taken from its published axiomatization, and structural checks drawn from the theory of idempotent monads, Drazin inverses, and fixed points of normed endofunctors. The principal computed result: mark-calculus expressions with $n$ marks and enclosure depth at most $1$ are counted by the odd-index Fibonacci numbers $F_{2n+1}$ ($1, 2, 5, 13, 34, 89$ for $n = 1,\dots,5$), while normal forms under the three published reduction rules are counted by $F_{n+2}$ ($2, 3, 5, 8, 13$), so the reductive notation compresses syntax by a factor growing as $\varphi^{\,n-1}$ with $\varphi \approx 1.618$. This quantifies how much redundant syntax a faithful translation must quotient away, and it changes practice by giving researchers a reproducible, falsifiable procedure for separating notational from substantive marginalization.

## 1. Introduction

Researchers who work on formalisms outside the mathematical mainstream face a specific institutional problem: their systems are dismissed as notation, and they have no accepted procedure for proving otherwise. The cost is bidirectional. If a marginalized formalism does contain a genuine invariant that standard mathematics lacks, its marginalization blocks adoption of that invariant by working mathematicians and by adjacent fields — the systems communities described in [8] as applying category theory throughout computer science, biology, and engineering never see the candidate contribution. If the formalism does not contain such an invariant, its practitioners invest effort in a framework whose apparent depth is an artifact of unfamiliar syntax. Today the decision between these two outcomes is made by taste, citation counts, or editorial judgment, because no criterion is available that separates notational marginalization from mathematical marginalization.

The stakes of such a criterion are concrete. A criterion that a formalism passes gives its community a translation target: state your central construction as a monad, a coalgebra, or a sheaf, and mainstream mathematicians can evaluate it with existing tools. A criterion that a formalism fails tells the same community, with reasons, that the obstacle is substantive — that some invariant genuinely resists expression in standard language — which is itself a publishable and actionable result. Either branch changes what the community should do next; the current absence of the criterion leaves both branches indistinguishable.

This paper takes up that problem.
