# The Energy Floor of Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing at the 1,000-Logical-Qubit Scale

**Abstract.** Two fundamental bounds bracket the energy cost of fault-tolerant quantum computation: the Margolus–Levitin (ML) quantum speed limit, which requires average energy above the ground state of at least E ≥ πħ/(2Δt) to complete an orthogonal state transition in time Δt, and the Landauer bound, which prices the erasure of one classical bit at k_B T ln 2. For a logical operation on a 1 μs clock, the ML floor is ≈1.66×10⁻²⁸ J — about one nanoelectronvolt — many orders of magnitude below any realizable cost. The Landauer floor applies to the classical control stack: a distance-27 surface-code round erases on the order of a thousand syndrome bits per logical qubit, giving a thermodynamic control floor of ≈10⁻¹⁹–10⁻²⁰ J per logical operation at 4 K. Neither bound is the binding constraint. Building a full-stack energy ledger for a surface-code-protected 1,000-logical-qubit superconducting processor with ≈2–10 million physical qubits, we estimate a wall power of order 1–10 MW, dominated by cryogenic cooling of control and readout wiring and by real-time decoding — not by the qubit manipulation itself — yielding an order-of-magnitude estimate of ≈0.5–20 mJ per logical operation (central ≈2 mJ), roughly 10⁷ times the energy of a modern classical floating-point operation. We propose the 2026 revision of the joules-per-compute (JPCUB) benchmark: the headline metric becomes energy per logical qubit-operation (J/LO) measured full-stack at the wall, reported with code distance, decoder type, and round rate; the physical-gate count is demoted to a secondary implementation-efficiency metric. A concrete falsifying observation is stated: fully integrated cryo-CMOS control that reduces per-line dissipation by two orders of magnitude would shift the dominant term from cryogenics to decoding and lower the central estimate by ≈100×.

**1. Introduction**

Fault-tolerant quantum computing has moved from threshold theorems to working hardware: a 105-qubit processor operating below the surface-code threshold was demonstrated in 2024 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check], and multiple vendor roadmaps target 100–10,000 logical qubits within the decade [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check]. Energy has been a second-order concern during this era because machines were small. At the 1,000-logical-qubit scale it becomes a first-order concern: the physical overhead of the surface code multiplies qubit count by (2d−1)², and cryogenic infrastructure does not scale for free. This paper asks three questions. (Q1) What do the Margolus–Levitin and Landauer bounds imply for the minimum energy per logical operation? (Q2) What is a defensible full-stack estimate of the energy per logical operation for a 1,000-logical-qubit surface-code processor, and where is the dominant term? (Q3) How should the joules-per-compute benchmark be revised in 2026 so that it normalizes energy per logical qubit rather than per physical gate?

The analysis is deliberately conservative: every machine-scale number is labeled as an order-of-magnitude estimate with stated assumptions, and every bibliographic entry carries an unverified marker because the authoring environment blocked live tool access to citation databases (see accompanying notes.md). Nothing here should be read as a claim that these estimates are measurements.

**2. The Margolus–Levitin bound and the Landauer floor**

The ML theorem (Margolus and Levitin, 1998 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check]) states that a quantum system with average energy E above its ground state requires a time of at least τ ≥ πħ/(2E) to evolve between two orthogonal states. Restated as a cost, any logical operation that must move a state to an orthogonal subspace within time Δt carries an average energy of at least

E_min = πħ/(2Δt) ≈ (1.66×10⁻³⁴ J·s) / Δt.

With ħ = 1.05457×10⁻³⁴ J·s (CODATA value; established), a 1 μs logical clock yields E_min ≈ 1.66×10⁻²⁸ J ≈ 1.0 neV per operation; even a 1 ns gate yields only ≈1.66×10⁻²⁵ J ≈ 1 μeV. These are extraordinarily small numbers: the coherent quantum layer is not where the energy goes.

The Landauer bound (Landauer, 1961 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check]) prices logically irreversible erasure of one classical bit at k_B T ln 2. With k_B = 1.38065×10⁻²³ J/K: 2.87×10⁻²¹ J at 300 K, 3.83×10⁻²³ J at 4 K, and 1.91×10⁻²⁵ J at 20 mK (all established arithmetic). Fault-tolerant quantum computation is thermodynamically classical at the control layer: each syndrome-extraction round measures and then discards an extensive number of classical bits. A distance-d surface code performs roughly 2d(d−1) stabilizer measurements per logical qubit per round — ≈1,400 bits for d = 27 — so the Landauer floor per logical operation is ≈1,400 × 3.83×10⁻²³ J ≈ 5.4×10⁻²⁰ J at 4 K, and ≈2.7×10⁻²² J at 20 mK. The combined floor per logical operation is therefore the larger of the ML coherent term and N_bits·k_B T ln 2; the classical term dominates by ≈10⁵ per erased bit and ≈10⁸ per full round at 4 K. Yet, as Section 4 shows, even this is ≈10¹⁶ below achievable engineering cost. Thermodynamics is not the binding constraint; Carnot-limited cooling and electronics are. This separation — a physical floor set by ML + Landauer, and a practical floor set by refrigeration and silicon — is the central organizing fact of this paper. For the general theory of quantum speed limits see Deffner and Campbell, 2017 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check].

**3. A surface-code machine at 1,000 logical qubits**

The rotated surface code of distance d allocates (2d−1)² physical qubits per logical qubit (Fowler et al., 2012 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check]; established formula). At d = 27 this is 2,809 physical qubits per logical qubit, or ≈2.8 million for 1,000 logical qubits; a full architecture adds routing qubits and magic-state distillation factories, typically 50–100% overhead (order-of-magnitude assumption), so the realistic physical count is ≈4–6 million, with a stated range of 2–10 million across d = 15–31 (order-of-magnitude estimate). Each logical operation is a network of physical gates plus syndrome extraction; the natural clock is the syndrome round, assumed here to be 0.5–2 μs (order-of-magnitude assumption, consistent with current single-qubit gate and measurement speeds, though the round time of a real multi-million-qubit machine is an open engineering question). Three infrastructure layers carry the energy: the qubit plane at 10–20 mK; control and readout electronics at 4 K and 300 K (DACs, ADCs, microwave chains, and increasingly cryo-CMOS); and a real-time decoder running on FPGAs, ASICs, or GPU/CPU clusters. The estimates in Section 4 assume this reference architecture; photonic-fusion and trapped-ion machines have different ledgers (Section 6).

**4. Energy accounting: where the joules go**

The ledger has four layers (all machine-scale figures are order-of-magnitude estimates with stated assumptions):

1. *Qubit layer.* Microwave drive and readout pulses reach the chip at pW–nW per qubit; dissipation at the millikelvin stage is negligible in the ledger, and the ML-floor coherent cost of Section 2 is another eleven orders of magnitude below even that.
2. *Control wiring.* Each control/readout line carries mW-scale signals from 300 K that are attenuated to nW at the chip; the attenuators dissipate ≈1–10 mW per line at the 4 K stage. Adopting 1–3 mW per line as the working assumption across 2–10 million lines gives a 4 K load of ≈2–30 kW (order-of-magnitude estimate).
3. *Cryogenics.* The Carnot coefficient of performance for lifting heat from 4 K to 300 K is T_c/(T_h−T_c) ≈ 1/74, so every watt dissipated at 4 K costs at least 74 W of wall power; practical dilution refrigerators operate at a fraction of Carnot, so the effective factor is ≈300–700 W/W (order-of-magnitude estimate). A 2–30 kW 4 K load therefore maps to ≈1–20 MW at the wall. The millikelvin stage adds little in absolute terms: its Carnot factor is ≈15,000 W/W, but it acts on a load of only milliwatts.
4. *Decoding.* Real-time surface-code decoding at a 1 μs round rate for millions of qubits is a known bottleneck; FPGA decoders consume on the order of 10–100 W per ~10⁴–10⁵ qubits of decode throughput, and CPU/GPU clusters scale to kW–MW (Das et al., 2020 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check]; order-of-magnitude). At the 1,000-logical-qubit scale the decoder plausibly contributes 10 kW–1 MW, comparable to — and in cryo-CMOS-multiplexed futures, larger than — the cryogenic term.

*Central result.* Combining layers 2–4, the wall power is P ≈ 1–10 MW for the reference architecture (order-of-magnitude estimate; the full cryo range reaches 20 MW under the most pessimistic wiring assumptions). The logical throughput is R = 1,000/Δt_round ∈ [0.5, 2]×10⁹ logical-qubit-operations per second, so the energy per logical operation is

J/LO = P/R ≈ 0.5–20 mJ, central ≈ 2 mJ (order-of-magnitude estimate).

The dominant term is cryogenic cooling of the control/readout wiring (layer 3 acting on layer 2), with decoding (layer 4) as the second term; the qubit layer itself is orders of magnitude below. This estimate is consistent in spirit with the resource-constraint analysis of superconducting architectures (Fellous-Asiani et al., 2021 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check]) and with the energy-agenda framing of Auffèves, 2022 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check], but is derived here independently from the stated assumptions. For scale: a modern 64-bit floating-point operation costs ≈10–100 pJ (order-of-magnitude), so J/LO is ≈10⁷–10⁸ times the classical per-op energy. That ratio is not the honest comparison — a logical qubit participates in a 2^N-dimensional computation — but it fixes the magnitude of the engineering gap the benchmark must track. The honest energy comparison is per problem instance (e.g., factoring a 2,048-bit integer), where existing analyses find a quantum energy advantage only in restricted regimes (Jaschke and Montangero, 2023 [UNVERIFIED - needs parent check]; open question for fault-tolerant machines).

**5. Revising joules-per-compute in 2026**

The current JPCUB normalization of energy per physical gate [UNVERIFIED - internal program] mis-measures fault-tolerant machines twice: it rewards architectures with fewer physical qubits per logical qubit even when their logical operations are slower, and it makes cross-architecture comparison (superconducting vs. photonic vs. trapped-ion vs. topological) meaningless, since a "physical gate" is not a shared currency. Proposed 2026 revision:

1. *Headline metric:* joules per logical operation (J/LO), measured full-stack at the wall — cryogenics, control, decoding, and classical orchestration included — with the wall-power measurement boundary stated explicitly.
2. *Normalization:* report J/LO per logical qubit (total throughput normalized by the logical-qubit count), never per physical gate.
3. *Context fields:* every J/LO figure is accompanied by code distance d, decoder type, syndrome-round time, and achieved error rate; without these, J/LO is uninterpretable.
4. *Secondary metrics:* physical overhead efficiency (physical gates per logical operation at distance d) and joules per syndrome round per logical qubit, which separately expose decoder and cooling overhead.
5. *Interim regime:* machines below the fault-tolerance threshold may report joules per physical gate as a provisional metric, explicitly flagged as non-comparable with J/LO.
6. *Floor tracking:* report the ML and Landauer floors for the stated clock and temperature alongside the measured J/LO, so the gap-to-floor — currently ≈10¹⁶ — is a visible, tracked quantity rather than an unstated embarrassment.

The revision carries its own falsification test: if improvements in decoders or cryo-CMOS move J/LO while physical-gate-normalized metrics stay flat, logical normalization is measuring the right thing; if J/LO is flat while physical metrics move, the physical-gate metric still carries information and both should be reported.

**6. Limitations and falsifying observations**

The central estimate would be falsified or displaced by any of the following. (a) Fully integrated cryo-CMOS control — multiplexing thousands of qubits onto a few microwave lines at μW-scale per-line dissipation — would cut the 4 K load by two orders of magnitude, shift the dominant term from cryogenics to decoding, and lower J/LO by ≈100×; this is an active research direction, and if it succeeds the central 2 mJ is far too pessimistic. (b) Any architecture that avoids millikelvin operation and dense analog wiring entirely (photonic fusion, room-temperature topological qubits) escapes the Carnot argument; the estimate applies only to superconducting surface-code machines. (c) The ML bound governs physical gates, not logical operations; a protocol-dependent speed limit for fault-tolerant logical operations remains an open question. (d) The 0.5–2 μs round-time assumption is the single most load-bearing number in the estimate; if real rounds run at 10 μs, J/LO scales by ≈5–20× at fixed wall power. (e) All bibliographic entries are unverified at the time of writing (tool access blocked); a parent-process verification pass may correct or remove several.

**7. Conclusion**

At the 1,000-logical-qubit scale, the energy floor of fault-tolerant quantum computing has two stories. The thermodynamic story (ML + Landauer) is essentially free: ≈10⁻²⁸–10⁻¹⁹ J per logical operation. The engineering story (Carnot-limited cryogenics on mW-scale control lines, plus real-time decoding) is expensive: ≈0.5–20 mJ per logical operation — roughly 10¹⁶ times the Landauer control floor at 4 K and 10²⁵ times the Margolus–Levitin coherent floor — dominated by cooling and classical processing rather than by quantum physics. The 2026 joules-per-compute revision should measure the engineering story honestly — joules per logical operation, full-stack, per logical qubit, with distance, decoder, and round context — while tracking the gap to the physical floor as a first-class metric. The gap itself is the roadmap: every order of magnitude closed between 2 mJ and 10⁻¹⁹ J is an order of magnitude of fault-tolerant computing that becomes economically deployable.
