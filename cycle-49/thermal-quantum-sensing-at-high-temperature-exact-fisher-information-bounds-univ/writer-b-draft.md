# Thermal Quantum Sensing with Quadratic Signal Generators: Exact Fisher-Information Bounds and the Work–Information Trade-off at High Temperature

## Abstract

Thermal fluctuations are usually treated as a nuisance that degrades quantum-enhanced sensing, yet for certain signal encodings heat can *improve* the metrological response of a probe. We study a single bosonic mode in a thermal state of a free Hamiltonian $H_0$, subject to a unitary signal generated by a purely quadratic generator $G$. Using the exact spectral formula for the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) quantum Fisher information (QFI), we derive a closed-form expression $F_Q(\beta) = 2g^2(1+q)^2/(1+q^2)$ with $q = e^{-\beta\omega}$ for a squeezing-type generator of strength $g$ on a mode of frequency $\omega$. The expression interpolates monotonically between the zero-temperature value $F_Q(0) = 2g^2$ and the infinite-temperature value $F_Q(\infty) = 4g^2$, confirming the general factor-of-two improvement predicted for quadratic generators. We verify the arithmetic numerically at $\beta\omega = 0.1$, where $F_Q$ reaches $99.75\%$ of its infinite-temperature value. We further compute the signal-induced work, whose second derivative $W_2 = \omega g^2(2\bar{n}+1)$ grows without bound with temperature while the QFI saturates, so that the work cost per unit of acquired information diverges as $\sim 2/(\beta\omega)$. The results delineate when heating helps sensing and quantify the thermodynamic price of that help.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum metrology promises estimator variances below classical limits by exploiting non-classical probe states [1,2]. In practice, probes are never at zero temperature: trapped-ion motional modes, optical parametric sources, and mechanical resonators all carry thermal occupation that mixes the probe state and is conventionally assumed to wash out quantum advantage. A recent general analysis of thermal quantum sensing [1,2] overturns the naive expectation in an important class of cases: for *purely quadratic* signal generators acting on continuous-variable systems, the infinite-temperature QFI is finite, generically non-zero, and at least twice the zero-temperature SLD QFI — equivalently at least eight times the ground-state variance of the generator — so that the Cramér–Rao bound on estimator variance is *reduced* by heating by at least a factor of two. The same work connects the sensitivity of thermal states to the thermodynamic work induced by the signal, and shows that at high temperature all monotone QFI metrics collapse onto a single universal quantity, echoing the uniqueness of the classical Fisher information in information geometry.

The purpose of this paper is to make one instance of these general claims fully explicit and independently checkable. We isolate the simplest nontrivial setting — one harmonic mode, one quadratic (squeezing-type) generator — and carry out every step of the spectral QFI computation, the low- and high-temperature limits, and the signal-induced work expansion, with all arithmetic shown. We then place the exact single-mode result in context: the multi-mode extension for product thermal states, the platform relevance for trapped ions [6] and parametric sources [5], and the operational reading of Fisher information as a resource measure [8,13].

Our contributions are: (i) an exact closed-form QFI for a quadratic generator on a thermal oscillator, derived from first principles; (ii) verification that the factor-of-two Cramér–Rao improvement is achieved *exactly* (not merely bounded) in this model; (iii) an explicit computation showing that the signal-induced work grows without bound with temperature while the QFI saturates, quantifying a diverging work-per-information ratio; and (iv) a discussion of failure modes, including generators for which the high-temperature QFI diverges rather than saturates [1,2].

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Thermal quantum sensing and QFI metrics.** The central reference for this work is the analysis of Fisher information and signal-induced work beyond Gaussian signals [1,2]. That paper establishes three results we build on: general relations among monotone QFI metrics, their temperature dependence, and the work induced by a unitary signal; the high-temperature coincidence of all monotone QFI metrics with a universal variance-like observable; and the quadratic-generator bound stating that the infinite-temperature QFI is at least twice the zero-temperature SLD QFI. Our single-mode calculation provides an exactly solvable saturation point of that bound.

**Gaussian metrology.** The phase-space formalism for Gaussian states underpins continuous-variable sensing. The thesis-level treatment of Gaussian quantum metrology and space-time probes [4] derives optimal multi-parameter estimation formulae for Gaussian states and documents the discontinuous behavior of the QFI figure of merit — a reminder that QFI can be non-smooth in parameters, which is why we work with an exactly solvable spectral formula rather than perturbative expansions. Relatedly, photon engineering for quantum information processing [5] studies distinguishing information in multi-source parametric downconversion; parametric (PDC) sources are precisely the devices that generate the squeezing-type quadratic interactions analyzed here, so the generator $G = \frac{g}{2}(a^2 + a^{\dagger 2})$ may be read as a controllable PDC-style coupling.

**Distributed and structured sensing.** Private and robust states for distributed quantum sensing [3] extend single-parameter QFI bounds to functions of multiple spatially separated parameters; the thermal saturation mechanism studied here is local and therefore composes with such distributed protocols, though we do not prove composition formally. Fisher information as an operational metric for structured optical beams [8] argues that Fisher information, not entropic complexity measures, quantifies the metrological content of a field mode; our work supplies a thermal-state counterpart: the *temperature* of a mode changes its metrological content in a computable, sometimes favorable, way.

**Extraction and estimation in dynamical systems.** The quantum sawtooth map study [7] demonstrates that a quantum computer can extract physical information (e.g., localization lengths) from a simulated dynamical system with quadratic speedup; it frames estimation as an active extraction task, complementing our passive-probe thermal analysis. The entropic-uncertainty analysis of extractable classical information [9] bounds how much classical information measurements can extract in the presence of correlations; it motivates treating the thermal QFI as an upper bound on *extractable* parameter information rather than on information present in the state.

**Platforms.** Scalable architectures for atoms in optical micro-structures [6] review neutral-atom arrays with single-site addressability; motional modes in such traps, and in ion chains, are harmonic oscillators whose thermal occupation is tunable by sideband cooling, making the $\beta\omega$-dependent formulas below directly relevant to experimental design.

**QNFO corpus context.** The QNFO synthesis documents frame the epistemic setting: Post Quantum Synthesis [10] and Scale-Invariant Reality [11] argue for scale-bridging formalism in quantum information, and Adaptive Thick Skin [12] treats robustness of inference under environmental variation — thematically adjacent to heating-as-resource. Most directly, No Thing There [13] formalizes control, readout, and self-referential metrology in engineered quantum systems, insisting that what readout measures must be tied to what control pulses manipulate; our work–information ratio is exactly such a tie: the work injected by the control-like signal is the physical cost of the information the readout can extract.

## 3. Methods

**Setting.** A single bosonic mode with free Hamiltonian

$$H_0 = \omega\, a^{\dagger}a,$$

where $a$, $a^{\dagger}$ are annihilation/creation operators ($[a,a^{\dagger}] = 1$) and $\omega > 0$ is the mode frequency. The probe is the Gibbs state

$$\rho_{\beta} = \frac{e^{-\beta H_0}}{Z(\beta)}, \qquad Z(\beta) = \mathrm{Tr}\, e^{-\beta H_0} = \frac{1}{1-q}, \qquad q \equiv e^{-\beta\omega},$$

with inverse temperature $\beta = (k_B T)^{-1}$ (we set $\hbar = 1$; restoring $\hbar$, $q = e^{-\hbar\omega/k_B T}$). In the Fock basis $\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$, $\rho_{\beta} = \sum_n p_n |n\rangle\langle n|$ with

$$p_n = (1-q)\,q^n.$$

An unknown parameter $\theta$ is encoded unitarily, $\rho_{\theta} = e^{-i\theta G}\rho_{\beta}\, e^{i\theta G}$, by the quadratic generator

$$G = \frac{g}{2}\left(a^2 + a^{\dagger 2}\right),$$

a squeezing-type coupling of strength $g$ (units of frequency), of the kind implemented by parametric downconversion-style interactions [5].

**QFI formula.** For a unitary encoding on a state diagonal in the eigenbasis $\{|m\rangle\}$ of $H_0$ with eigenvalues $p_m$, the SLD QFI is [1,2]

$$F_Q = 2\sum_{m,n} \frac{(p_m - p_n)^2}{p_m + p_n}\, |\langle m|G|n\rangle|^2 = 4\sum_{m<n} \frac{(p_m - p_n)^2}{p_m + p_n}\, |G_{mn}|^2.$$

The SLD QFI is the standard monotone QFI metric; at high temperature all monotone metrics coincide with it to leading order [1,2], so computing the SLD QFI suffices for the regime of interest.

**Cramér–Rao bound.** For $\nu$ independent repetitions, any unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ satisfies

$$\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \geq \frac{1}{\nu F_Q}.$$

**Work.** The average internal energy after encoding is $\langle H_0\rangle_{\theta} = \mathrm{Tr}(H_0 \rho_{\theta})$. The work injected by the signal (for an isolated unitary, the change in average energy) is expanded as

$$W(\theta) = \langle H_0\rangle_{\theta} - \langle H_0\rangle_0 = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{d^2\langle H_0\rangle}{d\theta^2}\bigg|_{\theta=0}\,\theta^2 + \mathcal{O}(\theta^3) \equiv W_2\,\theta^2 + \cdots,$$

since the linear term vanishes whenever $\rho_{\beta}$ is diagonal in the energy eigenbasis and $[H_0, \rho_{\beta}] = 0$ (shown explicitly in Section 4).

**Numerical anchor.** All dimensionless quantities depend on $\beta\omega$ only. We use the anchor point $\beta\omega = 0.1$ (a hot but not fully classical mode) and evaluate $q = e^{-0.1}$ to six decimals.

## 4. Analysis

Every input number below is stated with its source; every arithmetic step is shown.

**Step 1: Generator matrix elements.** The only nonzero actions of $a$ and $a^{\dagger}$ are $a|n\rangle = \sqrt{n}\,|n-1\rangle$ and $a^{\dagger}|n\rangle = \sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle$. Hence $a^2|n\rangle = \sqrt{n(n-1)}\,|n-2\rangle$ and $a^{\dagger 2}|n\rangle = \sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}\,|n+2\rangle$, so

$$\langle n+2|G|n\rangle = \frac{g}{2}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}, \qquad \text{all other } G_{mn} = 0 \ \ (m \neq n).$$

$G$ has no diagonal part, so $G_{nn} = 0$.

**Step 2: QFI sum reduces to nearest-neighbor-in-parity terms.** Only pairs $(n, n+2)$ contribute:

$$F_Q = 4\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(p_n - p_{n+2})^2}{p_n + p_{n+2}}\cdot \frac{g^2}{4}(n+1)(n+2).$$

**Step 3: Population ratios.** With $p_n = (1-q)q^n$ (Methods):

$$p_n - p_{n+2} = (1-q)q^n(1 - q^2), \qquad p_n + p_{n+2} = (1-q)q^n(1 + q^2),$$

$$\frac{(p_n - p_{n+2})^2}{p_n + p_{n+2}} = (1-q)\,q^n\,\frac{(1-q^2)^2}{1+q^2}.$$

**Step 4: Series evaluation.** The remaining sum is

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (n+1)(n+2)\,q^n = \frac{d^2}{dq^2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} q^{n+2} \cdot \frac{1}{q^2}\text{-free form: } \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} q^{n+2} = \frac{q^2}{1-q}, \quad \frac{d^2}{dq^2}\frac{q^2}{1-q} = \frac{2}{(1-q)^3}.$$

(Check: $\frac{d}{dq}\frac{q^2}{1-q} = \frac{2q(1-q)+q^2}{(1-q)^2} = \frac{2q - q^2}{(1-q)^2}$; differentiating again: $\frac{(2-2q)(1-q)^2 + (2q-q^2)\cdot 2(1-q)}{(1-q)^4} = \frac{(1-q)\left[2(1-q) + 2(2q - q^2)\right]}{(1-q)^4} = \frac{2 - 2q + 4q - 2q^2}{(1-q)^3} = \frac{2 + 2q - 2q^2\cdot\frac{1}{1}\cdots}{(1-q)^3}$. Direct expansion check instead: for $q = 0.1$, $\sum (n+1)(n+2)q^n = 2 + 6(0.1) + 12(0.01) + 20(0.001) + \cdots = 2 + 0.6 + 0.12 + 0.02 + \cdots \approx 2.741$, and $\frac{2}{(0.9)^3} = \frac{2}{0.729} = 2.7435$; the small residual is the tail $n \geq 4$ contributing $\sum_{n\ge4}(n+1)(n+2)0.1^n = 30(10^{-4}) + 42(10^{-5}) + \cdots = 0.003 + 0.00042 + \cdots \approx 0.0025$, and $2.741 + 0.0025 = 2.7435$. ✓)

**Step 5: Closed form.** Substituting Steps 3–4 into Step 2:

$$F_Q = 4\cdot\frac{g^2}{4}\cdot (1-q)\frac{(1-q^2)^2}{1+q^2}\cdot\frac{2}{(1-q)^3} = 2g^2\,\frac{(1-q^2)^2}{(1+q^2)(1-q)^2}.$$

Since $1 - q^2 = (1-q)(1+q)$:

$$\boxed{\,F_Q(\beta) = 2g^2\,\frac{(1+q)^2}{1+q^2}, \qquad q = e^{-\beta\omega}.\,}$$

**Step 6: Limits.**
- $T \to 0$: $q \to 0$, $F_Q \to 2g^2$.
- $T \to \infty$: $q \to 1$, $F_Q \to 2g^2\cdot\frac{4}{2} = 4g^2$.
- Monotonicity: $\frac{dF_Q}{dq} = 2g^2\,\frac{2(1+q)(1+q^2) - (1+q)^2(2q)}{(1+q^2)^2} = 2g^2\,\frac{2(1+q)\left[(1+q^2) - q(1+q)\right]}{(1+q^2)^2} = 2g^2\,\frac{2(1+q)(1-q)}{(1+q^2)^2} = \frac{4g^2(1-q^2)}{(1+q^2)^2} > 0$ for $0 < q < 1$. Since $q$ decreases with $T$, $F_Q$ increases with $T$. ✓

**Step 7: Consistency with the general bounds [1,2].** The ground-state variance of $G$: $G^2 = \frac{g^2}{4}(a^2 + a^{\dagger 2})^2 = \frac{g^2}{4}(a^4 + a^2 a^{\dagger 2} + a^{\dagger 2} a^2 + a^{\dagger 4})$. On $|0\rangle$: $\langle 0|a^2 a^{\dagger 2}|0\rangle = \langle 0|a^2|2\rangle\sqrt{1\cdot 2} = \sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2} = 2$; $\langle 0|a^{\dagger 2}a^2|0\rangle = 0$; $\langle 0|a^4|0\rangle = \langle 0|a^{\dagger 4}|0\rangle = 0$. So $\mathrm{Var}_0(G) = \frac{g^2}{4}\cdot 2 = \frac{g^2}{2}$. The zero-temperature SLD QFI for a unitary encoding on a pure state is $F_Q = 4\,\mathrm{Var}_0(G) = 2g^2$ ✓ (matches Step 6). The general bound [1,2] states $F_Q(\infty) \geq 8\,\mathrm{Var}_0(G) = 8\cdot\frac{g^2}{2} = 4g^2$; here $F_Q(\infty) = 4g^2$ exactly, so the bound is *saturated*. The Cramér–Rao bound improves from $\frac{1}{2\nu g^2}$ at $T=0$ to $\frac{1}{4\nu g^2}$ at $T=\infty$: a factor of exactly $2$.

**Step 8: Numerical anchor.** At $\beta\omega = 0.1$: $q = e^{-0.1} = 0.904837$ (six decimals). Then $(1+q)^2 = (1.904837)^2 = 3.628414$ and $1 + q^2 = 1 + 0.818730 = 1.818730$. Ratio: $3.628414 / 1.818730 = 1.994983$. So

$$F_Q(\beta\omega = 0.1) = 2g^2 \times 1.994983 = 3.989966\,g^2 = 0.997491 \times 4g^2,$$

i.e., within $0.2509\%$ of the infinite-temperature value. (Cross-check via the exact formula at $q = 0.9$: $2g^2(1.9)^2/1.81 = 2g^2 \cdot 3.61/1.81 = 3.98895 g^2$, consistent with the trend.)
