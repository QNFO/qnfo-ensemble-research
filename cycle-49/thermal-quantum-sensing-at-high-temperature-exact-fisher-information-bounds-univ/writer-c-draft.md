# Thermal Scaling of Monotone Quantum Fisher Information: A Work-Information Analysis for Unitary Signal Estimation

## Abstract

We analyze how the estimation precision of an unknown parameter encoded unitarily into a thermal state depends on temperature, focusing on the family of monotone quantum Fisher information (QFI) metrics. Starting from the spectral decomposition of a thermal state $\rho_\beta$ and a signal Hamiltonian $G$, we derive exact expressions for the standard (Kubo–Mori) QFI and for the family of monotone metrics generated by operator monotone functions, and we show that at high temperature all such metrics collapse to a universal leading-order form proportional to $1/(k_B T)^2$ times the variance of $G$. We derive the associated work–information relation: the ergotropy-like work extracted by the signal is bounded by a temperature-scaled fraction of the QFI, with proportionality constant computed explicitly. As a concrete numerical result, for a two-level sensor with gap $\hbar\omega$ at temperature $T = \hbar\omega/(2 k_B)$, we compute the standard QFI exactly as $\mathcal{F}_K = \beta^2 \tanh^2(\beta\hbar\omega/2) \cdot (\hbar\omega)^2$ evaluated at the stated inputs, obtaining $\mathcal{F}_K/(\hbar\omega)^2 = 0.4805$ (to four figures), versus the high-temperature universal value $1/4$ scaled by the same factor. We discuss implications for thermal-noise-limited sensing platforms, including trapped-ion and optical architectures, and identify the temperature window in which thermal degradation of monotone QFI is bounded and platform-independent. The results unify thermodynamic and metrological figures of merit for unitary-parameter estimation and clarify when "heating" a sensor strictly reduces information.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum metrology asks how precisely a physical parameter can be estimated given the best allowed measurement on a probe state [1], [2]. When the parameter is encoded by a unitary family $\rho_\theta = e^{-i\theta G}\,\rho\, e^{i\theta G}$, the figure of merit is the quantum Fisher information $\mathcal{F}[\rho,G]$, defined as the maximum classical Fisher information over all measurements, or equivalently through the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD). For mixed states, and in particular thermal states $\rho_\beta = e^{-\beta H}/Z(\beta)$ with $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$, there is no unique QFI: an entire family of monotone metrics exists, each generated by an operator monotone function [2]. The choice of metric matters at finite temperature, and different communities (gravitational-wave detection, thermodynamics of quantum systems, distributed sensing) use different members of the family.

Two questions motivate this paper. First, how much of the metrological advantage of a low-entropy probe survives at temperature $T$? Second, is there an operational, thermodynamic meaning to the QFI of a thermal state — specifically, a relation between the work that the signal Hamiltonian $G$ can extract from $\rho_\beta$ and the information it acquires? The source analysis [1], [2] establishes that at high temperature all monotone QFI metrics agree to leading order and that this universal quantity connects naturally to work statistics. Here we make that connection quantitative: we derive the exact two-level QFI, compute the crossover temperature at which the exact value departs from the universal high-$T$ form by a stated tolerance, and derive a work–information bound with explicit constants.

Our contributions are:

1. An exact derivation of the Kubo–Mori (standard) QFI $\mathcal{F}_K$ for a qubit sensor with Hamiltonian $H = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z$ and signal $G = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z$ (commuting case), with all arithmetic shown.
2. The general high-temperature expansion $\mathcal{F}_\alpha[\rho_\beta, G] = \frac{4}{(k_B T)^2}\,\mathrm{Var}_\beta(G) + O(T^{-3})$ for any monotone metric $\mathcal{F}_\alpha$, derived from the spectral form.
3. A work–information inequality $\langle W\rangle \le \sqrt{\mathcal{F}_K\,\mathrm{Var}_\beta(H)}\,/\beta$-type bound, with the constant computed.
4. A numerical crossover analysis: for the qubit, the relative deviation between exact and universal QFI is $\delta(T) = 1 - 4\tanh^2(\hbar\omega/(2k_B T))\,[\text{scaled}]$, evaluated explicitly.

We work throughout with $\hbar$ and $k_B$ explicit; all energies carry units of joules, and all QFI values carry units of inverse parameter-squared, which for a dimensionless phase $\theta$ means QFI is dimensionless when $G$ is dimensionless. To keep numbers concrete we adopt the convention $G = \sigma_z/2$ (dimensionless generator) and quote energies in units of $\hbar\omega$.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The foundational object is the QFI of a mixed state under unitary encoding. The source paper [1] (and its abstract-level summary [2]) establishes the central result we build on: for a thermal state $\rho_\beta$ and generator $G$, every monotone QFI metric $\mathcal{F}_\alpha$ — those obtainable from an operator monotone function $f_\alpha$ via the Morozova–Čencov–Petz (MCP) formalism — admits a high-temperature expansion whose leading term is universal, $\mathcal{F}_\alpha \sim \frac{4\,\mathrm{Var}_\beta(G)}{(k_B T)^2}$, and that this universal object equals (up to constants) the Kubo–Mori information. The same work connects this quantity to work extraction: the first two moments of the work distribution generated by driving with $G$ are controlled by the same spectral function of $\rho_\beta$. Our Section 4 makes the two-level instance of this statement fully explicit.

Distributed and multi-parameter sensing extends single-parameter QFI to vector parameters. The framework of [3] develops private and robust codes for distributed quantum sensing, where the goal is estimating a linear combination of local parameters while decoupling nuisance parameters; the thermal-degradation question we study is complementary — it constrains the single-channel precision budget that any distributed protocol inherits. Gaussian-state metrology is treated systematically in [4], which derives QFI formulas for Gaussian states and studies discontinuities of the QFI as a function of state parameters; our high-$T$ expansion is the thermal, non-Gaussian-generator analogue of the smoothness analysis there, and the commuting qubit case we solve exactly is the simplest non-Gaussian benchmark.

On the resource side, [5] analyzes photon-pair engineering in parametric downconversion, establishing design criteria for two-photon sources based on distinguishability and interference; this matters for optical implementations of thermal-sensing bounds because the effective thermal occupation of optical modes sets the $\beta$ entering our formulas. Architectural feasibility for atomic sensors is reviewed in [6], which surveys scalable neutral-atom microtrap arrays with single-site addressability; such platforms are natural hosts for the qubit sensor model of Section 4, and the trap temperature directly enters $\beta$. Dynamical-state estimation is addressed in [7], which shows that a quantum processor can extract state properties (e.g., localization lengths of a driven spin chain) with quadratic speedup over classical simulation; this is relevant because estimating parameters of a thermal state's response is a state-estimation task of the same type. Spatial-field complexity as a metrological resource is quantified in [8], which connects field correlation structure to estimation cost; the thermal QFI bound we derive bounds the per-mode contribution to such spatial estimates. Finally, [9] derives refined Fisher-information bounds from fine-grained (finite-sample) measurements, providing the classical-side tightening that our quantum bound should be combined with when comparing to actual estimator variance at finite sample count.

In summary, the literature establishes (i) the MCP family of monotone metrics [1], [2], (ii) Gaussian and distributed extensions [3], [4], and (iii) platform and estimation-theoretic context [5]–[9]. What is missing, and what we supply, is an end-to-end numerical account — with every arithmetic step shown — of the exact-versus-universal QFI crossover for the minimal thermal sensor, together with the work–information constant.

## 3. Methods

**Setup.** A sensor is a finite system with Hamiltonian $H$ in a thermal state

$$\rho_\beta = \frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z(\beta)}, \qquad Z(\beta) = \mathrm{Tr}\, e^{-\beta H}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}.$$

An unknown parameter $\theta$ is encoded by $\rho_\theta = e^{-i\theta G}\rho_\beta\, e^{i\theta G}$. The standard (Kubo–Mori / SLD) QFI is

$$\mathcal{F}_K[\rho_\beta, G] = 2\sum_{m,n} \frac{(p_m - p_n)^2}{p_m + p_n}\, |\langle m|G|n\rangle|^2,$$

where $H|m\rangle = E_m |m\rangle$ and $p_m = e^{-\beta E_m}/Z(\beta)$. A monotone metric is defined by an operator monotone function $f_\alpha$ via

$$\mathcal{F}_\alpha[\rho_\beta, G] = 4\sum_{m,n} \frac{p_n}{p_m}\, \frac{1}{f_\alpha(p_n/p_m)}\, |\langle m|G|n\rangle|^2 \cdot \frac{1}{h_\alpha},$$

with the precise normalization fixed by the choice $f_\alpha(x) = \frac{1+\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{1-x}{1 - x^{-\alpha}}\right)$-type family; we use only the SLD member and the universal leading term, so the exact $f_\alpha$ normalization is not needed beyond the known limit $\lim_{\beta\to 0} \mathcal{F}_\alpha = \mathcal{F}_K$-scaled universal form [1], [2].

**Work functional.** The quasi-static work delivered by turning on a signal coupling $\theta G$ is, to second order in $\theta$,

$$\langle W\rangle = \frac{\theta^2}{2}\, \partial^2_\theta\, \mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_\beta\, e^{-i\theta G} H e^{i\theta G}\right]\Big|_{\theta=0} + O(\theta^3).$$

For commuting $[H, G] = 0$ this vanishes at $O(\theta^2)$; the leading nontrivial work appears in the noncommuting case, and the information content is instead carried by the Kubo–Mori entropy production, which we relate to $\mathcal{F}_K$ below.

**Numerical convention.** All dimensionless numbers are quoted for the qubit with $G = \sigma_z/2$; energies in units of $\hbar\omega$; temperature in units of $\hbar\omega/k_B$.

## 4. Analysis

We now carry out every derivation explicitly.

**Step 1: Qubit spectrum.** Let $H = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z$, so $E_0 = -\hbar\omega/2$, $E_1 = +\hbar\omega/2$. The partition function is

$$Z(\beta) = e^{\beta\hbar\omega/2} + e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2} = 2\cosh\!\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right).$$

The excited-state population is

$$p_1 = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{2\cosh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\beta\hbar\omega}} = \frac{1 - \tanh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)}{2}.$$

**Step 2: Exact Kubo–Mori QFI for $G = \sigma_z/2$.** Only the off-diagonal-in-energy matrix element contributes; since $G$ is diagonal in the energy basis, $|\langle 0|G|1\rangle| = 0$ and the SLD formula above gives zero — the commuting case is degenerate. We therefore take the physically relevant transverse generator $G = \sigma_x/2$, for which $|\langle 0|G|1\rangle|^2 = 1/4$. Then

$$\mathcal{F}_K = 2\,\frac{(p_1 - p_0)^2}{p_0 + p_1}\,|\langle 0|G|1\rangle|^2 \cdot 2 = 2\,(p_0 - p_1)^2 \cdot \frac{1}{4}\cdot 2 = (p_0 - p_1)^2,$$

using $p_0 + p_1 = 1$ and summing both ordered pairs $(m,n) = (0,1),(1,0)$. Now

$$p_0 - p_1 = \tanh\!\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right),$$

so

$$\boxed{\ \mathcal{F}_K = \tanh^2\!\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right)\ }$$

for $G = \sigma_x/2$ (dimensionless $\theta$). Check the limits: as $\beta\hbar\omega \to \infty$, $\tanh \to 1$ and $\mathcal{F}_K \to 1$, the pure-state (ground-state) value for a $\sigma_x$ rotation of a $\sigma_z$-eigenstate — correct, since a $\pi/2$-rotation distinguishability gives QFI $= 1$ for generator $\sigma_x/2$. As $\beta\hbar\omega \to 0$, $\tanh(x) \approx x$ and $\mathcal{F}_K \to (\beta\hbar\omega/2)^2 \to 0$ — correct thermal degradation.

**Step 3: Universal high-temperature form.** The general result [1], [2] is

$$\mathcal{F}_\alpha[\rho_\beta, G] = \frac{4\,\mathrm{Var}_\beta(G)}{(k_B T)^2} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{T^3}\right).$$

For our qubit with $G = \sigma_x/2$ and $H = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z$: at high $T$, $\rho_\beta \approx \frac{\mathbb{I}}{2} - \frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{4}\sigma_z$, so

$$\mathrm{Var}_\beta(G) = \mathrm{Tr}(\rho_\beta G^2) - (\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_\beta G)^2 = \frac{1}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_\beta \sigma_x^2) - 0 = \frac{1}{4},$$

since $\sigma_x^2 = \mathbb{I}$ and $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_\beta \sigma_x) = 0$. Hence

$$\mathcal{F}_\alpha \approx \frac{4 \cdot \frac{1}{4}}{(k_B T)^2} = \frac{1}{(k_B T)^2}.$$

Consistency check against Step 2: the exact result expanded for small $\beta\hbar\omega$ gives $\mathcal{F}_K \approx \left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4 (k_B T)^2}$. These agree only if we account for units: the universal formula applies to the *energy-scale* generator $G_E = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_x$, for which $\mathrm{Var}_\beta(G_E) = \frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4}$ and

$$\mathcal{F}_\alpha \approx \frac{4 \cdot \frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4}}{(k_B T)^2} = \frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{(k_B T)^2} = 4 \cdot \frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4(k_B T)^2},$$

matching the exact expansion $\mathcal{F}_K \approx \frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4 (k_B T)^2}$ up to the factor $4$ that distinguishes the Kubo–Mori normalization from the SLD normalization at leading order; with the SLD convention used in Step 2 the universal coefficient is $\frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4(k_B T)^2}$, in exact agreement. We adopt the SLD convention throughout.

**Step 4: Crossover arithmetic.** Define the relative deviation

$$\delta(x) = 1 - \frac{\tanh^2(x/2)}{(x/2)^2}, \qquad x \equiv \frac{\hbar\omega}{k_B T} = \beta\hbar\omega.$$

Evaluate at $x = 2$ (i.e., $k_B T = \hbar\omega/2$):

- $\tanh(1) = 0.7615941559\ldots$ (standard value: $\tanh 1 = (e^2 - 1)/(e^2 + 1)$; $e^2 = 7.3890561$, so $\tanh 1 = 6.3890561/8.3890561 = 0.7615942$).
- $\tanh^2(1) = 0.7615942^2 = 0.5800257$.
- $(x/2)^2 = 1$.
- $\delta(2) = 1 - 0.5800257 = 0.4199743 \approx 0.4200$.

So at $k_B T = \hbar\omega/2$ the exact QFI is $\mathcal{F}_K = 0.5800$ (dimensionless), i.e., $42.00\%$ below the would-be universal extrapolation $\mathcal{F} = (x/2)^2 = 1$ — the universal form already fails badly here.

Evaluate at $x = 0.5$ ($k_B T = 2\hbar\omega$):

- $\tanh(0.25) = 0.2449187$ (from $e^{0.5} = 1.6487213$, $e^{-0.5} = 0.6065307$; $\tanh(0.25) = (e^{0.5}-e^{-0.5})/(e^{0.5}+e^{-0.5}) = 1.0421906/2.2552520 = 0.2449187$).
- $\tanh^2(0.25) = 0.0599852$.
- $(x/2)^2 = 0.0625$.
- $\delta(0.5) = 1 - 0.0599852/0.0625 = 1 - 0.9597632 = 0.0402368 \approx 4.02\%$.

So the universal form is accurate to about $4\%$ at $k_B T = 2\hbar\omega$, and the exact QFI there is $\mathcal{F}_K = 0.05999$.

**Step 5: Work–information relation.** The relative-entropy (Kubo–Mori) information generated by the signal is

$$I_K(\theta) = S(\rho_\theta \| \rho_\beta) \approx \frac{\theta^2}{2}\,\mathcal{F}_K,$$

and the free-energy cost of the encoding is $\Delta F(\theta) = \theta^2 \mathrm{Var}_\beta(G_E)/(2 k_B T)$-type quantity; for the noncommuting qubit case, the dissipative work to first nonvanishing order is bounded by

$$\langle W_{\mathrm{diss}}\rangle \le \frac{\theta^2}{2\beta}\,\mathcal{F}_K \cdot \kappa,$$

where $\kappa$ is an $O(1)$ constant fixed by the metric; for the SLD metric and commuting-free encoding the tight statement is the standard thermodynamic uncertainty relation $\langle W_{\mathrm{diss}}\rangle \cdot \mathcal{F}_K \ge$ (const)$\,\theta^2$-type bounds [1], [2]. We do not claim a new constant here; we note only the scaling $\langle W_{\mathrm{diss}}\rangle \propto \theta^2 k_B T\, \mathcal{F}_K$, which combines with Step 4 to give $\langle W_{\mathrm{diss}}\rangle \propto \theta^2 (\hbar\omega)^2/(4 k_B T)$ at high $T$ — i.e., dissipative cost *decreases* with temperature while information also decreases, both as the same power of $T$, so their ratio is temperature-independent at leading order. This ratio-invariance is the operational content of the universality result [1], [2].

## 5. Results

All numbers below are computed in Section 4 with shown arithmetic; none are simulated or measured.

**R1 (Exact qubit QFI).** For $H = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z$, $G = \sigma_x/2$, dimensionless $\theta$:

$$\mathcal{F}_K = \tanh^2\!\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2 k_B T}\right).$$

Limits: $\mathcal{F}_K \to 1$ as $T \to 0$; $\mathcal{F}_K \to \left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_B T}\right)^2$ as $T \to \infty$.

**R2 (Crossover values).** At $k_B T = \hbar\omega/2$ ($x = 2$): $\mathcal{F}_K = 0.5800$; deviation from the universal extrapolation is $\delta = 0.4200$ ($42.00\%$). At $k_B T = 2\hbar\omega$ ($x = 0.5$): $\mathcal{F}_K = 0.05999$; deviation is $\delta = 0.0402$ ($4.02\%$).

**R3 (Universal high-$T$ coefficient, SLD convention).** For the energy generator $G_E = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_x$ with $\mathrm{Var}_\beta(G_E) = \frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4}$ at high $T$:

$$\mathcal{F}_\alpha \approx \frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4 (k_B T)^2},$$

verified against the exact expansion in Step 3.

**R4 (Scaling of dissipative work).** At leading order in $\theta$ and $1/T$, $\langle W_{\mathrm{diss}}\rangle \propto \theta^2 (\hbar\omega)^2/(4 k_B T)$, and the ratio $\langle W_{\mathrm{diss}}\rangle/\mathcal{F}_K$ is temperature-independent at leading order (both scale as $1/T$). This is a scaling statement derived from Steps 3–5, not a new bound with a new constant.

**R5 (Projection, labeled as such).** For an $N$-sensor uncorrelated array, the total QFI is the sum $\mathcal{F}^{(N)} = N\,\mathcal{F}_K$ (standard additivity for product probes). *Projection assumption:* independent sensors, no cross-correlations, identical temperature. Under this assumption, at $k_B T = 2\hbar\omega$, $\mathcal{F}^{(N)} = 0.05999\,N$; reaching $\mathcal{F}^{(N)} = 1$ (the single cold-sensor value) requires $N = 1/0.05999 \approx 16.7$, i.e., at least $17$ warm sensors to match one ground-state sensor. Uncertainty: this projection inherits the exact two-level arithmetic (no sampling error) but fails if inter-sensor correlations or non-qubit structure are present.

## 6. Discussion

**Limitations.** The exact result R1 is restricted to a two-level sensor with a transverse generator; for commuting generators the SLD QFI vanishes identically (Step 2), which is itself a nontrivial warning: a sensor whose signal Hamiltonian commutes with its bath-coupled Hamiltonian carries *no* unitary-encoding information in the SLD sense, and any claimed sensitivity in that configuration must come from non-unitary encoding or from a different metric in the monotone family. The universal form R3 is a leading-order statement; the $O(T^{-3})$ corrections are not computed here, and the $4.02\%$ deviation at $k_B T = 2\hbar\omega$ indicates that the universal regime requires $k_B T \gtrsim 2\hbar\omega$ for few-percent accuracy in this model.

**Failure modes.** (i) If the sensor is strongly coupled to its environment, the effective state is not $\rho_\beta$ of $H$ alone and the entire spectral derivation fails. (ii) The additivity projection R5 assumes uncorrelated probes; entangled or correlated thermal probes can violate $N$-scaling, in either direction. (iii) The work–information scaling R4 is not a tight bound; the literature constants [1], [2] apply under stated convexity conditions we have not verified case by case.

**What would falsify the claims.** R1 is directly falsifiable: any experiment on a well-thermalized qubit measuring phase-estimation variance $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge 1/(\nu \mathcal{F}_K)$ for $\nu$ repetitions that finds $\mathcal{F}_K$ exceeding $\tanh^2(\hbar\omega/2k_B T)$ at the same $T$ would falsify the derivation (it relies only on the spectral form of $\rho_\beta$ and the SLD definition). R3 would be falsified if different monotone metrics were found to differ at leading order in $1/T$ for a fixed thermal state — this would contradict the operator-monotone collapse argument of [1], [2].

**Against ourselves.** One might object that the two-level model is too degenerate to represent real sensors, since the commuting case gives exactly zero and the transverse case gives a one-line formula. The defense is that the degeneracy is the point: the ratio of transverse to commuting sensitivity is infinite, so generator geometry, not temperature, is the dominant design variable near the commuting boundary. A stronger objection concerns the choice of SLD normalization in Step 3; we showed the exact expansion matches the universal form only after fixing the convention, and a reader using the Kubo–Mori normalization would quote a coefficient larger by $4$. We have stated the convention explicitly, but cross-literature comparisons must always check it. Finally, the $17$-sensor projection is illustrative only; it assumes the two-level model holds for each sensor, which real atomic platforms [6] with multi-level structure and inhomogeneous shifts may violate.

**Open questions.** The exact finite-$T$ QFI for noncommuting $H$, $G$ beyond the qubit; the tight constant in the work–information relation for the full monotone family; and the extension of the crossover analysis to Gaussian probes [4] and to distributed settings [3] where thermal noise may be correlated across nodes.

## 7. Conclusion

For a thermal two-level sensor with transverse generator, the exact SLD QFI is $\mathcal{F}_K = \tanh^2(\hbar\omega/2k_B T)$, interpolating between the ground-state value $1$ and the universal high-temperature form $(\hbar\omega)^2/(4(k_B T)^2)$. The universal form is accurate to $4.02\%$ at $k_B T = 2\hbar\omega$ but fails by $42.00\%$ at $k_B T = \hbar\omega/2$, locating the metrological crossover at temperatures of order the sensor gap. Dissipative work and QFI scale with the same power of $T$ at leading order, making their ratio temperature-independent — the operational signature of the monotone-metric universality established in [1], [2]. Compensating thermal loss with sensor count is expensive: under stated assumptions, $17$ warm sensors match one cold one at $k_B T = 2\hbar\omega$. The framework extends naturally to Gaussian [4], distributed [3], and multi-level platforms, where the same spectral method applies.
