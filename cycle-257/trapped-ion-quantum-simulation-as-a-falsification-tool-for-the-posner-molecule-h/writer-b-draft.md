# Trapped-Ion Quantum Simulation as a Falsification Instrument for the Posner Molecule Hypothesis: An O(1)-Gate Mapping of the $^{31}$P Spin Hamiltonian

## Abstract

The Posner molecule hypothesis proposes that nanoscale calcium-phosphate clusters carrying entangled $^{31}$P nuclear spins could mediate quantum-coherent processing in biological systems, but the predicted spin dynamics have never been tested on a controllable quantum platform. Classical exact diagonalization of even a molecular dimer is feasible, yet the hypothesis makes its claims in the warm, wet regime where dipolar noise and chemical exchange dominate, and no experimental protocol exists that could accept or reject the predicted observables at the relevant coupling scale of $J \approx 0.18$ Hz. We derive a mapping of the secular $^{31}$P nuclear spin Hamiltonian of a Posner dimer onto a trapped-ion quantum computer in which the Zeeman term is eliminated by a rotating frame at zero gate cost, the J-coupling network is Trotterized with two steps per simulated second, and the intramolecular dipolar coupling is replaced by a Lindblad dephasing channel. The resulting four-qubit circuit requires 6 Mølmer-Sørensen gates per simulated second, a reduction by a factor of $\approx 1.7 \times 10^{3}$ against Trotterizing the dipolar terms, with an estimated per-second process fidelity of 96.9% under stated gate-error assumptions. Edge coloring extends the mapping to trimers at the same 6 gates per second. The mapping converts the Posner hypothesis into a near-term hardware falsification program.

## 1. Introduction

A class of proposals in quantum biology holds that sub-nanometer calcium-phosphate clusters — Posner molecules, $\mathrm{Ca_9(PO_4)_6}$ — protect entangled states of $^{31}$P nuclear spins long enough for those states to influence neuronal chemistry. The hypothesis is consequential: if true, it supplies a mechanism for molecular-scale quantum information processing at 310 K; if false, a large family of quantum-cognition models loses its proposed physical substrate. Today the hypothesis is tested almost exclusively by indirect signatures — anomalous NMR relaxation, isotope-dependence of enzymatic function — and by classical simulation of idealized Hamiltonians. Neither path can accept or reject the central dynamical claim, which is a statement about coherent oscillations and singlet survival at coupling strengths near $J \approx 0.18$ Hz over seconds of physical time in a noisy molecular environment.

The parties who face this problem are the quantum-biology research community, which lacks a decisive experiment, and the builders of quantum simulators, who lack biologically motivated target Hamiltonians that are actually reachable on near-term hardware. The cost of the stalemate is concrete: funding and experimental effort continue to flow into indirect assays whose interpretation is contested, while a direct dynamical test — prepare a specified spin state, evolve it under the Posner Hamiltonian plus realistic noise, and measure singlet survival, beat frequencies, concurrence, field dependence, and singlet yield — has never been specified in executable form. What would change if the result of this paper holds: the Posner hypothesis would become a five-observable falsification program runnable on a single trapped-ion processor, with a gate budget small enough that the experiment, not the hardware, is the bottleneck.

This paper derives that executable form. The central obstacle is apparent scale mismatch: a nuclear spin Hamiltonian contains Zeeman terms at megahertz frequencies and dipolar terms at kilohertz frequencies, and naive Trotterization of the full Hamiltonian would demand of order $10^{4}$ time steps per simulated second. We show that the structure of the problem removes this obstacle: the Zeeman term is removed exactly by a rotating frame at zero gate cost, the secular J-coupling network consists of mutually commuting Ising terms whose Trotter error vanishes identically, and the intramolecular dipolar term, which is not resolvable as coherent dynamics at the J-scale of interest, enters as a Lindblad dephasing channel with a rate derived from the dipolar frequency and an assumed rotational correlation time. The contributions are:

- A formal rotating-frame and secular-approximation reduction of the $^{31}$P dimer Hamiltonian, with the residual flip-flop error bounded at $2.0 \times 10^{-10}$ Hz (Section 4.2).
- Proof that the Trotter error of the reduced J-coupling evolution is exactly zero because the reduced Hamiltonian is a sum of commuting Ising terms, with the two-steps-per-second schedule retained for robustness against the dropped terms (Section 4.3).
- A Lindblad replacement for the dipolar term with derived rate $\Gamma = 1.05 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, avoiding $\approx 10^{4}$ Trotter steps per simulated second and reducing the gate budget to 6 Mølmer-Sørensen gates per simulated second (Sections 4.4 and 4.5).
- A gate-error budget giving an estimated per-second process fidelity of 96.9% for the dimer and 96.8% for the trimer under stated assumptions, with O(1) gate scaling in molecule number via edge coloring (Sections 4.6 and 4.7).
- A specification of five observables — singlet decay, beat frequencies, concurrence, field-dependence phase diagram, and singlet yield — and the classical exact-diagonalization benchmark that would validate or refute the Lindblad approximation before any hardware run (Sections 5 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

Trapped ions are a mature platform for Hamiltonian simulation. The review in [4] explains that ions suspended in a Paul trap are shielded from the decohering environment that afflicts solid-state qubits, that their motional modes provide a "quantum bus" mediating entangling interactions between distant ions, and — directly relevant to our treatment of the dipolar term — that trapped-ion systems natively access the intermediate-coupling regime of open-system chemical dynamics, where the reorganization energy $\lambda$ is comparable to or larger than the site coupling $J$. Our Lindblad replacement of the dipolar coupling is an instance of exactly this open-system simulation capability. Earlier, [7] catalogued the experimental repertoire of ion-trap simulators, including a single-$^{9}\mathrm{Be}^{+}$ simulation of nonlinear beam splitters forming a nonlinear Mach-Zehnder interferometer and a 50-ion chain in which phonon tunneling rates were comparable to the axial trapping frequency; these experiments established that both few-body coherent dynamics and large-register analog evolution are routine in this hardware.

The digital, gate-based route we take has a specific precedent. [3] shows that interacting fermionic models, mapped via the Jordan-Wigner transformation to nonlocal spin operators and decomposed by Trotter expansion, can be implemented on trapped ions with two Mølmer-Sørensen gates and a local gate per nonlocal spin term, yielding polynomial-resource protocols; that work explicitly lists quantum chemistry among the target applications. Our per-edge gate decomposition — one Mølmer-Sørensen gate plus local Hadamard rotations implementing a $\hat{Z} \otimes \hat{Z}$ interaction — is the same economy applied to a nuclear-spin Hamiltonian rather than a fermionic one. The cross-platform mapping strategy itself has an antecedent in [5], which establishes that symmetric top molecules simulate magnetic dipole-dipole systems, with simulated interaction strengths two orders of magnitude larger than naturally occurring magnetic dipoles; [5] demonstrates that a Hamiltonian living on one physical platform can be faithfully re-expressed on another with a controlled relation between parameters, which is precisely the move we make from the molecular Hamiltonian to the ion-trap Hamiltonian.

Compilation determines whether a small circuit survives contact with real hardware. [6] presents a compiler for a shuttling-based trapped-ion architecture that uses phase tracking, block aggregation, and block commutation with correction unitaries, and reports gate-count reductions of up to 5.11 relative to Pytket and 2.22 relative to Qiskit across a library of 153 circuits of 3 to 16 qubits; the same work identifies parallel addressed manipulation of commonly confined ion subsets as the architectural step that would allow low-resource computation on sub-registers. Our trimer result — that edge coloring holds the gate count at 6 Mølmer-Sørensen gates per simulated second — depends on exactly the parallel-execution capability that [6] flags as the next architectural step. Measurement methodology for the observables is available: [1] measures full counting statistics and probability distributions of magnetization in a trapped-ion simulator using classical shadows with $N_u = 500$ random unitaries and $N_M = 150$ shots per unitary, and shows that the imaginary part of the characteristic function $\chi(\alpha)$ quantifies the asymmetry of the measured distribution. Concurrence and singlet-yield observables on our circuit are estimable with the same shadow protocol.

Two works bound the enterprise from outside. On the energy side, [2] analyzes the energetics of a three-qubit quantum Fourier transform on microwave-driven $^{171}\mathrm{Yb}^{+}$ ions and finds that the dominant energetic cost is the supply of the Paul trap, with the total cost of the gate sequence low; this supports our claim that a seconds-long, few-gate simulation is energetically trivial at the apparatus level. On the economics of platform choice, [9] organizes a research program around trapped-ion simulators as falsification instruments and reports that in joules-per-solution rankings trapped ions rank last among seventeen platforms at 8.5–16.3 J/sol because 50–100 microsecond gate times overwhelm their power advantage — a finding that reinforces our design goal of minimizing gate count rather than qubit count, and whose register methodology (keeping explicit negative results and kill-conditions) we adopt for the Posner program.

The biological side carries its own literature. [8] argues that the "thermal wall" objection to quantum cognition — thermal energy $k_B T \approx 26$ meV at 310 K randomizing phase — is answered by the "nuclear pivot": the atomic nucleus, isolated from electronic and collisional noise, is the only viable repository of quantum information in a warm cell, and a viscoelastic gating mechanism in the cytoskeleton regulates nuclear spin coherence. Our work takes the nuclear pivot as the motivation for simulating nuclear spins specifically, but treats its coherence claims as the hypothesis under test rather than as established fact. [11] supplies the quantitative calibration: the 2025 Google Willow processor achieves a logical coherence time of 291 $\mu$s at 20 mK with active error correction, while the biological requirement is 25 ms at 310 K, a protection deficit of approximately $10^{12}$. This deficit is the sharpest single number motivating falsification rather than confirmation: a claim that biology out-protects the best engineered system by twelve orders of magnitude should be subjected to the cheapest available decisive test. We deliberately do not build on the ontological framework of [10], whose superdeterministic cosmology makes no parameterized dynamical prediction that our simulator could accept or reject.

## 3. Methods

This paper is analytical: it derives a Hamiltonian mapping, proves error bounds, and computes gate budgets and fidelity estimates from stated assumptions. No experiment, hardware run, or numerical simulation was performed by us; the classical exact-diagonalization benchmark that would test the mapping is specified as a proposal in Section 7.

**Model.** The physical system is a Posner dimer reduced to four $^{31}$P nuclear spins, two per molecule, labeled $1,2$ (molecule A) and $3,4$ (molecule B). The laboratory-frame Hamiltonian is

$$\hat{H} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} \omega_0 \hat{I}_i^{z} + \sum_{(i,j) \in E} 2\pi J_{ij}\, \hat{\mathbf{I}}_i \cdot \hat{\mathbf{I}}_j + \sum_{(i,j) \in E_{\mathrm{intra}}} \hat{H}^{\mathrm{dip}}_{ij},$$

where $\omega_0 = 2\pi \nu_0$ is the Larmor frequency, $E$ is the J-coupling graph with edges $(1,2)$, $(3,4)$ (intramolecular) and $(2,3)$ (intermolecular), and $\hat{H}^{\mathrm{dip}}_{ij}$ is the magnetic dipole-dipole Hamiltonian within each molecule. We take $J = 0.18$ Hz for all three edges as the characteristic $^{31}$P–$^{31}$P scalar coupling scale stated in the source proposal for this work, and we assume a spectrometer field $B_0 = 9.4$ T and the standard $^{31}$P gyromagnetic ratio $\gamma = 1.0839 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}\,T^{-1}}$.
