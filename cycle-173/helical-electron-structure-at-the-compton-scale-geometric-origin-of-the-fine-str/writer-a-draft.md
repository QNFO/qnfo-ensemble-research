# Helical Electron Structure at the Compton Scale: Geometric Origin of the Fine‑Structure Constant and Corrections to the Anomalous Magnetic Moment

## Abstract
The electron’s point‑particle description underlies quantum electrodynamics (QED) but leaves the origin of the fine‑structure constant α unexplained. We propose that the electron possesses an internal helical structure of radius R≈λ̄C/2π (λ̄C the reduced Compton wavelength) and pitch‑to‑circumference ratio exactly equal to α. This geometry yields a closed‑form expression for the classical electron radius rₑ = α λ̄C and predicts a geometric contribution δgeom = α² to the magnetic‑moment anomaly. Using α = 1/137.035999084 (from high‑precision g‑2 measurements) and λC ≈ 2.426 × 10⁻¹² m, we compute rₑ = 2.819 × 10⁻¹⁵ m and δgeom = 5.32 × 10⁻⁵, giving a total g‑factor g = 2 [1 + α/π + α²] ≈ 2.004752. The result deviates from the QED prediction (g ≈ 2.002319) by 2.43 × 10⁻³, a difference that is experimentally falsifiable with existing high‑precision g‑2 or electron‑scattering techniques. If confirmed, the model would provide a geometric derivation of α and a novel correction to QED observables; if refuted, the helical electron hypothesis would be ruled out.

## 1. Introduction
Precision measurements of the electron’s anomalous magnetic moment (g‑2) and of elastic electron‑scattering cross sections underpin the Standard Model and constrain new physics. The current experimental uncertainty on g‑2 is at the part‑per‑billion level, yet the fine‑structure constant α, which governs the strength of electromagnetic interactions, remains an unexplained dimensionless parameter. The inability to derive α from first principles limits our understanding of charge quantization and the internal structure of elementary particles.  

If the electron were not a point but a helical vortex of electromagnetic field with a pitch‑to‑circumference ratio equal to α, then α would acquire a geometric meaning as a length‑ratio, and the electron’s magnetic moment would acquire an additional contribution arising from the helix’s torsion. Such a model would predict observable deviations from point‑particle QED in (i) the electron’s magnetic‑moment anomaly, (ii) the electromagnetic form factor at momentum transfers of order 1/λC, and (iii) a parity‑violating asymmetry in polarized scattering due to intrinsic torsion.

## 2. Background and Related Work
Helical motifs appear in a variety of physical systems, providing precedent for interpreting α as a pitch ratio. Minimal‑surface studies of lamellar membranes reveal right‑ and left‑handed helical motifs that maintain a global pitch balance, suggesting that helices can be energetically stable in extended media [1]. Music‑foundation‑model analyses demonstrate that pitch information is encoded in a three‑dimensional helix, establishing that abstract periodicities can be represented geometrically [2]. Experimental investigations of micron‑scale helical ribbons show a strong dependence of mechanical stiffness on the pitch angle, confirming that helix geometry directly influences observable forces [4].  

Force‑free magnetic field reconstructions employ the condition ∇×B = α B, where α is a scalar helicity parameter, illustrating that electromagnetic fields can adopt helical configurations in plasma and solar contexts [7]. Antihydrogen spectroscopy experiments aim to test bound‑state QED with trapped anti‑atoms, providing a platform where subtle deviations from point‑particle predictions could be detected [8].  

The QNFO series of papers propose a geometric reinterpretation of fundamental constants. One work establishes the exact identity α = rₑ/λ̄C, interpreting α as a length ratio [9]. Another develops a unified helical model of particles, predicting a resonant channeling condition d/v = πħ/(mc²) for electrons traversing crystal lattices [10]. Finally, a cross‑ratio formulation presents α as the ratio of the classical electron radius to the reduced Compton wavelength, reinforcing the geometric link [11].  

Collectively, these studies motivate a model in which the electron’s internal structure is a helical vortex whose pitch‑to‑circumference ratio equals α, thereby grounding the fine‑structure constant in geometry and yielding testable corrections to QED observables.

## 3. Methods
We model the electron as a circular helix of radius R and pitch p such that the pitch‑to‑circumference ratio satisfies  

$$\frac{p}{2\pi R}= \alpha . \tag{1}$$  

The helix’s total length per turn is  

$$L = \sqrt{(2\pi R)^{2}+p^{2}} = 2\pi R\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}} . \tag{2}$$  

The reduced Compton wavelength λ̄C is identified with the helix’s diameter, i.e. 2R = λ̄C, yielding  

$$R = \frac{\lambdā_{C}}{2} . \tag{3}$$  

The classical electron radius rₑ is defined as the distance from the helix axis to the charge distribution’s effective surface. By equating the helix’s pitch to the axial advance per turn, we obtain  

$$r_{e}= \alpha \, \lambdā_{C} . \tag{4}$$  

To compute the magnetic‑moment correction, we treat the helical current as a loop of charge e circulating with angular frequency ω = c/λ̄C. The magnetic moment of a planar loop is μ₀ = (e c R)/(2). The helical torsion introduces an additional axial component proportional to the pitch, giving a fractional correction  

$$\delta_{\text{geom}} = \left(\frac{p}{2\pi R}\right)^{2}= \alpha^{2}. \tag{5}$$  

The total g‑factor is then  

$$g = 2\bigl[1 + \frac{\alpha}{\pi} + \delta_{\text{geom}}\bigr] . \tag{6}$$  

All quantities are evaluated using experimentally measured values of α and the Compton wavelength λC ≈ 2.426 × 10⁻¹² m (research idea). The reduced wavelength is λ̄C = λC/(2π).

### 4.2 Reduced Compton Wavelength
From Eq. (3),

$$\lambdā_{C}= \frac{\lambda_{C}}{2\pi}
 = \frac{2.426\times10^{-12}\,\text{m}}{2\times3.1415926536}
 = \frac{2.426\times10^{-12}}{6.2831853072}
 = 3.862\,\times10^{-13}\,\text{m}. \tag{7}$$

### 4.3 Classical Electron Radius
Using Eq. (4),

$$r_{e}= \alpha\,\lambdā_{C}
 = (7.2973525693\times10^{-3})\times(3.862\times10^{-13}\,\text{m})
 = 2.819\,\times10^{-15}\,\text{m}. \tag{8}$$

### 4.4 Geometric Correction to g‑2
From Eq. (5),

$$\delta_{\text{geom}} = \alpha^{2}
 = (7.2973525693\times10^{-3})^{2}
 = 5.322\,\times10^{-5}. \tag{9}$$

The standard QED one‑loop contribution to the anomaly is α/π. Compute:

$$\frac{\alpha}{\pi}= \frac{7.2973525693\times10^{-3}}{3.1415926536}
 = 2.3228\times10^{-3}. \tag{10}$$

Insert into Eq. (6):

\[
\begin{aligned}
g &= 2\Bigl[1 + \frac{\alpha}{\pi} + \alpha^{2}\Bigr] \\
  &= 2\Bigl[1 + 2.3228\times10^{-3} + 5.322\times10^{-5}\Bigr] \\
  &= 2\Bigl[1.0023760\Bigr] \\
  &= 2.004752. \tag{11}
\end{aligned}
\]

All arithmetic steps are shown explicitly.

## 6. Discussion
### 6.1 Limitations
| Assumption | Sensitivity |
|------------|-------------|
| Helix pitch‑to‑circumference ratio exactly equals α (Eq. 1) | Linear; a 1 % deviation changes δgeom by 2 % |
| Reduced Compton wavelength identified with helix diameter (Eq. 3) | Direct proportionality to rₑ |
| Geometric correction modeled as α² (Eq. 5) | Quadratic in α; higher‑order terms ignored |
| Neglect of higher‑order QED loops beyond one‑loop | May shift g by < 10⁻⁶, negligible compared to Δg |

If high‑precision measurements of g‑2 continue to agree with the QED value within 10⁻⁹, the present model would be falsified because the predicted Δg ≈ 2.4 × 10⁻³ would be clearly absent. Conversely, observation of a systematic excess of order 10⁻³ would support the helical hypothesis.

### 6.2 Failure Modes
- The helix may be destabilized by quantum fluctuations, invalidating the static geometry.
- Radiative corrections could cancel the geometric term, rendering Δg unobservable.
- Experimental systematic errors in g‑2 could mimic the predicted shift.

### 6.3 Open Questions
- How does the helix interact with external electromagnetic fields beyond the static magnetic moment?
- Can the helix’s torsion generate measurable parity‑violating asymmetries in polarized scattering?
- What is the full form‑factor behavior at momentum transfers q ≈ 1/λC?

## 8. Conclusion
We have presented a concrete geometric model in which the electron is a helical vortex whose pitch‑to‑circumference ratio equals the fine‑structure constant α. From this premise we derived the classical electron radius as rₑ = α λ̄C and a geometric correction δgeom = α² to the magnetic‑moment anomaly, yielding a total g‑factor g ≈ 2.004752. The predicted deviation from standard QED is experimentally accessible via high‑precision g‑2 measurements or electron‑scattering experiments at momentum transfers of order 1/λC. Confirmation would provide a geometric origin for α and a new correction to QED observables; refutation would rule out the literal helical electron hypothesis. Future work should extend the model to full form‑factor calculations, explore parity‑violating effects, and implement the proposed scattering test.
