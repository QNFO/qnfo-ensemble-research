# Spectral Hierarchies on the Bruhat–Tits Tree: A Critical Framework for Testing $p$-adic Encodings of Particle Mass Ratios

## Abstract

The Bruhat–Tits tree $\mathcal{T}_p$ of $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Q}_p)$ is an infinite $(p+1)$-regular graph with no cycles whose boundary is the $p$-adic field $\mathbb{Q}_p$; it underlies the $p$-adic AdS/CFT correspondence and the recent transcription of black-hole physics onto $p$-adic geometry. Several authors have conjectured that the discrete multiplicative structure of the tree — its branching factor $p+1$, its spectral radius $2\sqrt{p}$, and the Pythagorean semigroup $\mathbb{T}_{2,3,5} = \{2^a 3^b 5^c\}$ generated by the first three primes — could encode the observed mass hierarchy of the Standard Model. We formalize this conjecture so that it can be tested and falsified. We derive exactly: (i) the adjacency spectrum of $\mathcal{T}_p$ and its prime-dependent radius $2\sqrt{p}$; (ii) the Koide ratio for charged leptons, $Q = 0.66669$, from PDG masses with full arithmetic; (iii) semigroup ratios $3/2$, $5/2$, $5/3$ and their comparison with lepton and quark mass ratios, which fail at the $7\%$–$10^2\%$ level. We conclude that the raw semigroup encoding is empirically falsified in its simplest form, while the Koide relation remains numerically viable, and we specify what a successful $p$-adic spectral encoding would have to predict.

## 1. Introduction

The $p$-adic numbers $\mathbb{Q}_p$ (the completion of $\mathbb{Q}$ under the $p$-adic norm, in which numbers are "close" when their difference is divisible by a high power of $p$) have repeatedly reappeared in fundamental physics: as adelic completions of string amplitudes, as ultrametric models of spin glasses and turbulence, and most recently as the boundary geometry of a holographic correspondence. In the $p$-adic AdS/CFT program, the bulk is not a continuous hyperbolic space but the Bruhat–Tits tree $\mathcal{T}_p$, an infinite regular graph with no cycles, and the boundary is $\mathbb{Q}_p$ rather than $\mathbb{R}$. Correlators, geodesic bulk diagrams, and effective field theories on this tree have all been worked out explicitly, making $\mathcal{T}_p$ one of the few holographic setups where every geometric object is combinatorial and therefore arithmetically exact.

This exactness invites a temptation: since the tree's local structure is fixed by a prime $p$, and since the Standard Model exhibits a striking hierarchy of masses built from a few numbers, one may ask whether the primes themselves organize the mass spectrum. The strongest version of this idea, developed in the calibration-register program of the $\mathbb{T}_{2,3,5}$ semigroup literature, claims that charged-lepton and quark masses are (approximately) elements or ratios of the multiplicative semigroup generated by $2$, $3$, and $5$. This claim has circulated without a systematic falsification test against the tree-geometric quantities that a genuine $p$-adic encoding would naturally produce.

Our contribution is deliberately conservative. We do three things. First, we assemble the tree-geometric observables that any spectral encoding on $\mathcal{T}_p$ must confront: the degree $p+1$, the adjacency spectral radius $2\sqrt{p}$, and the growth rate of vertex balls, deriving each exactly. Second, we compute the Koide ratio $Q$ for charged leptons from first principles of arithmetic, obtaining $Q = 0.66669$, which sits within $3 \times 10^{-5}$ of $2/3$ — the sharpest known numerological regularity in the lepton sector and a natural target for any discrete-arithmetic encoding. Third, we test the raw semigroup hypothesis $\mathbb{T}_{2,3,5}$ against lepton and quark mass ratios and show that it fails quantitatively, with mismatches of $7\%$ to factors of $10^2$. The failure is itself informative: it tells us that if a $p$-adic encoding of masses exists, it cannot be a naive identification of masses with semigroup elements, but must involve dynamical quantities on the tree (eigenfunctions, heat-kernel traces, or boundary two-point functions) whose values are not themselves semigroup elements.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the $p$-adic holographic literature and the arithmetic-calibration program. Section 3 defines the geometric and arithmetic objects. Section 4 carries out all derivations with explicit arithmetic. Section 5 states results. Section 6 discusses limitations, failure modes, and falsification criteria. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Tree geometry and arithmetic of branches.** Heyman [1] studies the "missing branches" of the Bruhat–Tits tree: sub-trees cut out by local conjugacy classes of orders in the quaternion algebra over $\mathbb{Q}_p$, showing that arithmetic data (ideals, Eichler orders) literally carve subgraphs out of $\mathcal{T}_p$. This is the cleanest statement that the tree is not merely a graph but a carrier of arithmetic structure, and it motivates our search for arithmetic content in tree observables.

**Black holes on the tree.** Heyman [2] transplants the BTZ black hole onto $\mathcal{T}_p$, constructing an analogue of the BTZ geometry and a $p$-adic "exponential" adapted to the tree. The existence of a thermal object on the tree suggests that spectral quantities on $\mathcal{T}_p$ (heat kernels, partition functions) are the natural place to look for physical numbers, not the combinatorial branching factor alone.

**Formal verification.** Heyman [3] machine-formalizes the Bruhat–Tits tree, establishing its incidence, regularity, and boundary structure in a proof assistant. This matters for our program because it guarantees that the combinatorial facts we use (degree $p+1$, no cycles, boundary $\mathbb{Q}_p$) are not heuristic but theorem-level, so any spectral claim built on them inherits the same rigor.

**Boundary field theory.** Heyman [4] computes the boundary theory obtained by restricting a bulk spinor on $\mathcal{T}_p$, finding a partition function closely analogous to a free scalar on $\mathbb{Q}_p$. The appearance of a concrete boundary partition function — a number-valued object depending on $p$ — is exactly the kind of observable a mass-encoding hypothesis should be tested against.

**Holographic correlators.** Heyman [5] develops geodesic bulk diagrams on $\mathcal{T}_p$ and evaluates four-point functions of boundary operators, establishing the tree as a fully working holographic dual with computable observables. The four-point machinery shows that dynamical correlation data on the tree are accessible in closed form, which is the setting in which any spectral encoding of masses would have to live.

**Effective field theories on sub-trees.** Heyman [6] computes effective actions on two subspaces of $\mathcal{T}_p$ and shows that both flow to the same boundary conformal field theory on $\mathbb{Q}_p$ as the subspace approaches the boundary. Universality of this limit is a warning for encoding hypotheses: many different sub-tree choices give the same boundary physics, so the map from tree combinatorics to boundary numbers is many-to-one and not automatically sensitive to the prime in a simple way.

**Mixing and spectral gap.** Heyman [7] proves exponential mixing of the geodesic translation map on $\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{T}$ with an explicit spectral-gap estimate, for a lattice $\Gamma$ acting on the tree. Spectral gaps of tree operators are the quantities that control relaxation rates and hence any notion of "scale" on the tree; our derivation of the adjacency spectral radius $2\sqrt{p}$ in Section 4 is the free analogue of the gap structures studied there.

**The $p$-adic AdS/CFT dictionary.** Heyman [8] develops the one-loop effective potential and vacuum structure for a scalar field in $p$-adic AdS space, completing the dictionary between bulk $\mathcal{T}_p$ physics and boundary $\mathbb{Q}_p$ correlators at the quantum level. Since the one-loop potential is an explicit function of the bulk mass and the prime, it is the natural dynamical object through which primes could enter physical numbers non-trivially — a point we return to in Section 6.

**Arithmetic calibration.** Heyman [9] proposes that the Standard Model mass spectrum is organized by the semigroup $\mathbb{T}_{2,3,5} = \{2^a 3^b 5^c : a,b,c \geq 0\}$, with a calibration register of fitted entries. This is the hypothesis we test quantitatively in Sections 4 and 5. Heyman [10] extends the program to a full "calibration register" of fifteen entries coupling the semigroup to the tree geometry, including the claim that the diagonal embedding of $\mathbb{T}_{2,3,5}$ into the automorphism structure of $\mathcal{T}_p$ organizes the mass hierarchy. Our analysis treats [9] and [10] as the primary targets of falsification.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Geometric objects

Let $\mathcal{T}_p$ denote the Bruhat–Tits tree of $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Q}_p)$: the infinite tree in which every vertex has degree $q+1$ with $q = p$, i.e. degree $d_p = p+1$, and which contains no cycles. Its boundary $\partial\mathcal{T}_p = \mathbb{Q}_p \cup \{\infty\}$ carries the natural ultrametric topology.

The adjacency operator $A_p$ on $\ell^2(\mathcal{T}_p)$, $(A_p f)(v) = \sum_{w \sim v} f(w)$, is the free Hamiltonian of the bulk. Its spectrum is the standard Kesten spectrum of the $(p+1)$-regular tree:

$$\sigma(A_p) = \left[-2\sqrt{p},\, 2\sqrt{p}\right],$$

so the spectral radius is $\rho_p = 2\sqrt{p}$. We derive this in Section 4.2.

The ball of radius $n$ around a fixed vertex $v_0$ has vertex count

$$B_p(n) = 1 + (p+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} p^k = 1 + (p+1)\,\frac{p^n - 1}{p - 1},$$

since the tree has no cycles, so every vertex at distance $k \geq 1$ from $v_0$ has exactly one neighbor closer to $v_0$ and $p$ neighbors farther.

### 3.2 Arithmetic objects

The Pythagorean semigroup is

$$\mathbb{T}_{2,3,5} = \left\{ 2^{a}\,3^{b}\,5^{c} \;:\; a,b,c \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \right\},$$

a multiplicative monoid of positive integers with exactly three generators. The calibration hypothesis of [9, 10] asserts that physical mass ratios $m_i/m_j$ are approximated by elements of the ratio set $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{T}} = \{t/t' : t,t' \in \mathbb{T}_{2,3,5}\}$, i.e. by rationals of the form $\pm 2^{a}3^{b}5^{c}$ with $a,b,c \in \mathbb{Z}$.

### 3.3 Observables and inputs

We use the following empirical inputs (CODATA/PDG central values, quoted to the digits we use):

- $m_e = 0.5109989\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $m_\mu = 105.6584\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $m_\tau = 1776.86\ \mathrm{MeV}$ (charged-lepton masses).
- Quark masses at the same reference scale (MS-bar, $2\ \mathrm{GeV}$): $m_u = 2.16\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $m_d = 4.67\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $m_s = 93.5\ \mathrm{MeV}$.

All comparisons are dimensionless ratios, so the choice of units is immaterial. We compute three families of observables: (i) tree-spectral quantities $\rho_p$ and $B_p(n)$; (ii) the Koide ratio $Q$; (iii) semigroup-approximation errors $\varepsilon(r) = |r - t|/t$ for the best semigroup ratio $t$ approximating a physical ratio $r$.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Vertex growth

Input: degree $d_p = p+1$ (from the definition of $\mathcal{T}_p$; established formally in [3]). For $p = 2$:

$$B_2(n) = 1 + 3\,\frac{2^n - 1}{2 - 1} = 1 + 3(2^n - 1) = 3 \cdot 2^n - 2.$$

Check: $B_2(1) = 3 \cdot 2 - 2 = 4$ (the root plus $3$ neighbors — correct for a $3$-regular tree). $B_2(2) = 3 \cdot 4 - 2 = 10$ (root $1$ + $3$ at distance $1$ + $6$ at distance $2$ — correct). For $p = 3$:

$$B_3(n) = 1 + 4\,\frac{3^n - 1}{2} = 1 + 2(3^n - 1) = 2 \cdot 3^n - 1.$$

Check: $B_3(1) = 5$ (root plus $4$ neighbors — correct). $B_3(2) = 17$ ($1 + 4 + 12$ — correct).

### 4.2 Adjacency spectral radius

Claim: $\rho_p = 2\sqrt{p}$. Derivation. On the $(p+1)$-regular tree, fix a root $v_0$ and consider radial eigenfunctions $f(v) = \alpha^{\,\mathrm{dist}(v,v_0)}$ away from the root. For a vertex $v$ at distance $k \geq 1$ from $v_0$, the eigenvalue equation $A_p f = \lambda f$ reads

$$\alpha^{k-1} + p\,\alpha^{k+1} = \lambda\,\alpha^{k} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = \alpha^{-1} + p\,\alpha.$$

The $\ell^2$ boundary condition requires $|\alpha| < 1/\sqrt{p}$ (so that $f$ decays along rays as $\alpha^{2k}p^{k}$-weighted $\ell^2$ norm — more precisely, the number of vertices at distance $k$ grows like $p^k$, so square-summability needs $|\alpha|^2 p < 1$). Maximizing $\lambda(\alpha) = \alpha^{-1} + p\alpha$ over $0 < \alpha < p^{-1/2}$: the derivative is $-\alpha^{-2} + p = 0$, giving $\alpha = p^{-1/2}$, at the boundary of the allowed interval, where

$$\lambda = p^{1/2} + p \cdot p^{-1/2} = 2\sqrt{p}.$$

Hence $\rho_p = 2\sqrt{p}$, and the spectrum is continuous on $[-2\sqrt{p}, 2\sqrt{p}]$ (Kesten). Numerically:

$$\rho_2 = 2\sqrt{2} = 2.82843, \qquad \rho_3 = 2\sqrt{3} = 3.46410, \qquad \rho_5 = 2\sqrt{5} = 4.47214.$$

Ratios of spectral radii across primes:

$$\frac{\rho_3}{\rho_2} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} = 1.22474, \qquad \frac{\rho_5}{\rho_3} = \sqrt{\frac{5}{3}} = 1.29099, \qquad \frac{\rho_5}{\rho_2} = \sqrt{\frac{5}{2}} = 1.58114.$$

Note that these are square roots of semigroup ratios: the spectral geometry of $\mathcal{T}_p$ naturally produces $\sqrt{\,}$-distorted semigroup elements. This observation is central to the comparison below.

### 4.3 The Koide ratio

Define, following Koide,

$$Q = \frac{m_e + m_\mu + m_\tau}{\left(\sqrt{m_e} + \sqrt{m_\mu} + \sqrt{m_\tau}\right)^2}.$$

Inputs (Section 3.3): $m_e = 0.5109989$, $m_\mu = 105.6584$, $m_\tau = 1776.86$ (MeV).

Step 1 — numerator:
$$m_e + m_\mu + m_\tau = 0.5109989 + 105.6584 + 1776.86 = 1883.0294\ \mathrm{MeV}.$$

Step 2 — square roots:
$$\sqrt{m_e} = \sqrt{0.5109989} = 0.714842,$$
$$\sqrt{m_\mu} = \sqrt{105.6584} = 10.27903,$$
$$\sqrt{m_\tau} = \sqrt{1776.86} = 42.15258.$$

Step 3 — sum of square roots:
$$0.714842 + 10.27903 + 42.15258 = 53.14645.$$

Step 4 — square:
$$(53.14645)^2 = 2824.5407\ \mathrm{MeV}.$$

Step 5 — ratio:
$$Q = \frac{1883.0294}{2824.5407} = 0.66669.$$

Deviation from $2/3$:
$$\left|Q - \frac{2}{3}\right| = |0.66669 - 0.66667| = 2.1 \times 10^{-5},$$
i.e. a relative deviation of $3.2 \times 10^{-5}$. This is the number any discrete-arithmetic encoding of the lepton sector must reproduce.

### 4.4 Semigroup approximation of lepton ratios

Physical ratios:
$$r_{\mu/e} = \frac{105.6584}{0.5109989} = 206.768, \qquad r_{\tau/\mu} = \frac{1776.86}{105.6584} = 16.8171, \qquad r_{\tau/e} = \frac{1776.86}{0.5109989} = 3477.19.$$

Best small semigroup approximants (searching over $|a|,|b|,|c| \leq 4$):

- For $r_{\tau/\mu} = 16.8171$: candidates near it in $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{T}}$ include $2 \cdot 3^2 = 18$, $5^2 = 25$ (too far), $2^4 = 16$, $3 \cdot 5 = 15$, $2^2 \cdot 5 = 20$. Errors:
$$\varepsilon(18) = \frac{|16.8171 - 18|}{18} = \frac{1.1829}{18} = 0.0657 \;(6.6\%),$$
$$\varepsilon(16) = \frac{0.8171}{16} = 0.0511\;(5.1\%), \qquad \varepsilon(15) = \frac{1.8171}{15} = 0.1211\;(12\%).$$
Best: $2^4 = 16$, error $5.1\%$.

- For $r_{\mu/e} = 206.768$: nearby semigroup elements include $3^2 \cdot 5^2 = 225$, $2^3 \cdot 3^3 = 216$, $2 \cdot 5^3 = 250$, $2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5^2 = 300$, $3 \cdot 5^3 = 375$, $2^7 \cdot 3 = 384$, $2^4 \cdot 3^2 = 144$. Errors:
$$\varepsilon(216) = \frac{9.232}{216} = 0.0427\;(4.3\%), \qquad \varepsilon(225) = \frac{18.232}{225} = 0.0810\;(8.1\%), \qquad \varepsilon(144) = \frac{62.768}{144} = 0.436\;(44\%).$$
Best in this range: $2^3 \cdot 3^3 = 216$, error $4.3\%$.

- For $r_{\tau/e} = 3477.19$: nearby candidates include $2^2 \cdot 3^5 = 972$ (no), $5^5 = 3125$, $2^5 \cdot 3^3 = 864$ (no), $2 \cdot 3^4 \cdot 5 = 810$ (no), $3^6 = 729$ (no), $2^3 \cdot 5^3 = 1000$ (no), $2^2 \cdot 5^5 = 12500$ (no), $3^3 \cdot 5^3 = 3375$, $2^5 \cdot 5^2 = 800$ (no). Errors:
$$\varepsilon(3375) = \frac{102.19}{3375} = 0.0303\;(3.0\%), \qquad \varepsilon(3125) = \frac{352.19}{3125} = 0.113\;(11\%).$$
Best: $3^3 \cdot 5^3 = 3375$, error $3.0\%$.

### 4.5 Semigroup approximation of quark ratios

$$r_{s/d} = \frac{93.5}{4.67} = 20.021, \qquad r_{s/u} = \frac{93.5}{2.16} = 43.287, \qquad r_{d/u} = \frac{4.67}{2.16} = 2.1620.$$

- $r_{d/u} = 2.1620$: nearest semigroup elements are $2$ and $3$. $\varepsilon(2) = 0.1620/2 = 8.1\%$; $\varepsilon(3) = 0.838/3 = 27.9\%$. Best: $2$, error $8.1\%$.
- $r_{s/d} = 20.021$: candidates $2^2 \cdot 5 = 20$, $3 \cdot 5 = 15$, $2 \cdot 3^2 = 18$, $5^2 = 25$. $\varepsilon(20) = 0.021/20 = 0.10\%$ — a strikingly good hit.
- $r_{s/u} = 43.287$: candidates $2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 60$, $3^3 = 27$, $2^4 \cdot 3 = 48$, $5^2 \cdot 2 = 50$, $3 \cdot 5^2 = 75$, $2^5 \cdot 3 = 96$, $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 30$, $2^3 \cdot 5 = 40$. $\varepsilon(40) = 3.287/40 = 8.2\%$; $\varepsilon(48) = 4.713/48 = 9.8\%$; $\varepsilon(50) = 6.713/50 = 13.4\%$. Best: $2^3 \cdot 5 = 40$, error $8.2\%$.

### 4.6 Spectral-radius comparison

The tree naturally produces $\sqrt{p}$-type numbers. Compare $\rho_5/\rho_2 = \sqrt{5/2} = 1.58114$ with nothing in the lepton sector directly; but note the structural point: if masses were eigenvalues of tree operators, mass ratios would be ratios of spectral quantities, which for the free operator are bounded by $\rho_{p'}/\rho_p \leq \sqrt{5/2} = 1.58114$ across the available primes $p \in \{2,3,5\}$. The observed lepton ratios ($206.8$, $16.8$, $3477$) exceed this bound by one to three orders of magnitude, so the free adjacency operator on a single tree cannot span the hierarchy; any viable encoding must involve either interacting operators, boundary correlators as in [5, 8], or ratios across different primes with additional dynamical enhancement factors.

## 5. Results

All numbers below are computed in Section 4 with the stated inputs; none are simulated or fitted.

**R1 (tree geometry, exact).** $\mathcal{T}_p$ has degree $p+1$; ball sizes $B_2(n) = 3 \cdot 2^n - 2$ and $B_3(n) = 2 \cdot 3^n - 1$; adjacency spectrum $[-2\sqrt{p},\,2\sqrt{p}]$ with $\rho_2 = 2.82843$, $\rho_3 = 3.46410$, $\rho_5 = 4.47214$.

**R2 (Koide, computed).** $Q = 0.66669$ from PDG masses, deviating from $2/3$ by $2.1 \times 10^{-5}$ (relative $3.2 \times 10^{-5}$).

**R3 (semigroup vs. leptons, computed).** Best $\mathbb{T}_{2,3,5}$ approximants: $m_\tau/m_\mu \approx 2^4 = 16$ (error $5.1\%$), $m_\mu/m_e \approx 2^3 3^3 = 216$ (error $4.3\%$), $m_\tau/m_e \approx 3^3 5^3 = 3375$ (error $3.0\%$). All three errors are mutually inconsistent as a multiplicative chain: $16 \times 216 = 3456$ vs. $3375$, a $2.4\%$ internal inconsistency, and the product of best-approximant errors does not cancel.

**R4 (semigroup vs. quarks, computed).** $m_d/m_u \approx 2$ (error $8.1\%$), $m_s/m_d \approx 2^2 \cdot 5 = 20$ (error $0.10\%$), $m_s/m_u \approx 2^3 \cdot 5 = 40$ (error $8.2\%$). The $s/d$ hit at $0.10\%$ is the single best semigroup match found; the $d/u$ and $s/u$ errors are an order of magnitude worse.

**R5 (spectral bound, computed).** Free-operator spectral radii satisfy $\rho_{p'}/\rho_p \leq \sqrt{5/2} = 1.58114$ for $p,p' \in \{2,3,5\}$; observed lepton mass ratios exceed this by factors of $10.6$ ($16.8/1.581$) to $2199$ ($3477/1.581$). The free tree spectrum cannot account for the hierarchy.

**R6 (projection, labeled).** If a dynamical operator on $\mathcal{T}_p$ with coupling $g$ shifts the spectral edge by $O(g)$ (as the one-loop potential of [8] does for bulk scalars), the ratio span could be enhanced by $O(1/g)$ factors. Assuming $g \sim 10^{-2}$, the achievable span is $O(10^2)$, which is the right order for $m_\mu/m_e$ but not for $m_\tau/m_e$ without $g \sim 10^{-3}$. This is a projection with stated assumptions, not a result: it indicates the parameter range a dynamical encoding must target, and carries order-of-magnitude uncertainty of at least a factor of a few.

## 6. Discussion

**Limitations.** Our semigroup search was restricted to $|a|,|b|,|c| \leq 4$ for leptons and small exponents for quarks; a larger search would find better approximants, but this is precisely the danger: $\mathbb{R}_{\mathbb{T}}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ (as $a \to \infty$ with $b,c$ fixed, ratios $2^a 3^b 5^c / 2^{a'} 3^{b'} 5^{c'}$ accumulate), so with enough exponents any ratio can be approximated arbitrarily well. Unbounded searches make the hypothesis unfalsifiable; our bounded search is the honest version, and it reports $3$–$8\%$ errors with no pattern of improvement toward the empirically sharpest relation (Koide's $3 \times 10^{-5}$).

**Failure modes of the encoding idea.** Three specific failures emerged. (i) The internal inconsistency of R3: the best pairwise approximants do not compose ($16 \times 216 = 3456 \neq 3375$), so the semigroup assignment is not coherent as a multiplicative structure — a genuine encoding should be. (ii) The spectral bound R5 rules out the most natural identification of masses with free-tree eigenvalues. (iii) The many-to-one universality of sub-tree effective theories [6] suggests that sub-geometric choices may not leave distinguishable traces in boundary numbers, weakening the map from tree arithmetic to observable masses.

**What would falsify our conclusions.** Our claim is that the naive semigroup encoding fails. This would be overturned if (a) a principled, non-tunable weighting of tree observables (e.g., heat-kernel traces at fixed proper time, or boundary two-point functions as in [5]) reproduced the Koide value $Q = 0.66669$ to better than $10^{-3}$ without fitting; or (b) a dynamical operator with a coupling fixed independently (e.g., by the one-loop potential of [8]) produced spectral ratios matching all three lepton ratios within their experimental errors simultaneously. Neither exists today; both are well-posed targets.
