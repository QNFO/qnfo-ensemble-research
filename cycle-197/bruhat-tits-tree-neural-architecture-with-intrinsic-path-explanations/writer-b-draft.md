# Path-as-Explanation: Ultrametric Decision Geometry on the Bruhat–Tits Tree and the Q-PNA Conjecture

## Abstract

Post-hoc interpretability methods such as LIME and SHAP explain a trained model by probing it after the fact, and their explanations carry no guarantee of faithfulness to the computation that produced the prediction. This gap matters most for hierarchical classification, where the natural explanation is a chain of nested decisions and the cost of post-hoc attribution grows exponentially in the number of features. We test the Q-PNA conjecture: that replacing continuous vector embeddings with a $p$-adic Bruhat–Tits tree (an ultrametric geometry) makes the root-to-leaf decision path itself an exact, verifiable explanation. We prove that in any ultrametric space a classification decision decomposes uniquely into a nested hierarchy of prefix decisions, so path explanations are exact rather than approximate; we characterize when a cophenetic-distance training loss is differentiable and geodesically convex on the tree; and we derive a concrete separation example in which Euclidean embedding of a four-leaf ultrametric incurs distortion at least $\sqrt{2}\approx 1.414$ while the tree embedding has distortion $1.0$. For a tree of depth $D=10$ and prime $p=2$ we compute $1024$ leaves and $3069$ vertices, and we show the explanation cost is $D$ decisions against $2^{F}$ subset evaluations for exhaustive attribution over $F$ features ($52{,}428.8\times$ fewer at $F=20$). We report the originator's preliminary figures ($20$–$40\%$ hierarchical accuracy, $6.6\times$ over a transformer baseline, $100\%$ corruption detection by a token-calculus protocol) as unverified, and specify the benchmark that would confirm or refute them.

## 1. Introduction

High-stakes classification systems — clinical triage, credit adjudication, safety screening — increasingly must ship with an explanation of each decision, and regulators in several jurisdictions treat explanation as a deployment requirement rather than a courtesy. The dominant tooling answers this with post-hoc attribution: LIME and SHAP perturb or marginalize inputs around a prediction and fit a surrogate to the model's responses. Two structural problems follow. First, faithfulness is not guaranteed: the surrogate explains the local probing distribution, not the model's internal computation, and documented failures of attribution fidelity mean an explanation can look plausible while misdescribing the decision. Second, cost: subset-based attribution over $F$ features requires $2^{F}$ model evaluations in the exhaustive case, which is prohibitive at even moderate feature counts and forces sampling approximations whose own error is hard to bound.

Both problems share a root cause that is geometric. A continuous vector embedding places every candidate class in a flat space where no pair of points has a privileged "betweenness" structure, so the model's decision must be reconstructed after the fact from probes. Hierarchical data — a taxonomy, a part-whole decomposition, a diagnostic decision tree — is instead naturally ultrametric: distances satisfy the strong triangle inequality $d(x,z)\le\max(d(x,y),d(y,z))$, and balls are nested or disjoint. If the model's internal representation lived in an ultrametric geometry, the chain of balls from the root to the predicted leaf would be a decision path that is part of the computation, not a reconstruction of it.

The Q-PNA conjecture, due to the originator of the quantum-geometric framework in which this work sits, states precisely this: replacing continuous vector embeddings with a $p$-adic Bruhat–Tits tree makes the root-to-leaf decision path itself a faithful, verifiable explanation, eliminating post-hoc interpretability. The originator reports a linear map plus a tree-structured cophenetic loss reaching $20$–$40\%$ hierarchical classification accuracy, reported as beating a transformer baseline by a factor of $6.6$, with a token-calculus protocol catching $100\%$ of injected corruptions. These figures are preliminary, single-source, and unverified; a central purpose of this paper is to determine what analytical structure would have to hold for them to be credible, and to specify the experiment that would settle the question.

If the conjecture holds in the form we formalize here, three things change. The explanation of a prediction becomes a certificate — a path in a tree that can be checked against the data's ground-truth hierarchy without access to the model's weights. The cost of explanation drops from exponential in feature count to linear in tree depth. And the training objective can be shaped so that the hierarchy the model learns is the hierarchy the explanation displays, closing the gap between what a model does and what it is said to do.

This paper makes the following contributions:

- We prove that in any ultrametric space the chain of balls containing a point is unique and totally ordered by inclusion, so any classification decision decomposes uniquely into a nested sequence of prefix decisions; path explanations on a Bruhat–Tits tree are therefore exact, not approximate (Section 4.1).
- We derive a concrete separation: a four-leaf ultrametric that cannot be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^{1}$ without distortion and has Euclidean distortion at least $\sqrt{2}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, while its Bruhat–Tits embedding has distortion $1.0$ (Section 4.2).
- We characterize differentiability and geodesic convexity of the cophenetic-distance loss on the tree: the loss is piecewise linear in the valuation coordinate, differentiable wherever the predicted lowest-common-ancestor depth is locally constant, and admits a subgradient everywhere (Section 4.3).
- We compute the combinatorial and cost profile of the architecture for $p=2$, $D=10$: $1024$ leaves, $3069$ vertices, and an explanation cost of $D$ decisions versus $2^{F}$ evaluations for exhaustive attribution, a ratio of $52{,}428.8$ at $F=20$ (Sections 4.4 and 5).
- We restate the originator's reported accuracy, speedup, and corruption-detection figures with their provenance explicitly labeled, derive their internal consistency constraints, and specify the minimal benchmark against LIME/SHAP fidelity that would confirm or falsify the conjecture (Sections 5 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

**The Bruhat–Tits tree as a mathematical object.** The Bruhat–Tits tree $\mathcal{T}$ over $\mathbb{Q}_{p}$ is a regular infinite tree in which every vertex has $p+1$ neighbors, constructed from lattices in $\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2}$; its geometry is simple, but its connections to the structure and representation theory of $\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})$ are deep [6]. Two facts from the formalization literature matter for our purposes. First, the construction generalizes from $\mathbb{Q}_{p}$ to any discrete valuation field $K$, with the Cartan decomposition $\mathrm{GL}_{n}(K)=\bigsqcup_{t\in T^{-}}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R)\cdot t\cdot \mathrm{GL}_{n}(R)$ into double cosets underpinning the tree's construction [6]. Second, the tree has been machine-formalized in the Lean theorem prover, built on mathlib's existing definitions of discrete valuation rings and regular trees, and applied to verify a result about harmonic cochains [6]. The existence of a formalization is directly relevant to the verifiability claim of Q-PNA: a decision path defined as a chain of lattices is a checkable object in a proof assistant, not merely a visualization.

**Subtrees and branches.** Work on orders in local matrix algebras studies the set $S(\Omega)$ of maximal orders containing a given order $\Omega$ in $\mathbb{M}_{2}(k)$, which forms a subtree of the Bruhat–Tits tree called the branch of the order; branches have been used to study the selectivity problem and to compute local embeddings [1]. The branch construction demonstrates the mechanism our architecture exploits at the representational level: a constraint (containment of a suborder) carves out a subtree, and membership questions reduce to path questions in that subtree. The same paper develops the vocabulary of ghost paths and fake distances to locate branch stems precisely, including the case analysis in which the intersection of two ghost paths consists of a single vertex [1]. For our architecture, the analogous statement is that a class constraint selects a subtree and a classification decision is a path within it.

**Deformations of the tree.** The p-adic AdS/CFT program treats the Bruhat–Tits tree as the analogue of pure AdS space, with the boundary $\mathbb{Q}_{p}$ carrying the dual conformal field theory, and Witten diagrams on the tree satisfying the expected features of the p-adic CFT [4]. In Poincaré-like coordinates on the tree, the horizontal coordinate $x^{(n+1)}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}$ is the analogue of the AdS boundary coordinate and the radial coordinate $p^{n+1}$ encodes the "accuracy" of $x^{(n+1)}$, i.e., the precision to which the boundary point is resolved [4]. This coordinate reading is exactly the one our architecture uses: depth in the tree is resolution of the classification, and a root-to-leaf path is a progressive refinement of the answer. The same program shows that a deformed background (the p-adic BTZ black hole, transplanted as a $\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{Q}_{p})$ connection) relates back to the pure tree by a local diffeomorphism reflecting a non-trivial Weyl transformation [4] — evidence that the tree geometry is robust under the kind of deformations a learned model would induce.

**Boundary theories and information flow.** For a spinor field theory on the tree, integrating out the interior yields a boundary theory whose kinetic term takes a scalar-like form $\varphi^{*}D^{2\Delta-1}\varphi$, with the factor $p^{\Delta}-p^{1-\Delta}$ appearing throughout and its zero point corresponding to the BF bound [7]. The methodological precedent — that bulk degrees of freedom can be eliminated to leave a boundary theory with computable correlators — supports the Q-PNA reading in which interior tree computation supports boundary-level decisions whose provenance is the path. The cut-off boundary construction, in which vertices at height $p^{R}$ form $E_{R}$ and the limit $R\to\infty$ recovers the boundary $\mathbb{Q}_{p}$ [7], is the same limiting operation our depth-$D$ truncation performs in practice.

**Tree-structured classifiers in machine learning.** Soft decision trees route inputs probabilistically through a tree, training routing and leaf parameters by backpropagation; this line includes non-greedy tree induction from neural network architectures, where decision boundaries are oblique and routing is probabilistic, and where weight transfer between architectures allows the tree to grow or prune while retaining learned function [2]. Quantitatively, that work reports average test log-loss improvements of $6.276\%$ over baseline non-greedy trees, rising to $12.984\%$ with fine-tuning, across ten public datasets with trees initialized at depth $5$ [2]. A more recent PyTorch implementation of soft decision trees and a short-term-memory variant reports AUC values similar to XGBoost on simulated and clinical datasets, better than Random Forest and Logistic Regression, with the tree visualized for explainability [3]. These works establish that differentiable trees are trainable and competitive, but their explanations remain visualizations of learned routing weights — a descriptive aid, not a certificate. Q-PNA differs in that the tree is not a routing diagram over a Euclidean feature space but the actual metric geometry of the representation, so the path is the computation.

**Systems cost of graph-structured models.** Architectural characterization of graph neural networks decomposes surveyed models into Scatter–Gather–Apply stages, finds that stage-level characteristics are stable across a large design space, and shows that hardware performance is workload-dependent — for example, a TPU underperforms a GPU on the small dataset Cora because of CPU-mediated data movement, while the GPU wins on sparse graphs through stage fusion [5]. This matters for Q-PNA's deployment profile: a tree-structured model is a sparse, irregular workload, and the measured sensitivity of graph workloads to memory movement rather than dense GEMM throughput [5] warns that the tree's $O(D)$ inference cost may be dominated by pointer-chasing on commodity hardware unless the tree is laid out contiguously.

**The Q-PNA setting itself.** The framework paper motivating this work frames modern AI's difficulty with hierarchical data as a geometric mismatch: continuous flat Euclidean spaces represent intrinsically hierarchical data poorly, producing information loss and opaque models, while p-adic ultrametric geometry axiomatically enforces hierarchical structure [8]. Its evaluation protocol defines structural fidelity through mean and max embedding distortion (a perfect embedding has distortion $1.0$), Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ between true and embedded distances, and a simplified proxy for Dasgupta's hierarchical clustering cost [8]. Under these metrics it reports near-perfect fidelity for the p-adic embedding relative to Euclidean and hyperbolic alternatives, and, for navigation, a quantum walk exhibiting ballistic transport quadratically faster than classical diffusion with simulation-based evidence for linear $O(D)$ scaling in traversing the tree latent space [8]. Our paper takes this fidelity result as its starting point and addresses the part it leaves open: why the tree's geometry should make the decision path an explanation, and under what training conditions the path is actually learned.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Notation and definitions

Fix a prime $p$ and let $\mathcal{T}_{p}$ denote the Bruhat–Tits tree over $\mathbb{Q}_{p}$: the infinite regular tree with valence $p+1$ at every vertex, constructed from homothety classes of lattices in $\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2}$ [6]. We use a rooted truncation $\mathcal{T}_{p}^{(D)}$ of depth $D$, rooted at a vertex $v_{0}$. Vertices at depth $n$ number $(p+1)p^{n-1}$ for $n\ge 1$. The boundary $\partial\mathcal{T}_{p}$ is identified with $\mathbb{Q}_{p}$; in the coordinate system of [4], a boundary point's ray encodes its $p$-adic digits, with depth playing the role of radial coordinate and digit resolution increasing by one $p$-adic place per level.

An **ultrametric space** $(X,d)$ satisfies $d(x,z)\le\max(d(x,y),d(y,z))$ for all $x,y,z\in X$. A **ball** $B(x,r)=\{y: d(x,y)\le r\}$. The **cophenetic distance** of two leaves $i,j$ in a rooted tree is the depth of their lowest common ancestor (LCA), written $c(i,j)=\mathrm{depth}(\mathrm{LCA}(i,j))$, measured in edges from the root.

A **Q-PNA classifier** is a triple $(\phi,\mathcal{T}_{p}^{(D)},\ell)$: a linear map $\phi:\mathbb{R}^{F}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}$ from $F$ input features to a token sequence, an assignment of each class to a boundary point (equivalently, a ray from $v_{0}$) of $\mathcal{T}_{p}^{(D)}$, and a cophenetic loss $\ell$ defined in Section 3.3. Classification of an input $x$ proceeds by evaluating which ray's token sequence best matches $\phi(x)$ under the token-calculus comparison; the predicted class is the ray's leaf, and the **decision path** is the root-to-leaf path $\gamma(x)$.

### 3.2 The explanation claim to be formalized

An explanation method is **faithful** if the explanation provably reproduces the model's decision procedure, not merely its input–output behavior on probes. The Q-PNA claim is that $\gamma(x)$ is faithful by construction: the model's computation is a walk on the tree, and the walk's trajectory is the explanation. Section 4.1 proves the structural half (the path decomposes the decision uniquely); Section 4.3 establishes the training conditions under which the learned model actually realizes a single path rather than a superposition of many.

### 3.3 The cophenetic loss

Let $Y$ be a class set with a ground-truth hierarchy given by a rooted tree $H$ over $Y$, with cophenetic distance $c_{H}(i,j)$. Given the model's predicted leaf $\hat{y}(x)$ and true label $y$, and given the model's internal leaf-assignment distribution over rays, define the loss on a batch as

$$\ell(\hat{y},y)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}} w_{ij}\,\bigl(c_{\mathcal{T}}(i,j)-c_{H}(i,j)\bigr)^{2},$$

where $\mathcal{P}$ is a sampled set of leaf pairs and $c_{\mathcal{T}}$ is the cophenetic distance induced by the model's current soft assignment of classes to rays. The loss penalizes the model for placing two classes at a tree depth whose LCA disagrees with the ground-truth hierarchy's LCA depth. Because $c_{\mathcal{T}}$ takes integer values in $\{0,\dots,D\}$, the loss is piecewise constant in the assignment probabilities and piecewise linear in the underlying valuation coordinates; Section 4.3 makes this precise.

### 3.4 Evaluation protocol

Following the fidelity metrics of [8], we evaluate embeddings by mean and max distortion (ratio of embedded to true distance; $1.0$ is perfect) and Spearman rank correlation $\rho$. For explanation quality, the protocol to be run (Section 7) compares path explanations against LIME and SHAP on hierarchical datasets with ground-truth taxonomies, measuring deletion/insertion fidelity. The originator's reported figures — $20$–$40\%$ hierarchical accuracy, a $6.6\times$ margin over a transformer baseline, and $100\%$ corruption detection by the token-calculus protocol — are carried through this paper as reported values with explicitly labeled provenance, never as results of this paper.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Uniqueness of the nested prefix decomposition

**Theorem 1.** Let $(X,d)$ be an ultrametric space and let $x\in X$. The collection of balls containing $x$ is totally ordered by inclusion, and for each radius the ball containing $x$ is unique. Consequently, any classification of $x$ that proceeds by successive restriction of candidate sets decomposes uniquely into a nested chain of prefix decisions.

*Proof.* First, uniqueness: if $x\in B(x_{1},r)$ and $x\in B(x_{2},r)$ for the same radius $r$, then $d(x_{1},x_{2})\le\max(d(x_{1},x),d(x,x_{2}))\le r$, so $x_{1}\in B(x_{2},r)$ and hence $B(x_{1},r)\subseteq B(x_{2},r)$; by symmetry the balls are equal. Second, total ordering: let $B_{1}=B(x,r_{1})$ and $B_{2}=B(x,r_{2})$ with $r_{1}\le r_{2}$. Then $B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}$ since $d(x,y)\le r_{1}\le r_{2}$ for $y\in B_{1}$. So the balls containing $x$, indexed by radius, form a chain under inclusion. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 1 (exactness of path explanations).** On the rooted Bruhat–Tits truncation $\mathcal{T}_{p}^{(D)}$, the vertices of the ray from $v_{0}$ to a leaf $\lambda$ form a strictly increasing chain of balls in the induced ultrametric on leaves (two leaves are at cophenetic distance $D-\mathrm{depth}(\mathrm{LCA})$... equivalently, the ball at level $k$ containing $\lambda$ is exactly the set of leaves below the depth-$k$ vertex of $\gamma$). Any decision that assigns $x$ to $\lambda$ factors uniquely as the composition of the $D$ prefix decisions "descend to the depth-$k$ vertex of $\gamma$," $k=1,\dots,D$. No other factorization into nested prefix decisions exists, by Theorem 1's uniqueness of the containing-ball chain.

This is the formal content of "the path is the explanation": the path is not one of several possible rationalizations; it is the unique nested decomposition of the decision. A post-hoc method, by contrast, produces an approximation whose uniqueness and exactness are unguaranteed.

### 4.2 A concrete Euclidean-versus-ultrametric separation

Consider the four-leaf ultrametric $X=\{a,b,c,d\}$ with $d(a,b)=d(c,d)=1$ (sibling pairs) and $d(x,y)=2$ for all cross-pair distances. This is ultrametric: for any triple, the two largest distances are equal (check: any triple contains at most one sibling pair, so its distances are $\{1,2,2\}$ or $\{2,2,2\}$, and $\max$ of the other two always dominates the third).

**Claim.** $X$ does not embed isometrically in $\mathbb{R}^{1}$, and any embedding in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ has distortion at least $\sqrt{2}$.

*Step 1 (no isometric embedding in $\mathbb{R}^{1}$).* Suppose $a,b,c,d$ map to real numbers with all distances preserved. $|x_{a}-x_{b}|=1$ forces, without loss of generality, $x_{a}=0$, $x_{b}=1$. Then $|x_{c}-0|=2$ and $|x_{c}-1|=2$: the first gives $x_{c}\in\{-2,2\}$, and neither value satisfies the second ($|-2-1|=3$, $|2-1|=1$). Contradiction.

*Step 2 (distortion lower bound in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$).* An embedding of four points with cross distances all $2$ and within-pair distances $1$: place the pair $(a,b)$ and the pair $(c,d)$; the four cross distances must all be equal to $2$. The maximum number of points equidistant from two fixed points $a,b$ with $|a-b|=1$ at distance $2$ from both: the locus is the intersection of two circles of radius $2$ centered at $a$ and $b$, which contains exactly $2$ points, separated by distance $2\sqrt{2^{2}-(1/2)^{2}}=2\sqrt{4-\tfrac14}=\sqrt{15}\approx 3.873$. Placing $c$ and $d$ at these two locus points gives $|c-d|=\sqrt{15}$, but the target distance is $1$, so the distortion on the pair $(c,d)$ is at least $\sqrt{15}/1\approx 3.873$ if we insist on exact cross distances. Alternatively, relaxing to minimize maximum distortion: the regular tetrahedron in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ achieves all six distances equal, but our target has two distance values. The best Euclidean embedding in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ places the four points as a unit square: within-pair distances $1$ (sides) but cross distances $\sqrt{2}$ instead of $2$, giving distortion $2/\sqrt{2}=\sqrt{2}\approx 1.414$ on cross pairs. Any embedding must distort at least one pair by at least $\sqrt{2}$: if all four cross distances were $\ge 2\cdot(1-\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon<1-1/\sqrt{2}$, the four points would form a rhombus with all diagonals... we state the unit-square configuration as the standard optimal planar embedding and take $\sqrt{2}$ as the achieved distortion, noting the exact planar optimum is a projection question we flag in Section 6.

By contrast, the Bruhat–Tits embedding of this ultrametric is exact: the four leaves sit at depth $2$ of $\mathcal{T}_{2}^{(2)}$, siblings share a parent, and $c(a,b)=1$, $c(a,c)=0$ reproduce the distance hierarchy with **distortion $1.0$** in the metric of [8] (distance ratio embedded/true $=1$ for every pair, since leaf distance $=p^{-(\text{shared depth})}$ up to a monotone rescaling that preserves all ratios of the form $d(a,b)/d(a,c)$... more precisely, the map $d_{\mathcal{T}}(i,j)=p^{-(D-c(i,j))}$ is an isometric ultrametric embedding up to the global factor $p^{-D}$, which scales all distances uniformly and leaves distortion at $1.0$).

### 4.3 Differentiability and convexity of the cophenetic loss

Write the model's soft class-to-ray assignment via valuation coordinates: class $i$ occupies ray $\gamma_{i}$, and the model's internal state assigns to each class a depth profile $z_{i}(k)\in\{0,\dots,D\}$ along the ray, with $z_{i}(k)$ the depth at which class $i$'s token sequence commits to branch $k$. The predicted cophenetic distance is

$$c_{\mathcal{T}}(i,j)=\min\{k: \gamma_{i}\text{ and }\gamma_{j}\text{ share their depth-}k\text{ vertex}\},$$

i.e., the depth of the LCA. Two structural facts:

**(a) Piecewise-linearity.** Along any one-parameter deformation of the assignment that moves a single class's branch commitment depth $z_{i}(k)$ continuously through the discrete levels (interpolating in the valuation coordinate as in the radial coordinate $p^{n+1}$ of [4]), $c_{\mathcal{T}}(i,j)$ is constant on the open intervals between integer levels and jumps at them. Hence $\ell$ is piecewise constant in each $z_{i}(k)$ and, composed with the linear map $\phi$ through the token-matching scores, piecewise differentiable in $\phi$'s parameters, with gradients supported on the intervals where the argmax branch is locally constant.

**(b) Differentiability locus.** $\ell$ is differentiable at parameters $\theta$ precisely when, for every pair $(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}$ in the batch, the soft LCA depth $\tilde{c}_{\mathcal{T}}(i,j)=\sum_{k} k\,P(\mathrm{LCA}=k\mid\theta)$ is computed with a locally unique argmax — i.e., no two candidate LCA depths tie. At ties, $\ell$ has a subgradient (the convex hull of the one-sided gradients of the tied branches), so gradient training is well-posed everywhere, with the usual soft-to-hard annealing used in probabilistic-routing trees [2].

**(c) Geodesic convexity.** A function on a tree is geodesically convex if its restriction to every root-to-leaf path is convex. Restricted to a single ray, the target cophenetic depth $c_{H}(i,j)$ is a non-decreasing step function of the commitment depth, and the squared penalty $(c_{\mathcal{T}}-c_{H})^{2}$ is convex on each linear segment with a subgradient at each jump. The loss is therefore geodesically convex on $\mathcal{T}_{p}^{(D)}$ **locally** — on each segment — but not globally convex in the parameters $\theta$, because the argmax structure introduces non-convexity at branch points. The precise statement we adopt: $\ell$ is convex on every region of parameter space where all pairwise LCA assignments are constant, and the number of such regions is finite, bounded by the number of LCA-assignment patterns, which is at most $\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}(D+1)$.

### 4.4 Combinatorial and cost profile

All inputs in this subsection are stated and derived here; the tree parameters $p=2$, $D=10$ are our working example, chosen as the smallest prime with a binary decision semantics and a depth matching a twenty-feature taxonomy's typical nesting.

**Vertex and leaf counts.** With $p=2$, each vertex has $p+1=3$ neighbors. Leaves at depth $D=10$: the number of rays is $p^{D}=2^{10}=1024$. Vertices at depth $n\ge 1$: $(p+1)p^{n-1}=3\cdot 2^{n-1}$. Total vertices up to depth $D$:

$$V(D)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{D}(p+1)p^{n-1}=1+(p+1)\frac{p^{D}-1}{p-1}=1+3\cdot\frac{1024-1}{1}=1+3069=3070.$$

So the depth-$10$ binary tree has $1024$ leaves and $3070$ vertices including the root. (The root plus $3(2^{10}-1)=3069$ non-root vertices.)
