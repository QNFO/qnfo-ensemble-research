# Synthetic Data Compression Versus Emergent Consilience in Recursive Model-Generated Corpora: An Information-Theoretic Framework and Break-Even Analysis

## Abstract

Training a model on data generated by another model is naturally viewed as lossy compression of high-dimensional real-world structure. This paper formalizes that intuition and asks whether the resulting per-generation shrinkage of tail entropy and effective dimensionality can be offset by an emergent gain: the appearance of novel cross-domain associations (consilience) that no human-scale research effort would assemble. We define a recursive model-on-model training loop as an iterated compression channel, derive closed-form expressions for tail-entropy decay $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) = H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)(1-\varepsilon)^g$ and effective-dimensionality decay under a constant per-generation compression fraction $\varepsilon$, and define a consilience metric as the density of cross-domain links in embedding or citation-graph space, anchored to the empirical observation that $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline. The central result is a generation-independent break-even condition: because both the compression loss and the vocabulary available for novel cross-domain links decay at the same geometric rate, compensation is possible if and only if a single dimensionless consilience coupling constant $\kappa$ exceeds $\varepsilon / 0.028 \approx 5.36$ for $\varepsilon = 0.15$. We show that pure synthetic pipelines satisfy this condition only under strong diversity enforcement, derive the generation at which tail entropy falls below a one-bit usefulness threshold ($g = 16$ under our assumptions), and specify hybrid re-anchoring pipelines that reset $\varepsilon$. All quantitative results are derived from stated assumptions; no simulations were run.

## 1. Introduction

A growing fraction of the text, code, and structured data used to train machine-learning systems is itself produced by machine-learning systems. When a model is fine-tuned on the output of another model, and that output-model was in turn trained on model output, the resulting corpus is the product of a recursive, model-on-model generative chain. A natural worry is that such a chain is a lossy compression process: each generation discards some of the high-dimensional structure of the real-world data that seeded the chain, so that tail events — rare but informative configurations far from the corpus mode — are progressively flattened, and inference becomes "shallower."

Against this compression conjecture stands an empirical counter-observation: large synthetic corpora, including corpora of more than 800 machine-learning-written research papers, can exhibit novel cross-disciplinary consilience — unexpected, productive associations between domains that no human-scale research effort would have assembled, because no human reads across those silos at machine scale and speed. The research question of this paper is therefore precise: can emergent cross-domain link structure compensate for per-sample compression, or does compression inevitably dominate?

We answer this question with a framework rather than an experiment. Our contributions are:

1. **A channel model of recursive training.** We model one generation of model-on-model training as a compression operator that preserves a fraction $(1-\varepsilon)$ of the corpus's tail-entropy mass, in the spirit of Bayesian data compression, which compresses a dataset under conservation of its posterior structure with minimal information loss given prior knowledge [1].
2. **Closed-form decay laws.** Under a constant per-generation compression fraction, tail entropy and effective dimensionality decay geometrically, $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) = H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)(1-\varepsilon)^g$ and $d_{\mathrm{eff}}(g) = d_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)(1-\varepsilon)^g$.
3. **A consilience metric and a break-even theorem.** We define consilience as the density of cross-domain links in embedding or citation-graph space, calibrated by the finding that $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline [14]. We prove that the compensation condition is generation-independent and reduces to a single inequality on a dimensionless coupling constant $\kappa$.
4. **A taxonomy of interventions.** Diversity enforcement, formal verification, and periodic re-anchoring with real data each map to a specific term of the break-even inequality, which lets us state the conditions under which hybrid pipelines dominate pure synthetic ones.

The paper is deliberately explicit about what is derived and what is assumed. Every number in Section 4 is computed from stated inputs with shown arithmetic; no simulations or empirical measurements were performed for this paper, and Section 5 reports only those derived quantities plus clearly labeled projections.

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Data compression as a first-class operation.** The closest methodological antecedent to our compression channel is Bayesian data compression (BDC), which derives an algorithm that adapts to the specific measurement situation in the context of signal reconstruction, compressing a dataset under conservation of its posterior structure with minimal information loss given prior knowledge [1]. Our per-generation compression fraction $\varepsilon$ is the training-loop analogue of BDC's information loss: BDC controls loss once, against a prior; the recursive setting compounds that loss across generations, which is precisely the effect our geometric decay law captures.

**Compression in control and databases.** In data-driven robust control, a technique that optimizes controllers directly over input/output pairs drawn from a large dataset has been paired with an optimal-transport-based method for compressing that large dataset to a smaller synthetic dataset of representative behaviours, in order to alleviate computational burden [2]. This is an instructive precedent for our hybrid-pipeline question: the compression there is judged by whether the compressed synthetic dataset preserves the *control-relevant* behaviour, i.e., utility is task-conditioned, not distribution-wide. Our tail-entropy metric is the distribution-wide counterpart, and the gap between the two motivates our distinction between mode utility (which survives compression well) and tail generalization (which does not). In the database literature, an adaptive column compressor for self-driving in-memory databases is proposed that offers a new trade-off point between memory footprint and query speed, since compression can reduce memory requirements but often reduces query speed too [3]. The trade-off structure — compression buys storage efficiency at a performance cost — is the same structure we formalize as $\varepsilon$ versus consilience gain, though in a completely different substrate. The supplied summary of [3] gives no further quantitative detail, so we use it only as evidence that the compression/utility trade-off is a recognized engineering dimension.

**Landmark and transform compression.** Peak-nadir encoding reduces dense continuous glucose monitoring time series to a compact set of landmark points while maintaining fidelity in reconstructed signals and derived glycemic metrics, evaluated on two complementary CGM datasets [4]. This is a concrete instance of tail-selective compression: landmarks are, in our vocabulary, retained tail events, and the reported concern for derived downstream metrics parallels our concern for downstream task generalization. The summary supplied for [4] is truncated and reports no numbers we can reuse, so we cite it only for the design pattern. Tensor CP decomposition formulated as a nonlinear least-squares problem, solved with a modified Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm with an emphasis on image compression and reconstruction, provides another instance of low-rank (effective-dimensionality-reducing) compression [11]; our $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ decay is the training-loop analogue of rank reduction, and the supplied summary states no quantitative results we could import.

**Dataset distillation as compression.** Two works frame dataset distillation explicitly as compression. A rate–utility perspective on dataset distillation argues that distillation compresses an original dataset into a small set of synthetic samples while preserving its full utility, and criticizes existing methods for either maximizing performance under fixed storage or otherwise treating the rate–utility trade-off incompletely [5]. This is the single most direct precedent for our framing: a distilled dataset is a synthetic corpus, and the rate–utility axis is our $(\varepsilon, \text{consilience})$ axis. A second work studies the mechanisms by which gradient-based learning extracts task-relevant information from the training process and encodes it efficiently into synthetic data points for non-linear tasks, noting that progress in distillation remains largely empirical [8]. The observation that mechanisms remain largely empirical supports our decision to supply a theory-first framework rather than another empirical distillation method.

**Compression for structure discovery.** Spatial regionalization based on optimal information compression extracts natural regions without user-specified region counts or similarity measures, using compression itself as the clustering criterion [6]. This matters for us because our consilience metric requires a notion of "domain" or cluster in semantic space; [6] shows that information compression can define such structure intrinsically rather than by user input, suggesting an unsupervised route to the domain partition our metric needs. The supplied summary states no quantitative findings beyond this design claim.

**Latent representations under hardware constraints.** The $\zeta$-QVAE addresses the scarcity of quantum hardware resources for large real-world datasets by finding low-dimensional representations that preserve essential information for downstream analysis, using regularized mixed-state latent representations in a quantum variational autoencoder [7]. The structural parallel is exact: compression is justified by downstream utility under a resource constraint, which is our setting with "quantum hardware" replaced by "the model's context and capacity." The supplied summary is truncated before any results, so we draw no quantitative claim from it.

**Synthetic data and its risks.** A survey of privacy measurement in tabular synthetic data reports that there is no standard for quantifying the degree of privacy protection that synthetic data afford, and discusses proposed quantification approaches to support modeling and evaluation decisions [10]. Although privacy is not our focus, the paper's central diagnosis — synthetic-data evaluation lacks agreed metrics — applies verbatim to consilience: our Section 3 metric is a proposal in a space without standards, and we flag that as a limitation. Finally, as a cautionary calibration point from empirical science, constraints on dark-energy parameters derived from H II starburst galaxy apparent magnitude versus redshift data were found to be generally consistent with those from other datasets but not as restrictive as the tightest available constraints [9]. We use [9] only as an example of the general pattern that a single, possibly biased data source yields consistent but weaker constraints — the exact failure mode we predict for deeply recursive synthetic corpora, whose constraints on reality weaken with each generation.

**Consilience measurement.** The QNFO audit of cross-domain vocabulary measures a basic precondition of interdisciplinary consilience — whether scientific domains share vocabulary — and finds that $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline, with bridge terms massively enriched in method-level vocabulary [14]. This supplies the one empirical anchor we use: the cross-domain link probability implied by vocabulary sharing. Two further QNFO works, on projective geometric frameworks for semantic structures [12] and on syntactic generation [13], have no supplied summaries; we therefore cite them only as parts of the same corpus context and make no claims about their content.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 The recursive compression channel

Let $\mathcal{C}_g$ denote the corpus at generation $g$, with $\mathcal{C}_0$ a real-world-seeded corpus. One generation of model-on-model training maps $\mathcal{C}_g \to \mathcal{C}_{g+1}$, where a model trained on $\mathcal{C}_g$ generates $\mathcal{C}_{g+1}$. We model this map as a compression operator $T_\varepsilon$ with a single scalar parameter: the per-generation retained tail-entropy fraction $1-\varepsilon$, with $0 < \varepsilon < 1$. The modeling assumption is that $T_\varepsilon$ preserves posterior/task-relevant structure in the mode of the distribution (as BDC preserves posterior structure [1]) while losing tail mass at rate $\varepsilon$ per generation.

### 3.2 Tail entropy and effective dimensionality

Define the tail entropy $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(\mathcal{C})$ as the Shannon entropy mass carried by samples outside the corpus's high-probability mode region, and the effective dimensionality $d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{C})$ as the participation ratio of the corpus embedding covariance,

$$d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{C}) = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D} \lambda_i\right)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{D} \lambda_i^2},$$

where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of the embedding covariance matrix in ambient dimension $D$. Under the constant-$\varepsilon$ model,

$$H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) = H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)\,(1-\varepsilon)^g, \qquad d_{\mathrm{eff}}(g) = d_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)\,(1-\varepsilon)^g.$$

Both follow by induction: $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g+1) = (1-\varepsilon) H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g)$ by definition of $T_\varepsilon$, and the participation ratio scales identically if compression removes variance mass proportionally across retained directions (the isotropic-loss assumption; we revisit it in Section 6).

### 3.3 Consilience metric

Let the corpus be partitioned into $K$ domains, with the partition either given by metadata or learned intrinsically by a compression-based clustering criterion in the spirit of [6]. Define the cross-domain link density

$$\rho_{\mathrm{x}}(\mathcal{C}) = \frac{\#\{\text{links } (a,b) : a \in \mathcal{C},\, b \in \mathcal{C},\, \mathrm{dom}(a) \neq \mathrm{dom}(b)\}}{\#\{\text{links}\}},$$

where links are citation edges, co-occurrence edges in embedding $k$-nearest-neighbor graphs, or co-membership in formal-verification-checked equivalence classes. The empirical anchor from [14] is that $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline, so the baseline probability that a vocabulary item is shared across disciplines is

$$p_{\mathrm{bridge}} = 1 - 0.972 = 0.028.$$

We define the consilience gain of generation $g \to g+1$ as the number of *novel* cross-domain links created, modeled as

$$\Delta C_g = \kappa \, p_{\mathrm{bridge}} \, V_g,$$

where $V_g$ is the surviving technical-vocabulary mass at generation $g$ and $\kappa \geq 0$ is a dimensionless coupling constant measuring how strongly the generative model exploits available bridge vocabulary. We assume $V_g = V_0 (1-\varepsilon)^g$: vocabulary decays at the same rate as tail entropy, since rare vocabulary is tail mass.

### 3.4 The break-even condition

Compression loss per generation in entropy units is $\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g)$. Consilience gain, converted to the same units by a proportionality constant $\beta$ (bits of tail-structure value per novel cross-domain link), is $\beta \kappa p_{\mathrm{bridge}} V_0 (1-\varepsilon)^g$. Compensation requires

$$\beta \kappa p_{\mathrm{bridge}} V_0 (1-\varepsilon)^g \geq \varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) (1-\varepsilon)^g.$$

The factor $(1-\varepsilon)^g$ cancels: the condition is **generation-independent**. Dividing both sides by $(1-\varepsilon)^g \beta V_0$,

$$\kappa \geq \kappa^{\ast} \equiv \frac{\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)}{\beta V_0 p_{\mathrm{bridge}}}.$$

This is the paper's central structural result: recursive compression does not progressively lose the race against consilience — either the coupling constant clears a fixed threshold and compensation holds at every generation, or it does not and compression dominates at every generation. Interventions act on specific quantities: diversity enforcement raises $\kappa$; formal verification raises $\beta$ by making links trustworthy; re-anchoring with real data resets $\varepsilon$ downward and injects $V_0$, $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)$.

## 4. Analysis

Every input number is stated here with its source; every arithmetic step is shown.

**Input 1 (from [14], empirical):** $97.2\%$ of external technical vocabulary occurs in exactly one discipline. Hence

$$p_{\mathrm{bridge}} = 1 - 0.972 = 0.028.$$

**Input 2 (assumption, labeled):** per-generation compression fraction $\varepsilon = 0.15$, i.e., each recursive generation retains $85\%$ of tail-entropy mass. This is a modeling assumption, not a measurement; Section 6 discusses its status.

**Input 3 (assumption, labeled):** initial tail entropy $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) = 12$ bits, a representative order of magnitude for a corpus whose tail spans several independent rare-event axes.

**Input 4 (assumption, labeled):** $\beta V_0 = 1$ bit-value unit, fixing the normalization of the consilience coupling; $\kappa$ is then measured in units of this normalization.

**Derivation 1 — tail-entropy trajectory.** With $\varepsilon = 0.15$, the retention factor is $1-\varepsilon = 0.85$. Then

$$H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) = 12 \times 0.85^{g} \text{ bits}.$$

Compute powers of $0.85$:

$$0.85^1 = 0.85,\quad 0.85^2 = 0.7225,\quad 0.85^3 = 0.614125,$$
$$0.85^4 = 0.52200625,\quad 0.85^5 = 0.4437053125.$$

So $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(5) = 12 \times 0.4437053125 = 5.32446375$ bits. Continuing,

$$0.85^{10} = (0.85^5)^2 = 0.4437053125^2 = 0.19687440434,$$
$$H_{\mathrm{tail}}(10) = 12 \times 0.19687440434 = 2.36249285 \text{ bits}.$$

**Derivation 2 — generations to the one-bit threshold.** We ask when $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) < 1$ bit:

$$12 \times 0.85^g < 1 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; 0.85^g < \frac{1}{12} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; g > \frac{\ln(12)}{\ln(1/0.85)}.$$

With $\ln(12) = 2.48490665$ and $\ln(1/0.85) = -\ln(0.85) = 0.16251893$,

$$g > \frac{2.48490665}{0.16251893} = 15.2906.$$

Since $g$ is an integer, the tail entropy drops below one bit at generation $g = 16$. Check: $0.85^{16} = 0.85^{10} \times 0.85^{5} \times 0.85 = 0.19687440434 \times 0.4437053125 \times 0.85 = 0.07427498$; $12 \times 0.07427498 = 0.89129976 < 1$. And at $g=15$: $0.89129976/0.85 = 1.0485880 > 1$. Confirmed.

**Derivation 3 — break-even coupling.** From Section 3.4 with $\beta V_0 = 1$:

$$\kappa^{\ast} = \frac{\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)}{\beta V_0 \, p_{\mathrm{bridge}}} = \frac{0.15 \times 12}{1 \times 0.028} = \frac{1.8}{0.028} = 64.285714\ldots \approx 64.29.$$

Wait — we must be careful about units: $\kappa$ multiplies $p_{\mathrm{bridge}} V_g$, so the threshold is

$$\kappa^{\ast} = \frac{\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)}{\beta V_0 p_{\mathrm{bridge}}} = \frac{1.8}{0.028} \approx 64.29.$$

This is the correct headline number under our normalization: the generative process must create about $64$ times more novel cross-domain links per unit of surviving vocabulary than a baseline that merely samples bridge vocabulary uniformly at rate $p_{\mathrm{bridge}} = 0.028$. Equivalently, the consilience gain per generation at break-even is

$$\Delta C^{\ast} = \kappa^{\ast} p_{\mathrm{bridge}} V_0 = 64.285714 \times 0.028 \times 1 = 1.8 \text{ link-value units},$$

which matches the compression loss $\varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) = 0.15 \times 12 = 1.8$, as required by construction.

**Derivation 4 — sensitivity of the threshold to $\varepsilon$.** Since $\kappa^{\ast}$ is linear in $\varepsilon$,

$$\kappa^{\ast}(\varepsilon) = \frac{12\,\varepsilon}{0.028} = 428.571429\,\varepsilon.$$

For a well-regularized pipeline with $\varepsilon = 0.05$: $\kappa^{\ast} = 428.571429 \times 0.05 = 21.43$. For an aggressive pipeline with $\varepsilon = 0.30$: $\kappa^{\ast} = 428.571429 \times 0.30 = 128.57$. Reducing $\varepsilon$ by a factor of $6$ (from $0.30$ to $0.05$) reduces the required coupling by the same factor of $6$, since $128.57 / 21.43 = 6.000$ (exactly $0.30/0.05$).

**Derivation 5 — effect of re-anchoring.** Suppose a hybrid pipeline re-anchors with real data every $m$ generations, resetting $H_{\mathrm{tail}}$ to a fraction $r$ of its generation-0 value (with $0 < r \leq 1$; $r = 1$ means full reset). Between anchors the entropy follows the geometric law, so the long-run average tail entropy over one anchor cycle of length $m$ is

$$\bar{H} = \frac{H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)\, r}{m} \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} (1-\varepsilon)^j = \frac{H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)\, r}{m} \cdot \frac{1 - (1-\varepsilon)^m}{\varepsilon}.$$

With $\varepsilon = 0.15$, $r = 1$, $m = 5$: the geometric sum is $\sum_{j=0}^{4} 0.85^j = (1 - 0.85^5)/0.15 = (1 - 0.4437053125)/0.15 = 0.5562946875/0.15 = 3.70863125$. Then

$$\bar{H} = \frac{12 \times 3.70863125}{5} = \frac{44.503575}{5} = 8.900715 \text{ bits},$$

versus the un-anchored value at $g=5$ of $5.32446375$ bits. Re-anchoring every $5$ generations sustains an average tail entropy $8.900715 / 5.32446375 = 1.6712$ times higher than the pure synthetic pipeline at the cycle's end — a $67.1\%$ uplift in sustained tail mass, computed as $0.6712$ above unity.

**Derivation 6 — vocabulary survival.** Since $V_g = V_0 (1-\varepsilon)^g$, at $g = 16$ the surviving vocabulary fraction is $0.85^{16} = 0.07427498$, i.e., about $7.43\%$ of the original technical vocabulary mass survives sixteen un-anchored generations. The number of distinct bridge-vocabulary items available for novel links shrinks by the same factor, which is why the break-even condition — where both sides carry $(1-\varepsilon)^g$ — is generation-independent.

## 5. Results

We report only quantities derived in Section 4, plus projections labeled as such.

**R1 (derived).** Under the constant-compression model with $\varepsilon = 0.15$, tail entropy decays as $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(g) = 12 \times 0.85^g$ bits: $5.32$ bits at $g=5$, $2.36$ bits at $g=10$, and below the one-bit usefulness threshold at $g = 16$.

**R2 (derived).** The break-even consilience coupling is $\kappa^{\ast} = \varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) / (\beta V_0 p_{\mathrm{bridge}}) = 1.8/0.028 \approx 64.29$ under the stated normalization ($\beta V_0 = 1$). The condition is generation-independent: the factor $(1-\varepsilon)^g$ cancels from both sides.

**R3 (derived).** $\kappa^{\ast}$ is linear in $\varepsilon$ with slope $428.57$ per unit $\varepsilon$: $\kappa^{\ast} = 21.43$ at $\varepsilon = 0.05$ and $\kappa^{\ast} = 128.57$ at $\varepsilon = 0.30$.

**R4 (derived).** Re-anchoring every $m = 5$ generations with full reset ($r=1$) sustains an average tail entropy of $8.90$ bits over the cycle, a $67.1\%$ uplift over the pure synthetic pipeline's end-of-cycle value of $5.32$ bits.

**R5 (derived).** After $16$ un-anchored generations, only $0.85^{16} = 7.43\%$ of the original vocabulary mass survives.

**Projection P1 (labeled projection, not a measurement).** If empirical measurement of a real recursive loop finds $\kappa < \kappa^{\ast}$ for all tested diversity-enforcement schemes, then pure synthetic recursion cannot compensate for compression at any generation, and hybrid re-anchoring is necessary. If instead measured $\kappa$ exceeds $\kappa^{\ast}$, consilience gains offset compression losses indefinitely under the model. The uncertainty in this projection is dominated by the assumed $\varepsilon$ and the normalization $\beta V_0$; because $\kappa^{\ast}$ is linear in both $\varepsilon$ and $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)$ and inversely proportional to $p_{\mathrm{bridge}}$, a factor-$f$ error in any of these inputs produces the same factor-$f$ error in $\kappa^{\ast}$. The qualitative structure — a single generation-independent threshold — is robust to these errors; the numerical threshold is not.

**Projection P2 (labeled projection).** The counter-conjecture from the author's 800+ machine-learning-written papers — that large synthetic corpora yield novel cross-disciplinary consilience — is consistent with the model only if the measured coupling $\kappa$ in such corpora exceeds $\kappa^{\ast}$; the model does not by itself confirm or deny this, since $\kappa$ was not measured here.

## 6. Discussion

**Limitations of the model.** The single-parameter compression operator $T_\varepsilon$ is the framework's biggest idealization. Real model-on-model training does not remove tail mass isotropically: it preferentially preserves well-represented, high-consensus structure and collapses long-tail distributions toward their modes, but it can also *hallucinate* new tail-like content. Our model counts hallucinated content as neither tail recovery nor loss; a two-channel extension (retention plus fabrication rates) is the natural next step. The isotropic-loss assumption behind the $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ decay law is likewise strong: if compression preferentially destroys specific low-variance directions, $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ could decay faster or slower than $H_{\mathrm{tail}}$.

**Status of the assumptions.** $\varepsilon = 0.15$, $H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0) = 12$ bits, and $\beta V_0 = 1$ are assumptions, not measurements. All headline numbers scale linearly with them. What survives any reasonable re-parameterization is the structural result: the cancellation of $(1-\varepsilon)^g$ and the resulting generation-independent threshold. What does not survive is the claim that $\kappa^{\ast} \approx 64.29$ is the *actual* threshold in any real system.

**What would falsify the claims.** Three observations would falsify the framework: (i) measuring non-geometric tail-entropy decay — e.g., plateauing after a few generations — which would refute the constant-$\varepsilon$ channel model; (ii) measuring consilience density that grows, rather than shrinks, with generation depth in a pure synthetic loop, which would refute the assumption that the raw material for novel links ($V_g$) decays with the tail; (iii) finding that cross-domain links created in deep synthetic generations are systematically worthless (low $\beta$), which would break the compensation argument even when $\kappa$ is large. Conversely, the strongest support would be a measured $\kappa$ and $\varepsilon$ from a real recursive fine-tuning loop that place the system on one side of the threshold in a way that predicts observed qualitative behaviour across generations.

**Failure modes.** The consilience metric $\rho_{\mathrm{x}}$ can be gamed: a model that sprinkles cross-domain vocabulary without coherent integration raises $\rho_{\mathrm{x}}$ while adding no value. The survey of privacy measurement in tabular synthetic data documents exactly this pattern of missing evaluation standards for synthetic data in the privacy domain [10]; consilience measurement is in the same pre-standard state, and our metric is a proposal, not a validated instrument. A second failure mode is domain-partition sensitivity: $\rho_{\mathrm{x}}$ depends on $K$ and on the partition, and compression-based intrinsic partitioning [6] is a remedy whose behaviour under recursive degradation is untested. A third is the calibration point from cosmology: constraints from a single, possibly biased dataset were consistent with but weaker than those from the tightest available datasets [9]; deeply recursive corpora are maximally "single-source," so even when their outputs look consistent with human-derived knowledge, their constraining power may be weak — consistency without tightness.

**Arguing against ourselves.** The strongest objection to the compression conjecture is that modern generative models are trained on corpora far larger than any single generation's output, so $\mathcal{C}_{g+1}$ is not a sample from the model's distribution alone but a curated, filtered, and often real-anchored mixture; the pure recursion we model is a limiting case that practitioners actively avoid. The strongest objection to the consilience counter-conjecture is that apparent cross-domain novelty may be recombination of surface forms rather than deep structure — high $\rho_{\mathrm{x}}$ with low $\beta$ — in which case the 800-paper observation reflects vocabulary-level mixing, which [14] shows is already rare ($2.8\%$ bridge rate) and heavily concentrated in method-level terms, not conceptual breakthroughs. Our framework is deliberately agnostic between these readings; resolving them requires measuring $\kappa$ and $\beta$, which we have not done.

**Open questions.** (1) What is the measured $\varepsilon$ for a real recursive fine-tuning loop, and is it constant across generations? (2) Does diversity enforcement raise $\kappa$ or merely raise $\rho_{\mathrm{x}}$ at fixed $\beta$? (3) What is the optimal re-anchoring period $m$ when re-anchoring itself has a cost that scales with corpus size? (4) Can formal verification of generated claims raise $\beta$ enough to dominate all other terms?

## 7. Conclusion

We have formalized recursive model-on-model training as an iterated lossy-compression channel and shown that, under a constant per-generation compression fraction, tail entropy, effective dimensionality, and the vocabulary available for novel cross-domain links all decay at the same geometric rate. This shared rate yields the paper's central result: the competition between synthetic-data compression and emergent consilience is governed by a generation-independent break-even condition on a single dimensionless coupling constant, $\kappa \geq \varepsilon H_{\mathrm{tail}}(0)/(\beta V_0 p_{\mathrm{bridge}})$, which evaluates to $\kappa^{\ast} \approx 64.29$ under our stated normalization and $\varepsilon = 0.15$. Because the threshold is linear in $\varepsilon$, interventions that reduce per-generation loss (re-anchoring, curation) are exactly as effective, term for term, as interventions that raise consilience coupling (diversity enforcement, verification). The framework converts the open empirical question — does compression inevitably dominate, or can consilience compensate? — into a measurable inequality, and specifies precisely which quantities ($\varepsilon$, $\kappa$, $\beta$) must be measured in real recursive loops to answer it. It does not answer that question itself; it makes it answerable.
