# Ultrametric Quantum Computation for Direct Factorization: A Resource Analysis of p-adic Period-Finding Without Phase Estimation

## Abstract

Factoring large integers underpins RSA, and the only known polynomial-time quantum method, Shor's algorithm, factors by estimating eigenvalues of a modular-exponentiation operator via the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) over a register of $2b$ qubits for a $b$-bit modulus. A conjecture in the ultrametric research program holds that a quantum computer whose native state space is $p$-adic rather than Archimedean could access the period of modular exponentiation directly, bypassing phase estimation. This paper formalizes the conjecture as a concrete computational model, evaluates it, and reports a largely negative result. We define an ultrametric register as the vertex set of a Bruhat–Tits tree of depth $d$ and a hierarchical $p$-adic transform on it, and we derive the costs exactly. For a $b$-bit modulus with $p=2$ and $d=2b$, the register has $3 \cdot 2^{2b} - 2$ vertices and the transform costs $3(3 \cdot 2^{2b}-2)$ operations, against $b(2b+1)$ QFT gates for Shor's control register: at $b=8$ the ratio is $4336.9$, and at $b=2048$ it is approximately $10^{1227}$. We further prove a structural obstruction: $p$-adic valuations do not determine residues modulo the period, so no valuation-only measurement can replace phase estimation. The ultrametric formulation is therefore exponentially more expensive unless an unstated compression mechanism exists; we state that mechanism as the open conjecture.

## 1. Introduction

RSA and related cryptosystems derive their security from the assumed hardness of integer factorization, and every organization that deploys RSA today faces a concrete planning problem: if a quantum computer capable of factoring $b$-bit moduli in polynomial time is built, keys must be replaced on a schedule set by that machine's arrival. Shor's algorithm reduces factoring to period-finding — finding the multiplicative order $r$ of a unit $a$ modulo $N$ — and executes it by preparing a superposition over exponents, computing $a^z \bmod N$ into a second register, and applying the QFT over roughly $N^2$ points to estimate an eigenphase from which $r$ is recovered by continued fractions. The phase-estimation detour is the entire quantum content of the algorithm: it converts a periodicity in the exponent into a measurable frequency. Any formulation of period-finding that removes this detour, or replaces it with something cheaper, would change both the gate-count projections used in quantum-threat assessments and the design targets of quantum hardware programs.

The ultrametric research program motivates exactly such a replacement. The adelic-physics argument is that the choice of the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ as the base field of physics is inherited from nineteenth-century analysis rather than from experiment, and that Ostrowski's theorem forces the $p$-adic completions $\mathbb{Q}_p$ to be treated as equally legitimate completions of the rationals [12]. The ultrametric-quantum-computation program proposes Bruhat–Tits trees as a physical substrate, Hecke operators as gates, and a threshold theorem for fault tolerance on the tree [10]. A companion register analysis computes exact gate counts for a finite adelic toy register and reports a structural separation of approximately $2.27 \times 10^8$ between a structured transform and a dense baseline, while explicitly cautioning that this separation is a statement about transform structure, not about physics [11]. The conjecture at issue here is sharper: that superposition itself is an artifact of projecting a discrete, tree-based ontology onto a continuous measurement screen, and that an "adelic quantum computer" could find the period of $a^z \bmod N$ directly on the tree, without eigenvalue estimation.

If that conjecture held, the consequence would be a factoring algorithm with a different resource profile from Shor's — potentially different asymptotics, and certainly different hardware requirements, since a tree-structured register is not a line of qubits. If it fails, the failure mode matters too: it would identify precisely which step of the direct approach is exponentially costly, converting a sweeping ontological claim into a bounded technical statement. Either outcome changes what the ultrametric program can honestly claim.

This paper tests the conjecture by building the smallest complete model of it and costing every component. The contributions are:

- A formal definition of an ultrametric quantum register as the vertex set of a Bruhat–Tits tree $T_{p,d}$ and of a hierarchical $p$-adic transform on it, with exact vertex counts $V(p,d) = 1 + (p+1)\frac{p^d-1}{p-1}$ verified against prior register computations (Section 3, derived in Section 4).
- An exact resource comparison showing that the direct $p$-adic period-finding register for a $b$-bit modulus requires $3 \cdot 2^{2b} - 2$ vertices and $3(3 \cdot 2^{2b}-2)$ transform operations, against $b(2b+1)$ QFT gates in Shor's control register — a ratio of $4336.9$ at $b=8$ and approximately $10^{1227}$ at $b=2048$ (Sections 4 and 5).
- A structural impossibility argument: the map from an integer to its vector of $p$-adic valuations does not determine its residue modulo the period $r$, so no measurement that reads only ultrametric magnitudes can extract $r$; recovering $r$ requires the full $p$-adic digit expansion, which carries the same information as the integer index itself (Section 4, discussed in Section 6).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The mathematical foundation for taking ultrametric state spaces seriously is well developed. [1] proves ultrametric analogues of three classical metric-geometry results: the Arens–Eells isometric embedding theorem, the Hausdorff extension theorem, and the Niemytzki–Tychonoff characterization of compactness, working over ultra-normed modules over arbitrary commutative rings and replacing the Dugundji extension theorem with a zero-dimensional Michael continuous selection theorem. For this paper, [1] establishes that the analytic infrastructure — embeddings, extensions, and function spaces over ultrametric spaces — exists and is rigorous, so defining a register on a Bruhat–Tits tree encounters no metric-geometric obstruction. What [1] does not provide, and does not attempt, is any notion of dynamics or unitary evolution; the obstacle examined here lies exactly there.

On the computational side, the direct-alternative-to-Shor genre has prior art. [3] constructs a quantum simulation of factorization by following Feynman's prescription: it defines a classical energy functional $E\{j; x_\sigma\} = \pi(x_\sigma)\pi(N_\sigma/x_\sigma)/j^2$ on a neighborhood $\mathcal{F}(j)$ of the input $N$, quantizes it with $p \to -i\partial_q$, and builds a computer state $\Psi(q;j)$ as a superposition over the dimension $F(j)$, drawing an explicit analogy to the Berry–Keating operator conjectured to realize the Hilbert–Pólya program for Riemann zeros. [3] thus shares this paper's target — factoring by a route other than QFT phase estimation — and also shares its epistemic status: the construction is exact in a stated range of validity but no asymptotic advantage over Shor is proven. Our work differs by costing the proposed alternative exactly and reporting where the cost explodes.

The universality question constrains what an ultrametric computer could even be. [8] proves that almost every two-qubit gate is universal, by showing that the non-universal gates form a lower-dimensional subset of the 16-dimensional manifold $\mathrm{U}(4)$, and states the complementary negative fact on which we rely: no gate that maps computation-basis states to computation-basis states of a fixed basis — "classical" gates in its terminology — can be universal, because such gates never create superposition. An ultrametric computer whose admissible unitaries are isometries of the tree, and therefore permute tree vertices, satisfies exactly this classicality condition with respect to the vertex basis. [8] also argues that the proper definition of universality depends on the laws of physics of the underlying technology, which is precisely the dependency this paper makes explicit for a $p$-adic technology.

Precedent for structure-protected computation that changes overhead but not power comes from measurement-based schemes. [7] considers qubits on which one can measure whether a pair is in the singlet or triplet state, protected by $\mathrm{SU}(2)$-invariance of the Hamiltonian, and proves (Theorem 1) that these protected measurements plus single-qubit $X,Z$ unitaries approximate the full Clifford group, Pauli measurements, and $T$ gates with polylogarithmic overhead in the error, via magic-state distillation. The lesson for the ultrametric program is that a protected or structured substrate re-derives the standard gate set at a quantified overhead; it does not yield a new computational model unless the substrate supports operations outside the standard power class.

Hardware context bounds what any new register model must eventually map onto. [2] describes the Tianyan cloud platform operating superconducting processors with 105 qubits at single-qubit, two-qubit, and readout fidelities of 99.90%, 99.56%, and 98.7% respectively, and demonstrates random circuit sampling on 74 qubits and 24 cycles claimed to exceed classical supercomputer simulation. Two features of [2] matter here: the qubit connectivity is a two-dimensional chip architecture adapted to the circuit layout, not a tree, and [2] itself notes that RCS tasks based on iSWAP-like gates have limited practical application. Any ultrametric register must either be embedded in such hardware with an embedding overhead or motivate new fabrication.

The adelic viewpoint that motivates the conjecture has serious mathematical exemplars. [4] proves an arithmetic Hodge index theorem for adelic line bundles over projective varieties over finitely generated fields, extending results from number fields, and applies it to prove rigidity of preperiodic points of polarized algebraic dynamical systems, using nefness of admissible extensions and a fundamental inequality on Minkowski heights. [4] demonstrates that packaging local data at every place into a single adelic object yields global rigidity statements unreachable from any one completion — the exact pattern the ultrametric-computation conjecture hopes to exploit for computation. [6] supplies the measure-theoretic piece: it proves equidistribution of Galois-invariant sequences of points of small height on the Berkovich compactification of $\mathbb{P}^1$ with respect to quasi-adelic measures, showing that the $\mu_v$-energy $\frac{1}{2|S_n|^2}\sum_{x \neq y} g_{\mu_v}(x,y)$ converges to zero at every place including the non-Archimedean ones. [6] thus shows that probability measures on $p$-adic analytic spaces are well behaved and admit limit theorems — a prerequisite for any measurement theory on a tree register — but again the content is analytic number theory, not computational dynamics.

The proximate source of the conjecture is the QNFO program itself. [9] reports five research lines converging on an "Adelic Core" of valuation theory, Bruhat–Tits geometry, Ostrowski's theorem, and the adelic ring, and is explicit about its own epistemic status: the framework's physical claims are marked speculative, with no $p$-adic experimental signature confirmed, while remaining falsifiable through quantitative predictions. [10] develops the ultrametric quantum computation thesis in detail, states Ostrowski's theorem as the anchor — every nontrivial absolute value on $\mathbb{Q}$ is equivalent to the Archimedean one or to a $p$-adic one — and formulates a Threshold Theorem conjecture for fault tolerance on the tree, together with an explicit falsification list that includes the failure of hierarchical couplings to decay exponentially with depth. [11] is the closest methodological precedent: it builds a finite adelic toy register of dimension $810{,}000$ for the prime set $S = \{2,3,5\}$ with local dimension $k_p = 4$, costs a structured functoriality transform at $2888$ operations against a dense baseline of $6.561 \times 10^{11}$, and — critically for our purposes — states in its discussion that the separation factor $2.27 \times 10^8$ compares transform structures on the same register and says nothing about whether the required encodings exist or are efficiently preparable. We adopt [11]'s discipline: every number below is derived with shown arithmetic, and structural separations are kept strictly distinct from physical claims. Finally, [5] illustrates the reverse projection at the protocol level: its continuous-variable direct secure communication schemes encode discrete three-bit symbols by partitioning the real line into eight intervals and applying displacement operators $D(\beta)$ with $\beta = r + ir$, so that discrete information rides on a continuous amplitude read out by homodyne detection. This is the continuous screen carrying discrete structure — the mirror image of the conjecture's claim that quantum superposition is a discrete tree projected onto a continuous screen — and it shows that both directions of projection are implementable in principle; the open question is which direction carries the computational advantage.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 The ultrametric register model

We define a model $\mathcal{M}_p$ intended to be the most charitable concrete realization of the ultrametric conjecture.

**State space.** Fix a prime $p$ and a depth $d$. The register is the vertex set of the Bruhat–Tits tree $T_{p,d}$ of $\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{Q}_p)$ truncated at depth $d$: a rooted tree whose root has $p+1$ children and in which every vertex at depth $1 \le \ell < d$ has $p$ children. The computational basis is $\{|v\rangle : v \in V(T_{p,d})\}$, and the state space is the free complex module $\mathbb{C}^{V(T_{p,d})}$ with the standard inner product. The number of vertices is

$$V(p,d) = 1 + (p+1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1} p^{\ell} = 1 + (p+1)\,\frac{p^d - 1}{p - 1}.$$

For $p = 2$ this simplifies to $V(2,d) = 3 \cdot 2^d - 2$.
