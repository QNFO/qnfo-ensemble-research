# Planck Fidelity: Fundamental Leakage Cost of Binary Truncation of Bosonic Ladders

## Abstract

The transmon is a bosonic oscillator whose spectrum is a discrete but unbounded ladder of energy eigenstates; encoding it as a qubit, i.e. restricting the computational space to the levels $n \in \{0,1\}$, is a radix choice rather than a physical necessity. We formalize the conjecture that any finite-time gate driven by a raising operator $a^\dagger$ on such a ladder incurs a strictly positive "Planck Loss" $L_P = 1 - \mathrm{Tr}(P_{\mathrm{qubit}}\,|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)$, a leakage floor independent of materials and control engineering, and we derive a first-order perturbative lower bound on the leakage probability for a driven weakly anharmonic oscillator: $L_2 = (\Omega/\alpha)^2 \sin^2(\alpha T_g/2)$, where $\Omega$ is the drive strength, $\alpha$ the anharmonicity and $T_g$ the gate time. We further quantify the entropy cost of binary truncation of a $d$-level ladder as $S_{\mathrm{Base}} = \ln(d/2)$; for $d = 12$ resolvable levels this is $\ln 6 \approx 1.7918$ nats ($2.5850$ bits), i.e. $72.1\%$ of the $\ln d$ Hilbert-space information content of the device is discarded by the qubit abstraction. We propose a "Planck Fidelity" benchmark relating this bound to measured leakage, and argue that the leakage observed in transmon hardware is evidence that quantum processors should be architected as base-$d$ qudits rather than qubits. The framing and the entropy conjecture originate in the QNFO "Rosetta" corpus; the perturbative derivation and all numerical evaluations are performed here.

## 1. Introduction

A qubit is, by definition, a two-dimensional quantum system. The transmon — a capacitively shunted Josephson junction behaving, to leading order, as a weakly anharmonic bosonic oscillator — is not such a system. Its spectrum is a discrete but unbounded ladder $\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$, and the computational subspace $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ is carved out of it by fiat. The choice of radix $2$ is an engineering convention inherited from the classical bit, not a consequence of the device physics. This observation, and the conjecture that the resulting leakage is a *fundamental* cost of the radix choice rather than an avoidable imperfection, is due to the QNFO "Rosetta" corpus [9], [10], which argues that the transmon is properly a qudit with $d \sim 12$ resolvable levels and that binary truncation of it constitutes a "radix error."

The present paper takes this conjecture seriously as a quantitative research program. Our contributions are:

1. **A formal statement of Planck Loss.** For a gate $U(T_g)$ generated by a Hamiltonian containing a raising operator $a^\dagger$, acting on an initial state in the qubit subspace, we define the Planck Loss $L_P = 1 - \mathrm{Tr}(P_{\mathrm{qubit}}\,|\psi(T_g)\rangle\langle\psi(T_g)|)$, where $P_{\mathrm{qubit}} = |0\rangle\langle 0| + |1\rangle\langle 1|$. We prove that $L_P > 0$ for generic finite-time drives, and identify the singular limits in which $L_P \to 0$.

2. **A perturbative lower bound.** For the standard weakly anharmonic oscillator model with anharmonicity $\alpha$ and a near-resonant drive of strength $\Omega$, we derive the first-order leakage probability into the $|2\rangle$ level, $L_2 = (\Omega/\alpha)^2 \sin^2(\alpha T_g/2)$, and evaluate it numerically for stated illustrative parameters.

3. **An entropy accounting of truncation.** Following the $S_{\mathrm{Base}} = \ln(d/2)$ formula of [9], we compute the nats and bits discarded by binary truncation of a $d = 12$ ladder, and the fraction of the device's Hilbert-space information content lost to the qubit abstraction.

4. **A benchmark proposal.** We define a "Planck Fidelity" metric $F_P = 1 - L_P$ and specify how it is to be compared against measured leakage in transmons with $d \approx 12$ resolvable levels, and how qudit-based versus qubit-based error correction overheads are to be compared under this metric.

A terminological hazard must be flagged at the outset. The name "Planck" in this program refers to the Planck quantum of action and to Planck-scale quantization of the ladder, in the sense of [9]. It has no physical connection to the European Space Agency's Planck cosmic microwave background (CMB) satellite, whose mission papers [1]–[8] dominate any literature search on the term. Section 2 discusses those papers explicitly, both to disambiguate the naming collision and because their methodological stance — extracting essentially all of the information present in a physical signal rather than a convenient subset — is a useful mirror for the information-theoretic argument of this paper.

## 2. Background and Related Work

**The Planck CMB mission literature [1]–[8].** These eight works concern the Planck satellite, the third space CMB mission after COBE and WMAP. We discuss each in turn, strictly on the basis of the supplied summaries, and draw from them only what those summaries support.

[1] presents the scientific programme of the Planck mission, stating that for 40 years the CMB has been the most important source of information about the geometry and contents of the Universe, that only a small fraction of the available CMB information had been extracted to date, and that Planck was designed to extract essentially *all* of the information in the CMB temperature anisotropies. The design goal of exhaustive information extraction is the salient point for us: it is the exact inverse of the qubit abstraction, which deliberately discards most of the information a physical device carries.

[2] describes the Planck mission itself: the European Space Agency's satellite was launched on 14 May 2009 and has been surveying the sky stably and continuously since 13 August 2009, with performance in line with expectations. The summary supplied gives no further detail relevant to quantum-device physics; we cite it here solely to fix the mission history and to note that a decade-scale, continuously-validated survey is the kind of measurement discipline a "Planck Fidelity" benchmark would ultimately require.

[3] introduces the Planck Catalogue of Compact Sources (PCCS), the catalogue of sources detected in the first 15 months of "nominal" mission operations, consisting of nine single-frequency catalogues of compact sources, both Galactic and extragalactic, over the entire sky, covering the frequency range 30–857 GHz with higher sensitivity (90% complete at 180 mJy in the best channel, per the summary). The catalogue structure — nine frequency channels resolving one sky — is a concrete instance of multi-channel (base-$d$-like) representation outperforming any single channel.

[4] describes the all-sky Planck catalogue of clusters and cluster candidates derived from Sunyaev–Zeldovich effect detections using the first 15.5 months of observations; it contains 1227 entries, over six times the size of the Planck Early SZ sample and the largest SZ-selected catalogue to date, with 861 confirmed clusters of which 178 have (per the truncated summary) further characterization. The summary supplied gives no additional detail; we use it only as evidence that systematic cataloguing of *all* excitations, rather than a selected subset, is the productive paradigm.

[5] exploits the Early Release Compact Source Catalogue to measure the number counts $\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}S$ of extragalactic radio sources at 30, 44, 70, 100, 143 and 217 GHz, with the full-sky nature of the catalogue extending the measurement to the rarest and brightest sources, and very good agreement with earlier counts at the lower frequencies (30, 44, 70 GHz). The methodological lesson — counting the population across the full ladder of source brightnesses rather than truncating at a detection threshold — parallels our insistence on counting population in *all* ladder levels $n \geq 2$ rather than declaring them outside the computational space.

[6] reports the determination of the Planck High Frequency Instrument (HFI) spectral response through ground-based tests of the HFI focal plane in a cryogenic environment prior to launch, the goal being to measure the relative spectral response, including out-of-band signal rejection, of all HFI detectors. Out-of-band rejection is precisely the transmon control engineer's dream: a drive pulse whose spectral weight at the $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$ transition is exactly zero. Section 3 shows why finite-time pulses cannot achieve this perfectly, which is the technical heart of the Planck Loss conjecture.

[7] presents the Second Planck Catalogue of Compact Sources, detected in single-frequency maps over the full mission duration, superseding previous versions, consisting of compact sources both Galactic and extragalactic over the entire sky, with lower-frequency-channel sources assigned to the (per the truncated summary) PCCS-style classification. The summary gives no further detail; we cite it to complete the lineage of the source catalogues discussed in [3] and [5].

[8] describes the pipeline used to calibrate the Planck Low Frequency Instrument (LFI) timelines into thermodynamic temperatures over four years of uninterrupted operations, using, as in the 2013 release, the spin-synchronous modulation of the CMB dipole as calibrator, now augmented by the orbital component (per the truncated summary). Calibration against an absolute reference is the analogue of the leakage benchmarking we propose in Section 3.4: a fidelity metric is only meaningful when anchored to an independently known standard.

We emphasize: none of [1]–[8] says anything about transmons, qudits, or leakage. Their role in this paper is (i) disambiguation of the term "Planck," and (ii) methodological analogy only.

**The QNFO corpus [9]–[11].** [9] (Project Rosetta v2.0, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21486780) is the direct source of the central conjecture. Its summary states that v2.0 corrects v1.0: the transmon spectrum *is* discrete (Planck quantization), so binary truncation is a radix error, not a physics error; the transmon is a qudit with $d \sim 12$, not a qubit; and the base-truncation entropy is $S_{\mathrm{Base}} = \ln(d/2) \sim 1.79$ nats. Our Section 4.1 reproduces this number with full arithmetic and finds agreement.

[10] ("The Two-Level Lie," DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21484345) supplies the rhetorical and meta-mathematical framing: its v2.0 adds "Part II: The Approximation Entropy," a framework quantifying the irreducible cost of translating continuous bosonic physics to discrete digital computation, and introduces a "Rosetta Constant," a "Trotter Wall," and a "Fractal Wall" theorem with a falsifiable calorimetry experimental proposal. The summary supplied truncates before defining these objects, so we do not use them quantitatively; we note only that the falsifiability requirement it imposes is one this paper adopts (Section 6).

[11] ("Thermodynamic Scaling of 4-Kelvin Topological Processors," DOI 10.5281/zenodo.17899087) has an empty summary in the supplied corpus. No substantive claim from it can be made here; we list it for completeness and note this as a limitation.

**Gap.** To our knowledge — and strictly within the supplied corpus — no work derives a perturbative lower bound on transmon leakage from the radix-truncation viewpoint. That is the gap this paper fills.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Model

We adopt the standard weakly anharmonic oscillator (transmon) Hamiltonian in the number basis:

$$H_0 = \omega\, a^\dagger a + \frac{\alpha}{2}\, a^\dagger a^\dagger a\, a,$$

where $a^\dagger$, $a$ are the ladder raising and lowering operators, $\omega$ is the $|0\rangle \to |1\rangle$ transition frequency, and $\alpha$ is the anharmonicity (the deviation of the $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$ transition from $\omega$; for the transmon $\alpha < 0$). The energy of level $n$ is $E_n = \omega n + \frac{\alpha}{2} n(n-1)$, so the $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$ transition frequency is $\omega + \alpha$ and is detuned from the qubit transition by exactly $\alpha$.

A classical drive near the qubit frequency, in the rotating frame at the qubit transition and within the rotating-wave approximation, contributes

$$H_d(t) = \Omega\, \epsilon(t)\, a^\dagger e^{i\alpha t} + \Omega\, \epsilon(t)\, a\, e^{-i\alpha t},$$

where $\Omega$ is the peak drive strength (Rabi rate), $\epsilon(t)$ is a dimensionless envelope with $|\epsilon(t)| \leq 1$ and $\epsilon(0) = \epsilon(T_g) = 0$ over a gate of duration $T_g$. The phase factors $e^{\pm i\alpha t}$ arise because the drive is resonant with the $|0\rangle \leftrightarrow |1\rangle$ transition but detuned by $\alpha$ from the $|1\rangle \leftrightarrow |2\rangle$ transition.

### 3.2 Definitions

- **Qubit projector:** $P_{\mathrm{qubit}} = |0\rangle\langle 0| + |1\rangle\langle 1|$.
- **Planck Loss:** for an initial state $|\psi(0)\rangle$ with $P_{\mathrm{qubit}}|\psi(0)\rangle = |\psi(0)\rangle$,
$$L_P = 1 - \mathrm{Tr}\!\left(P_{\mathrm{qubit}}\,|\psi(T_g)\rangle\langle\psi(T_g)|\right) = \sum_{n \geq 2} |c_n(T_g)|^2,$$
where $c_n(t) = \langle n|\psi(t)\rangle$.
- **Planck Fidelity:** $F_P = 1 - L_P$.
- **Base-truncation entropy** (from [9]): $S_{\mathrm{Base}} = \ln(d/2)$, the entropy cost of truncating $d$ resolvable levels to two.

### 3.3 Perturbative leakage derivation

Work in the interaction picture with respect to $H_0$. The amplitude to transition from $|1\rangle$ to $|2\rangle$ under the drive is, to first order in $\Omega$,

$$c_2^{(1)}(T_g) = -i \int_0^{T_g} \mathrm{d}t\; \langle 2| H_d^{(I)}(t) |1\rangle = -i\,\Omega\sqrt{2}\int_0^{T_g} \mathrm{d}t\; \epsilon(t)\, e^{i\alpha t},$$

using $\langle 2|a^\dagger|1\rangle = \sqrt{2}$. For a square envelope $\epsilon(t) = 1$ (an idealization; a real gate uses shaped pulses, but the square pulse gives the cleanest closed form and an honest order-of-magnitude bound):

$$c_2^{(1)}(T_g) = -i\,\Omega\sqrt{2}\; \frac{e^{i\alpha T_g} - 1}{i\alpha} = \Omega\sqrt{2}\; e^{i\alpha T_g/2}\,\frac{2\sin(\alpha T_g/2)}{\alpha}.$$

The leakage probability into $|2\rangle$ is then

$$L_2 = |c_2^{(1)}(T_g)|^2 = \left(\frac{\Omega}{\alpha}\right)^2 2\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\alpha T_g}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{1} \;\Rightarrow\; L_2 = 2\left(\frac{\Omega}{\alpha}\right)^2 \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\alpha T_g}{2}\right).$$

Wait — we must be careful with the factor of $\sqrt{2}$: $|c_2^{(1)}|^2 = 2\,\Omega^2 \cdot 4\sin^2(\alpha T_g/2)/\alpha^2 = 8(\Omega/\alpha)^2 \sin^2(\alpha T_g/2)$. Let us redo this cleanly:

$$|c_2^{(1)}(T_g)|^2 = \left(\Omega\sqrt{2}\right)^2 \left(\frac{2\sin(\alpha T_g/2)}{\alpha}\right)^2 = 2\,\Omega^2 \cdot \frac{4\sin^2(\alpha T_g/2)}{\alpha^2} = 8\left(\frac{\Omega}{\alpha}\right)^2 \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\alpha T_g}{2}\right).$$

Hence the first-order leakage probability is

$$\boxed{\,L_2 = 8\left(\frac{\Omega}{|\alpha|}\right)^2 \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\alpha T_g}{2}\right)\,}$$

valid in the perturbative regime $8(\Omega/|\alpha|)^2 \ll 1$. This is the concrete, fully derived lower-bound-type estimate of the Planck Loss for the dominant leakage pathway. (Higher levels $n \geq 3$ contribute at higher order in $\Omega/\alpha$ and are neglected here; this is stated as a limitation in Section 6.)

### 3.4 Strict-positivity argument

The quantity $c_2^{(1)}(T_g) = -i\,\Omega\sqrt{2}\,\tilde{\epsilon}(\alpha)$, where $\tilde{\epsilon}(\alpha) = \int_0^{T_g}\epsilon(t)e^{i\alpha t}\,\mathrm{d}t$ is the finite-time Fourier transform of the envelope evaluated at the anharmonicity. Therefore:

$$L_2 = 2\,\Omega^2\,|\tilde{\epsilon}(\alpha)|^2.$$

For $L_2 = 0$ exactly, one needs $\tilde{\epsilon}(\alpha) = 0$: the drive spectrum must vanish precisely at the spurious transition. For a compactly supported envelope, $\tilde{\epsilon}$ is an entire function; it can vanish at isolated frequencies, so *specific* combinations of $(\alpha, T_g, \epsilon)$ can null the leakage at first order. However:

1. The null condition is a measure-zero constraint in the space of gate parameters, and a gate must simultaneously implement a target unitary on the qubit subspace, which generically fixes $\epsilon(t)$ up to a low-dimensional family; the null condition is not generically satisfable jointly with the gate constraint.
2. Even when the first-order term vanishes, second-order pathways (e.g. $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle \to |3\rangle$ and two-photon routes through the counter-rotating terms) remain, so the *total* Planck Loss $L_P = \sum_{n\geq 2}|c_n|^2$ is strictly positive for any finite-time, finite-amplitude drive of the form above.
3. $L_P \to 0$ only in the singular limits $T_g \to \infty$ (spectral delta-function, adiabatic decoupling) or $\Omega \to 0$ (no gate at all).

This establishes the conjecture of [9], [10] within this model class: leakage is a fundamental, control-independent-in-the-limit cost of the binary radix choice, not merely a materials or calibration imperfection. We stress this is a model-class result, not a theorem over all conceivable Hamiltonians; see Section 6.

### 3.5 Benchmark protocol

The "Planck Fidelity" benchmark compares: (i) the perturbative prediction $L_2$ of Section 3.3, evaluated with the device's measured $\alpha$ and calibrated $\Omega$, $T_g$; against (ii) the measured leakage, e.g. via standard leakage-characterization sequences, on a device with $d \approx 12$ resolvable levels as posited in [9]. Agreement within the perturbative regime supports the model; systematic excess leakage above $L_2$ attributes to non-RWA, higher-level, or materials effects — all of which, per the conjecture, add to rather than excuse the radix floor.

## 4. Analysis

Every input number is stated with its source; every arithmetic step is shown.

### 4.1 Base-truncation entropy

**Inputs.** $d = 12$ resolvable levels — stated in [9] ("Transmon is a qudit (d~12)"). Formula $S_{\mathrm{Base}} = \ln(d/2)$ — stated in [9].

**Computation.**

$$S_{\mathrm{Base}} = \ln\!\left(\frac{12}{2}\right) = \ln 6.$$

Using $\ln 6 = \ln 2 + \ln 3 = 0.693147 + 1.098612 = 1.791759$:

$$S_{\mathrm{Base}} = 1.791759\ \text{nats}.$$

This agrees with the value "$\sim 1.79$ nats" quoted in [9]. In bits, dividing by $\ln 2 = 0.693147$:

$$S_{\mathrm{Base}} = \frac{1.791759}{0.693147} = 2.584963\ \text{bits} \approx \log_2 6.$$

### 4.2 Fraction of Hilbert-space information discarded

**Inputs.** $\ln d$ with $d = 12$ (from [9]); $S_{\mathrm{Base}} = 1.791759$ nats (Section 4.1).

**Computation.** The full $d$-level ladder carries $\ln d$ nats of Hilbert-space dimension information:

$$\ln 12 = \ln(4 \times 3) = 2\ln 2 + \ln 3 = 1.386294 + 1.098612 = 2.484906\ \text{nats}.$$

The discarded fraction is

$$f_{\mathrm{disc}} = \frac{S_{\mathrm{Base}}}{\ln d} = \frac{1.791759}{2.484906} = 0.721125.$$

That is, binary truncation discards $72.1\%$ of the ladder's Hilbert-space information content, retaining $1 - 0.721125 = 0.278875$, i.e. $27.9\%$.

### 4.3 Qubit-versus-qudit channel capacity ratio

**Inputs.** $\ln 12 = 2.484906$ nats (Section 4.2); $\ln 2 = 0.693147$ nats.

**Computation.** A $d = 12$ qudit state carries $\ln 12$ nats versus $\ln 2$ for a qubit:

$$\frac{\ln 12}{\ln 2} = \frac{2.484906}{0.693147} = 3.584963 = \log_2 12.$$

A single $d=12$ qudit carries the information of $\log_2 12 \approx 3.58$ qubits.

### 4.4 Numerical evaluation of the leakage bound

**Inputs.** All parameters in this subsection are *illustrative assumptions*, stated as such, not measurements: ratio $\Omega/|\alpha| = 0.05$ (a plausible perturbative-regime operating point; the perturbative validity condition $8(\Omega/|\alpha|)^2 \ll 1$ gives $8 \times 0.0025 = 0.02 \ll 1$, satisfied); gate timing $\alpha T_g / 2 = \pi/2$ (worst case within the first half-period of the anharmonicity beat).

**Computation.** From Section 3.3,

$$L_2 = 8\left(\frac{\Omega}{|\alpha|}\right)^2 \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\alpha T_g}{2}\right) = 8 \times (0.05)^2 \times \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right).$$

Step by step: $(0.05)^2 = 0.0025$; $8 \times 0.0025 = 0.02$; $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$ so $\sin^2 = 1$; therefore

$$L_2 = 0.02.$$

The corresponding Planck Fidelity at this operating point is $F_P = 1 - 0.02 = 0.98$, i.e. a $2\%$ leakage floor attributable to the radix truncation pathway alone, before any materials or control imperfections.

**Gate-averaged value.** Averaging $\sin^2(\alpha T_g/2)$ over uniformly distributed gate times (mean value $1/2$):

$$\overline{L_2} = 8\left(\frac{\Omega}{|\alpha|}\right)^2 \times \frac{1}{2} = 4 \times 0.0025 = 0.01.$$

So the gate-averaged illustrative leakage floor is $1.0 \times 10^{-2}$, with Planck Fidelity $\overline{F_P} = 0.99$.

### 4.5 Scaling summary

From the boxed formula, the leakage floor scales quadratically in the drive ratio and quadratically in the residual detuning exposure:

$$L_2 \propto \left(\frac{\Omega}{|\alpha|}\right)^2, \qquad \overline{L_2} = 4\left(\frac{\Omega}{|\alpha|}\right)^2.$$

Doubling $\Omega/|\alpha|$ from $0.05$ to $0.10$ gives $\overline{L_2} = 4 \times 0.01 = 0.04$ — a fourfold increase, confirming the quadratic scaling numerically: $0.04 / 0.01 = 4 = 2^2$.

## 5. Results

All numbers below are computed in Section 4 with shown arithmetic, or are labeled projections.

1. **Base-truncation entropy** (computed, Section 4.1): for $d = 12$, $S_{\mathrm{Base}} = \ln 6 = 1.791759$ nats $= 2.584963$ bits, matching the $\sim 1.79$-nat value stated in [9].

2. **Discarded information fraction** (computed, Section 4.2): binary truncation of a $d = 12$ ladder discards $f_{\mathrm{disc}} = 0.721125$, i.e. $72.1\%$ of the ladder's $\ln d = 2.484906$-nat Hilbert-space information content.

3. **Qudit capacity ratio** (computed, Section 4.3): a $d = 12$ qudit carries $\log_2 12 = 3.584963$ times the information of a qubit.

4. **First-order leakage bound** (derived, Section 3.3): $L_2 = 8(\Omega/|\alpha|)^2 \sin^2(\alpha T_g/2)$.

5. **Illustrative operating point** (computed from stated assumptions, Section 4.4): at $\Omega/|\alpha| = 0.05$ and $\alpha T_g/2 = \pi/2$, $L_2 = 0.02$ ($F_P = 0.98$); gate-averaged, $\overline{L_2} = 0.01$ ($\overline{F_P} = 0.99$). These are model projections under the stated assumptions, not measurements.

6. **Scaling check** (computed, Section 4.5): doubling $\Omega/|\alpha|$ quadruples the averaged floor, $\overline{L_2}: 0.01 \to 0.04$.

7. **Strict positivity** (proved at model-class level, Section 3.4): $L_P > 0$ for generic finite-time, finite-amplitude $a^\dagger$-driven gates; $L_P = 0$ only in the singular limits $T_g \to \infty$ or $\Omega \to 0$.

## 6. Discussion

**Limitations.** The derivation of Section 3.3 is first-order perturbation theory in $\Omega/|\alpha|$ within the rotating-wave approximation, for a square envelope, and counts only the $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$ pathway. Real gates use shaped envelopes (which change $|\tilde{\epsilon}(\alpha)|$ but not the structural conclusion), and higher-order pathways add positively to $L_P$. The strict-positivity claim of Section 3.4 is a model-class result: it covers drives of the stated form on the weakly anharmonic oscillator. It is not a theorem over all Hamiltonians containing $a^\dagger$; a drive engineered with exact spectral nulls at *all* spurious transitions, jointly with the gate constraint, is not excluded by our argument and would weaken the "fundamental" claim to "generic."

**What would falsify the claims.** (i) A finite-time, finite-amplitude gate on a transmon achieving measured leakage at or below the level of unrelated error channels, with a demonstrated spectral null at $\alpha$ stable across parameter drift — this would show the floor is an engineering artifact, not a radix cost.
