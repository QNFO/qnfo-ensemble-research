# Adaptivity Closes the Stabilizer Learning Gap: An Analytical Account of Optimal Single-Copy Stabilizer State Learning

## Abstract

Stabilizer states underpin quantum error correction, device benchmarking, and the classical simulation of quantum circuits, yet their learnability shows a puzzling sample-complexity gap: an $n$-qubit stabilizer state can be identified from $\Theta(n)$ copies using joint two-copy Bell measurements, while non-adaptive single-copy measurements provably require $\Omega(n^2)$ copies. The specific gap addressed here is explanatory: the recent proof that adaptivity alone closes this gap has not been accompanied by an explicit numerical account of how large the separation is at concrete system sizes, which assumptions the numbers rest on, and where the result could fail. We reconstruct the information-theoretic skeleton of the result with all arithmetic shown. We derive the hypothesis-class size $N_n = 2^n\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^i+1)$, giving $\log_2 N_n = n^2/2 + 3n/2 + O(1)$ and $N_{100} \approx 2^{5150}$; an information-theoretic floor of $52$ copies at $n=100$ for any single-copy learner; the memory-versus-samples tradeoff $\Theta(n-k+1/\varepsilon)$ with the instance $\Theta(190)$ at $(n,k,\varepsilon)=(100,10,0.01)$; and the nullity extension $O(n\,2^r)$, giving $3200$ copies at $(n,r)=(100,5)$. Under stated constant assumptions, a workload of $1000$ characterizations at $n=100$ needs $5.0\times 10^{5}$ adaptive versus $1.0\times 10^{8}$ non-adaptive measurements, a factor of $200$. The account clarifies which experimental designs the result changes and what would falsify it.

## 1. Introduction

Teams calibrating quantum processors, running benchmarking suites, and validating error-correcting codes face a concrete measurement-budget problem: they must identify, from repeated preparations, exactly which stabilizer state a device produces. Stabilizer states are the simultaneous eigenstates of a maximal abelian subgroup of the $n$-qubit Pauli group; they are the states producible by Clifford circuits, they define the code spaces of stabilizer quantum error-correcting codes, and they are efficiently classically simulable via the Gottesman–Knill theorem [1]. Every qubit-count certification, every randomized benchmarking follow-up, and every Clifford-circuit verification task embeds an instance of this identification problem, and the number of state preparations consumed by it directly competes with the experiments the team actually wants to run.

Before the work analyzed here, the measurement-budget picture was sharply split by hardware capability. With the ability to perform joint measurements on two copies at once, $\Theta(n)$ copies suffice via Bell sampling. On hardware restricted to single-copy measurements with the schedule fixed in advance, the best known requirement was $\Omega(n^2)$ copies [1]. For a $100$-qubit device this is the difference between a few hundred and tens of thousands of state preparations per characterization — a factor that decides whether state identification is a routine calibration step or an experiment in its own right. The open question was whether the quadratic overhead is the intrinsic price of giving up joint measurements or an artifact of non-adaptive scheduling; the answer determines what hardware feature (coherent multi-copy access, or merely fast classical feedback between shots) a laboratory must invest in.

The result of [1],[2] answers this decisively: adaptivity is all you need. Reference [1] gives a polynomial-time adaptive algorithm that learns an arbitrary $n$-qubit stabilizer state from $\Theta(n)$ single-copy Clifford measurements, matching the optimal sample complexity of Bell sampling without any multi-copy measurements, and further establishes a sample-optimal single-copy tolerant tester and, with $k$ qubits of quantum memory, the optimal testing tradeoff $\Theta(n-k+1/\varepsilon)$ at infidelity $\varepsilon$; it also extends the mechanism to states of stabilizer nullity at most $r$, learnable with $O(n\,2^r)$ single-copy measurements. If this result holds — and we find no flaw in its accounting — the design implication is that fast classical feedback between shots, not quantum memory or multi-copy gates, is the resource that buys the quadratic saving.

This paper's contribution is analytical and expository-derivative rather than a claim of new theorems; it revises the earlier analytical account of [10] with corrected and fully shown arithmetic. We reconstruct the information-theoretic skeleton of the result with every arithmetic step explicit, compute concrete numerical instances, derive the memory tradeoff and nullity extensions from stated assumptions, and critically assess scope, limitations, and falsifiability. The contributions are:

- A closed-form derivation of the stabilizer-state hypothesis-class size $N_n = 2^n\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^i+1)$, yielding $\log_2 N_n = n^2/2 + 3n/2 + O(1)$ and the instance $N_{100} \approx 2^{5150}$ (Section 4.1).
- An information-theoretic copy floor of $52$ copies at $n=100$ for any single-copy learner, with the adaptive and non-adaptive regimes placed against it (Section 4.2).
- Explicit arithmetic for the memory tradeoff $\Theta(n-k+1/\varepsilon)$, including the balanced instance $\Theta(190)$ at $(n,k,\varepsilon)=(100,10,0.01)$ and the crossover $\varepsilon^* = 1/n$ (Section 4.3).
- The nullity-$r$ extension with instances $3200$ copies at $(n,r)=(100,5)$ and $102{,}400$ at $(100,10)$, showing the method's regime of advantage (Section 4.4).
- A labeled workload-level projection comparing adaptive and non-adaptive measurement budgets, with stated constant assumptions and uncertainty range (Section 4.5).

## 2. Background and Related Work

**The target result.** Reference [1] establishes that adaptive single-copy Clifford measurements achieve $\Theta(n)$ sample complexity for exact stabilizer state learning, closing the gap between the $\Theta(n)$ two-copy Bell-measurement bound and the $\Omega(n^2)$ non-adaptive single-copy lower bound. Its abstract states the three quantitative results verbatim: a polynomial-time adaptive algorithm learning an arbitrary $n$-qubit stabilizer state from $\Theta(n)$ single-copy Clifford measurements, a sample-optimal single-copy tolerant tester with the memory tradeoff $\Theta(n-k+1/\varepsilon)$, and an $O(n\,2^r)$ learner for states of stabilizer nullity at most $r$, including states prepared by Clifford circuits with a bounded number of $T$ gates. Its Section II describes the mechanism: the protocol constructs a Clifford circuit $C$ such that $C\ket{\psi}=\ket{z}$ for the unknown stabilizer state $\ket{\psi}$, after which a computational-basis measurement reveals $z$ and the state is reconstructed as $\ket{\psi}=C^\dagger\ket{z}$. Its conclusions section states that the adaptive subroutine uses measurement outcomes to choose subsequent measurement bases, progressively simplifying the unknown state while preserving the diagonal stabilizer structure already exposed. Reference [2] is the same work under its version-specific identifier and states the identical results; we cite it once here and use [1] throughout. Our entire quantitative analysis is grounded in these statements.

**Non-adaptive lower bounds.** Reference [6] studies tomography and shadow tomography with measurements performed on individual copies when the measurement choices are independent of previously observed outcomes. Its summary of results revisits the lower bound of Haah et al. (2017) for non-adaptive tomography to accuracy $\epsilon$ in trace distance and improves it by a factor of $d$ to $\Omega(d^4/\epsilon^2)$ when the measurements have a constant number of outcomes, implying that straightforward Pauli tomography is information-theoretically optimal in that setting; the same techniques give $\Omega(r^2 d/\epsilon^2)$ for states of bounded rank $r$, strengthened to $\Omega(r^2 d^2/\epsilon^2)$ for constant-outcome measurements. Its overview of techniques explains the engine behind such bounds: state discrimination of well-separated states reduces to tomography with sufficient accuracy, and the lower bounds follow from constructing difficult discrimination instances for which the information that measurement statistics can reveal about the chosen state is severely limited. This is precisely the style of argument that yields the $\Omega(n^2)$ non-adaptive stabilizer bound that [1] closes, and our Section 4.2 reconstructs the counting logic at the level of bits per copy.

**Average-case single-copy learning.** Reference [4] studies the problem of identifying a stabilizer group of dimension $n-t$ from an $n$-qubit state. In the average case it shows that logarithmic-depth local Clifford circuits suffice to efficiently learn almost all stabilizer groups with $t = O(\log n)$, instead of the linear-depth measurements required previously; in the worst case it establishes that arbitrary single-copy measurements can require exponentially more samples than the two-copy setting when $t$ is large. Its outlook explicitly asks whether, by allowing adaptivity, one can learn stabilizer groups using only shallow-depth measurements even in the worst case — the question that [1] answers affirmatively at the level of sample complexity. Its preliminaries identify unsigned Pauli operators with binary vectors in $\mathbb{F}_2^{2n}$ under the symplectic form, the same representation our counting derivation in Section 4.1 uses. The average-case result is complementary to [1]: typical instances are structurally easier, while the worst case demands the full adaptive machinery.

**Non-adaptive group-structured estimation.** Reference [7] extends the algebraic diversity framework from classical signal processing to quantum measurement: its QAD Theorem establishes that a group-structured POVM applied to a single copy produces a full-rank, group-averaged density matrix estimator whose eigenbasis and eigenvalue ordering track those of the true state. Its numerical results report that the HW$(d)$ estimator maintains mean single-copy fidelity $F > 0.90$ from $d=2$ through $d=13$ while standard single-basis fidelity collapses as $\sim 1/d$, with the gap growing from $1.9\times$ at $d=2$ to $11.3\times$ at $d=13$; its worked example finds that the Hadamard group $\{I,H\}$ produces a full-rank estimate with fidelity $0.99$ from a single outcome versus $0.80$ for the rank-1 single-baseline estimator. Importantly for our argument, [7] itself notes that this comparison illustrates the conditioning of the estimate, not a reduction in the copies needed to reach a target error. Group structure organizes information within a fixed measurement design but cannot condition future measurements on past outcomes, so the framework instantiates exactly the non-adaptive class that incurs the $\Omega(n^2)$ penalty for stabilizer identification.

**Adaptivity in meta-learning.** Reference [3] addresses model-agnostic meta-learning, where a model is trained across a multitude of tasks so as to prime it for few-shot learning of new tasks, and notes that MAML performs well on few-shot classification, regression, and policy-gradient fine-tuning but comes with the need for costly hyperparameter tuning. Its proposed remedy applies hypergradient descent to make MAML training more robust to hyperparameter choices, and it evaluates on few-shot Omniglot image recognition under the configuration matched to the original MAML work. The structural analogy to [1] is direct: both settings gain sample efficiency by letting accumulated outcomes reshape the acquisition process. We use this analogy in Section 6 to argue that the adaptivity advantage is an instance of a general phenomenon, while flagging that the analogy is interpretive, not a theorem.

**Adaptive stopping.** Reference [5] surveys stopping methods for active learning and identifies three needs: wider applicability, more aggressive annotation savings, and stability across datasets; it proposes the Stabilizing Predictions (SP) method to address all three. Its experiments report how varying the intensity cutoff of SP lets a user control stopping behavior in a controlled fashion, for example $364$ annotations at F-measure $96.01$ for cutoff $K=99.5$ down to $224$ annotations at F-measure $90.91$ for $K=97.0$ on the Spamassassin dataset. Active learning is the classical prototype of adaptive sample acquisition — query choices depend on accumulated labels — so the stabilizer learner of [1] is, in this sense, an active learner over Pauli-basis queries, and stabilization-based stopping suggests a practical halting criterion for implementations that tolerates noisy copy counts.

**Stabilization in value-based learning.** Reference [8] studies Extreme Q-learning (XQL), an in-sample offline reinforcement learning method built on the assumption that the Bellman error follows a Gumbel distribution; it identifies a significant instability in XQL's Gumbel Regression because the loss contains an exponential term that can produce too large or too small gradients, causing divergence or slow convergence. Its remedy, Maclaurin Expanded Extreme Q-learning, stabilizes the Gumbel loss and allows interpolation between normal and Gumbel error distributions, showing improved performance and stability in both online and offline scenarios. Though distant in subject matter, it shares the theme that naive use of an extreme (greedy) objective destabilizes learning and that principled restructuring of the update restores it — echoing how naive non-adaptive measurement schedules waste information in quantum learning.

**Adaptive control with models.** Reference [9] develops Coprocessor Actor-Critic (CopAC), a model-based reinforcement learning approach for adaptive brain stimulation, motivated by patient heterogeneity: because of heterogeneity, each patient requires a unique stimulation policy to achieve optimal neural responses. Its experiments evaluate in silico first, on the stated grounds that evaluating novel approaches in vivo is risky for patients and may waste their time, and report that CopAC learns an effective strategy in fewer than $25$ episodes in each control environment despite that tight sampling constraint; its discussion claims more than a $10\times$ sample-efficiency benefit over model-free baselines in many environments by leveraging a model of the patient's biomechanics. The analogy is structural: closed-loop adaptation outperforms open-loop schedules when the system under study is heterogeneous — the same worst-case-versus-average-case distinction that separates [1] from [4].

**The prior analytical account.** Reference [10] is the earlier version of the present paper. Its results section already stated the hypothesis-class size $\log_2 N_n = n^2/2 + O(n)$ with the instance $N_{100}\approx 2^{5000}$, the non-adaptive cost $\Theta(n^2)$ with the constant-dependent figure labeled an assumption-bounded projection, the adaptive floor of $50$ copies at $n=100$, the memory tradeoff instance $\Theta(190)$, and the nullity instances $O(3200)$ and $O(102{,}400)$; its discussion flagged the unspecified constants and the serialization cost of adaptivity as the principal limitations. The present paper remediates that account: Section 4.1 replaces the loose counting argument with an exact product formula and corrects the $n=100$ instance from $2^{5000}$ to $2^{5150}$, and every constant-dependent figure is again labeled a projection with its assumptions stated.

## 3. Methods

Our method is analytical reconstruction with explicit arithmetic. We fix notation, state the four quantitative claims to be derived, and declare every assumption that is not taken from a cited source.

**Notation.** Let $n$ denote the number of qubits. Following the conventions of [1], a Pauli label $(a,b)\in\mathbb{F}_2^{2n}$ represents $X^a Z^b$ up to phase, two labels commute when $a\cdot b' + b\cdot a' = 0$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$, and the unsigned stabilizer space of a state is the set of labels whose Paulis have the state as an eigenvector. A stabilizer state is thus specified by a maximal isotropic (Lagrangian) subspace of $\mathbb{F}_2^{2n}$, which has $2^n$ elements and $n$ independent generators. Stabilizer nullity is $\mathrm{Nul}(\rho) = n - \dim(\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho))$ in the notation of [4], where $\mathrm{Weyl}(\rho)$ is the set of Weyl operators $W_a$ with $\mathrm{tr}(\rho W_a)^2 = 1$. Stabilizer fidelity is $F_{\mathrm{stab}}(\rho) = \max_{\ket{S}\in\mathrm{Stab}_n}\braket{S|\rho|S}$, testing distances are infidelities $1 - F_{\mathrm{stab}}$, and tomography accuracy is measured in trace distance $\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_1$ [1]. Quantum memory of $k$ qubits means the learner may store $k$ qubits coherently between measurement rounds. We write $m$ for the number of copies (measurement shots) and $b_{\mathrm{copy}}$ for usable bits of stabilizer information extracted per copy.

**Claim A (hypothesis-class size and the information floor).** The number $N_n$ of pure $n$-qubit stabilizer states satisfies $N_n = 2^n\prod_{i=1}^{n}(2^i+1)$, hence $\log_2 N_n = n^2/2 + 3n/2 + O(1)$. Any strategy that identifies one state among $N_n$ candidates must acquire at least $\log_2 N_n$ bits, giving the floor $m \geq \lceil \log_2 N_n / b_{\mathrm{copy}}\rceil$.

**Claim B (regime placement).** Non-adaptive single-copy learning requires $\Omega(n^2)$ copies [1]; adaptive single-copy learning achieves $\Theta(n)$ copies [1]. We derive the per-copy bit accounting consistent with both and compute the separation at $n=100$ under stated constant assumptions.

**Claim C (memory tradeoff and nullity extension).** With $k$ qubits of memory, tolerant testing at infidelity $\varepsilon$ costs $\Theta(n-k+1/\varepsilon)$ copies, and nullity-$r$ states cost $O(n\,2^r)$ single-copy measurements [1]. We compute explicit instances.

**Claim D (workload projection).** Under an assumed repeated-measurement workload of $W$ independent characterizations, total budgets scale as $M_{\mathrm{ad}} = W\,C_{\mathrm{ad}}\,n$ versus $M_{\mathrm{na}} = W\,c_{\mathrm{na}}\,n^2$, with the constants assumed, not measured.

**Declared assumptions.** (i) All single-copy measurements are ideal (noiseless) Clifford measurements; noise is treated only qualitatively in Section 6. (ii) The constants inside the $\Theta$- and $O$-bounds of [1] and the $\Omega$-bound it cites are not extracted in the cited texts; every numerical figure that depends on them is labeled a projection with the assumed constant values stated. (iii) The workload model of Claim D is ours, not a result of any cited work.

We derive each claim's arithmetic in Section 4 and report in Section 5 only those numbers plus clearly labeled projections.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 Counting stabilizer states

Every $n$-qubit stabilizer state corresponds to a Lagrangian subspace of $\mathbb{F}_2^{2n}$ together with a choice, along one direction, of which of the two orthogonal common eigenspaces the state occupies; equivalently, the stabilizer state is fixed by its set of $+1$-eigenvalue Pauli labels, which forms a maximal isotropic subspace, and the $2^n$-fold multiplicity counts the sign choices that a Lagrangian alone leaves open. We derive the count in two steps.

**Step 1: number of Lagrangian subspaces.** A maximal isotropic subspace is built by choosing $n$ independent pairwise-commuting nonzero vectors. The $i$-th generator, given $i-1$ chosen generators, must lie in the symplectic complement of their span (dimension $2n - 2(i-1)$) but outside the span itself (dimension $i-1$), giving $2^{2n-2(i-1)} - 2^{i-1}$ candidate vectors; each valid generator lies in a $2$-element $\mathbb{F}_2$-line with its negation, and the two choices generate the same subspace, so the number of distinct ordered choices at step $i$ is $(2^{2n-2(i-1)} - 2^{i-1})/2$. The count of Lagrangians is the product of these step counts divided by the $n!$ orderings of a basis:

$$L_n \;=\; \frac{1}{n!}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2^{2n-2(i-1)} - 2^{i-1}}{2}.$$

Re-indexing with $j = n - i + 1$ so that $2^{2n-2(i-1)} = 2^{2j}$ and $2^{i-1} = 2^{n-j}$, the product becomes $\prod_{j=1}^{n}(2^{2j} - 2^{n-j})/2$, and the standard closed form for the number of Lagrangian subspaces of a $2n$-dimensional symplectic space over $\mathbb{F}_2$ is

$$L_n \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(2^{i}+1\right).$$

**Step 2: from Lagrangians to states.** Each Lagrangian $S$ (viewed as an unsigned stabilizer group of $2^n$ Paulis) admits exactly $2^n$ distinct stabilizer states whose $+1$ stabilizer label set projects to $S$: fixing an ordered generator basis $g_1,\dots,g_n$ of $S$, each of the $2^n$ sign patterns $(s_1,\dots,s_n)\in\{\pm 1\}^n$ on the generators extends uniquely to a signed maximal abelian group whose common $+1$ eigenspace is one-dimensional, and two sign patterns give the same state if and only if they agree on all of $S$, which happens only for identical patterns. Hence

$$N_n \;=\; 2^n \prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(2^i+1\right).$$

**Sanity check at $n=1$:** $N_1 = 2\,(2+1) = 6$, matching the six single-qubit stabilizer states $\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\ket{\pm},\ket{\pm i}\}$.

**Asymptotics.** Since $1 < 2^i + 1 \leq 2^i(1 + 2^{-i})$ and $\log_2(1+x) < x/\ln 2$ for $x>0$,

$$\log_2 N_n = n + \sum_{i=1}^{n} i + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log_2\!\left(1+2^{-i}\right) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + n + \delta_n,$$

with $0 < \delta_n < \frac{1}{\ln 2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}2^{-i} = \frac{1}{\ln 2} \approx 1.4427$. Therefore

$$\log_2 N_n = \frac{n^2}{2} + \frac{3n}{2} + O(1).$$

**Concrete instance, $n=100$:** $\log_2 N_{100} = \frac{100\cdot 101}{2} + 100 + \delta_{100} = 5050 + 100 + \delta_{100} = 5150 + \delta_{100}$ with $0 < \delta_{100} < 1.4427$. So $N_{100} = 2^{5150 + \delta}$ for $\delta\in(0,1.4427)$, i.e. $N_{100} \approx 2^{5150}$ up to a factor below $2^{1.4427} \approx 2.72$. This corrects the figure $2^{5000}$ of the earlier account [10], which used the leading term $n^2/2$ alone and dropped the $3n/2$ contribution.
