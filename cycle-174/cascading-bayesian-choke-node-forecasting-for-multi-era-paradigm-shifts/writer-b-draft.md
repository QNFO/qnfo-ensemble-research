# Cascading Bayesian Choke-Node Forecasting for Multi-Era Paradigm Shifts: Transition-Matrix Formalism, Leverage Ranking, and Stress Testing

## Abstract

Long-horizon forecasts of technological and scientific paradigm shifts are routinely produced as lists of independently assessed milestones, an practice that ignores the dependency between successive eras of a technology's evolution and hides which single conditional probability dominates the expected outcome. This paper formalizes paradigm-shift forecasting as a Markov chain of eras whose states are optimistic, baseline, and pessimistic, with each era's outcome distribution conditioned on the previous era's state through a stochastic transition matrix. We derive closed-form marginal distributions by matrix multiplication, prove that the leverage of a conditional transition entry on total lifecycle expected value factors into a reachable-mass term and a downstream-value term, and derive an exact formula for the expected-value change under an adversarial "leverage shift" that moves probability mass between two successor states. In a fully worked three-state, two-transition example with all arithmetic shown, the framework identifies a single choke node (baseline-to-optimistic in the second transition, leverage $51.0$ in terminal-value units) and quantifies a stress blind spot: an independence-era baseline overstates stressed lifecycle expected value by $0.97425$ units, or $5.43\%$ of the stressed cascading value, because it cannot respond to choke-node deterioration. We specify a back-casting validation protocol against historical roadmaps and position the framework relative to probabilistic forecasting and hierarchical-coherence literature. The result gives foresight analysts a computable ranking of which conditional beliefs to stress-test first.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations that fund deep technology programs — government agencies writing decades-long roadmaps, corporate strategy teams deciding between battery chemistries or fusion approaches, investment committees sizing positions in pre-commercial platforms — must forecast outcomes that unfold across multiple eras, each era's possibilities shaped by what happened in the one before. The forecasting methods most such organizations use today treat each milestone as an independent estimate: a probability that solid-state batteries reach production, a separate probability that fusion achieves net plant economics, each elicited and stress-tested in isolation. This independence assumption has two costs. First, it discards the cascading structure that practitioners know intuitively — a pessimistic outcome in a materials-supply era makes an optimistic outcome in a manufacturing-scale era far less likely — and therefore misstates the full distribution of lifecycle value. Second, and more expensively, it gives the analyst no ranking of which conditional beliefs actually drive the answer, so red-team effort is spread uniformly over dozens of milestones instead of concentrated on the few transitions with maximal leverage. A decision such as whether to hedge a portfolio across eras, or which single assumption to attack first in a review, changes materially if the dominant transition can be identified and quantified.

The framework proposed here addresses both costs. Era outcomes are modeled as a Markov chain over three states — optimistic, baseline, pessimistic — with era-to-era conditional transition matrices. Marginal era distributions follow from matrix products, lifecycle expected value is a bilinear function of the transition entries, and "choke nodes" — the conditional probability pairs with maximal leverage on total expected value — are identified by an analytically computable derivative. An adversarial stress test, the leverage shift, moves mass out of a choke node's optimistic column and yields an exact expected-value change, giving foresight teams a defensible ordering for scarce review attention.

The intellectual context for this formalization is the Cascading Bayesian Foresight Engine (CFPE), an eight-stage methodology that already builds a "probability-weighted DAG with conditional dependencies" at its stage S5 and validates itself by historical back-casting from 1976, 1950, and 1926 vantage points [10]. CFPE, however, stops at the level of a qualitative DAG: it registers no closed-form quantity that ranks conditional entries by their leverage on expected value, and its adversarial red-teaming stage attempts to kill candidates wholesale rather than measure the expected-value cost of a specific conditional deterioration. Era-structured forecasting of the kind CFPE and successor papers practice — the stratigraphy of number systems as a sequence of enclosures [9], the history-constrained forecast of computing substrates with twelve registered falsifiable predictions [11], and the structured assessment of the adelic-substrate thesis with explicit reference-class base rates [12] — currently lacks a shared mathematical skeleton for the cascade itself. This paper supplies one.

The contributions are:

1. A closed-form derivation of era marginal distributions and lifecycle expected value as matrix products of stochastic transition matrices, with automatic probability conservation (Section 3, computed in Section 4).
2. A leverage theorem: the derivative of total expected value with respect to any conditional transition entry factors into a reachable-mass term and a downstream-value term, giving an exact, computable choke-node ranking; plus an exact formula for the expected-value change under a leverage shift (Section 4).
3. A fully worked numerical demonstration showing that choke-node identity depends on where value accrues in time, and quantifying the independence baseline's stress blind spot at $0.97425$ units, $5.43\%$ of stressed cascading value (Sections 4 and 5).
4. A validation protocol — back-casting calibration on historical roadmaps such as solid-state batteries and fusion, with pre-registered falsification criteria — and an assessment of readiness and engineering blockers (Sections 3 and 7).

## 2. Background and Related Work

The framework sits at the intersection of probabilistic forecasting methodology, hierarchical coherence, and structured paradigm-shift assessment. Eight strands of prior work shape its design.

Probabilistic rather than point forecasting is the first strand. [1] models intermittent demand with Gaussian-process latent functions coupled to negative binomial and Tweedie forecast distributions, and reports that on the M5 data set the scaled quantile score at the $0.95$ level is $1.16$ for TweedieGP against $1.18$ for NegBinGP, with the two models performing similarly overall but TweedieGP better on high quantiles; the paper's conclusion is that prediction "should be based on probability distributions rather than on point forecasts." We adopt the same distributional stance for era states: each era carries a full distribution over optimistic/baseline/pessimistic outcomes, not a single expected milestone date. [2] shows that a deliberately simple structured model — quantile regression on a log-transformed, trend-and-remainder decomposition of load — took 2nd place in the GEFCom2017 open data track among roughly 100 teams, with a pinball score smaller than the Vanilla benchmark in 90% of all cases; this supports our choice of a small, transparent parameterization (three states, a handful of matrices) over expressive but opaque alternatives. [3] reaches a compatible conclusion in the same competition family: a lasso-estimated threshold autoregressive model reduced average pinball score by $11.9\%$ against a Recency benchmark and $15.6\%$ against Vanilla in the GEFCom2014-E study, while its own discussion flags heteroscedastic residuals as an unmodeled assumption — a caution we mirror in Section 6 for our own constant-transition-matrix assumption.

Structure and coherence form the second strand. [4] introduces the Deep Poisson Mixture Network, which learns the joint distribution of bottom-level series so that hierarchical probabilistic coherence is guaranteed by construction rather than imposed by post-hoc reconciliation, evaluating with quantile and continuous ranked probability scores against HierE2E, PERMBU-MinT, ARIMA variants, and GLM-Poisson. Our cascade has the same self-coherence property in miniature: because each era's marginal is a stochastic matrix product, era probabilities sum to one automatically, and no reconciliation step is needed. [6] argues from electricity price forecasting that dependency structure must be preserved end to end — its contribution is a bivariate peak/off-peak model with bivariate jump occurrences in the mean-reverting-jump framework, evaluated with criteria that take dependencies into account, on the grounds that market participants trading derivatives need the correlation structure intact. The analogue in our setting is exact: an analyst hedging across eras needs the joint era distribution, which the cascade provides and an independent-era model destroys.

Automated selection and pooling form the third strand. [5] develops LASSO Principal Component Averaging, which pools forecasts from a single model estimated over a pool of 673 calibration window lengths ranging from 56 to 728 days, selects tuning by information criteria, and reports a fully automated mean percentage deviation of $-10.271\%$ relative to a reference in its Table 5 comparison — the argument being that automation removes researchers' ad hoc decisions. Our choke-node ranking serves the same discipline for red-teaming: rather than an analyst choosing which assumptions to stress by intuition, the leverage derivative orders all conditional entries mechanically. [8] contributes the cautionary result: a sequential Bayesian model-selection procedure for regular vine copulas beat the Dißmann frequentist heuristic in 97 and 98 of 100 replications on its first two simulation scenarios (relative log-likelihoods $81.0\%$ versus $76.6\%$ and $84.9\%$ versus $77.6\%$), but won 0 of 100 replications on Scenario 3, and its own conclusion states that the large gap between scenarios "shows the limits of our tree-by-tree approach towards the selection of the tree structure." Structure selection is where Bayesian cascade methods are most fragile, and our framework's assumption that the era ordering and three-state granularity are given — not selected — is a deliberate scope restriction in light of that result. [7] supplies the multimodal-pipeline precedent: the Hurricast framework combines deep feature extraction with gradient-boosted prediction and an ElasticNet ensemble, and its best model reaches 40% skill against CLP5 for 24-hour track forecasting on the Eastern Pacific basin while outperforming Decay-SHIPS by 12% in intensity MAE; the lesson we take is that staged pipelines with explicit evaluation against operational baselines are the accepted currency of forecasting credibility, which is why Section 3 specifies a back-casting baseline comparison rather than a standalone demonstration.

The fourth strand is the paradigm-forecast literature itself. [10] documents the CFPE pipeline: eight stages from domain scoping through a Bayesian cascading dependency model (S5) to a calibration register with back-testing, with key innovations including adversarial survival-rate tracking, four-level evidence grading (direct experiment through speculative extrapolation), fault-tree decomposition into AND/OR-gated leaves, and counterfactual pathway generation. Its back-cast from a 1976 vantage point to 2026 scores two era predictions as hits (microprocessor scaling, networked computing) and two as partial (ubiquitous computing plus AI, where the smartphone form factor was missed; quantum supremacy plus AGI, where the AGI timeline was overestimated). CFPE is the closest existing method, and the present paper is best read as the formalization of its stage S5: the DAG becomes a stochastic matrix chain, and the red-team stage acquires a quantitative target through the leverage derivative. [9] contributes the era concept itself — number systems as a stratigraphy of expanding distinction operations, each era "not wrong, but incomplete" — and, importantly, documents a merger failure mode found in cross-review: the possibility that two adjacent eras collapse into one, which forced a hedge allocation increase from 10% to 25%. Our model currently assumes a fixed number of eras; Section 6 treats era merging as an open structural risk. [11] demonstrates an era-structured forecast of computing machines built on an invariant dynamic — constraint saturation, substrate shift, Wright's Law cost decline, institutional adoption — and registers twelve falsifiable predictions for 2030–2040 with explicit disconfirmation criteria (for example, the thesis fails if CMOS scaling does not effectively end by 2035). That paper supplies the kind of forecast object our mathematics should underwrite; it registers predictions but does not publish a transition-matrix cascade or a leverage ranking. [12] supplies the reference-class discipline: its assessment of non-Archimedean geometry notes that phylogenetics and isogeny-based cryptography are existence proofs that the paradigm is viable, yet the historical reference class is unequivocal that zero of the three closest revolutionary analogues achieved mainstream adoption within a decade, and no empirical anomaly against the Archimedean default has been identified. This coexistence of mechanism-level plausibility with a pessimistic base rate is precisely the situation our three-state model is built to represent, and it motivates stress testing the optimistic column hardest.

What is missing from all strands, and what this paper adds, is a closed-form, computable ranking of conditional transition entries by their leverage on total lifecycle expected value, together with an exact stress-response formula. The forecasting literature [1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8] provides distributional machinery and evaluation discipline but not era cascades; the paradigm-forecast literature [9,10,11,12] provides era cascades but not the mathematics.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Model definition

We model a technology lifecycle as $K$ eras. Era $k \in \{1,\dots,K\}$ ends in one of three states: optimistic ($O$), baseline ($B$), or pessimistic ($P$). The state of era $k$ is a random variable $S_k \in \{O,B,P\}$. The initial belief before era 1 is a distribution $\pi_0 \in \mathbb{R}^3$ with $\pi_{0,j} \geq 0$ and $\sum_j \pi_{0,j} = 1$. Each era boundary $k$ carries a row-stochastic transition matrix

$$P_k = \begin{pmatrix} p^{(k)}_{OO} & p^{(k)}_{OB} & p^{(k)}_{OP} \\ p^{(k)}_{BO} & p^{(k)}_{BB} & p^{(k)}_{BP} \\ p^{(k)}_{PO} & p^{(k)}_{PB} & p^{(k)}_{PP} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p^{(k)}_{ij} \geq 0, \quad \sum_j p^{(k)}_{ij} = 1,$$

where $p^{(k)}_{ij} = \Pr(S_k = j \mid S_{k-1} = i)$ is the conditional probability that era $k$ ends in state $j$ given that era $k-1$ ended in state $i$. The Markov assumption is the framework's core modeling commitment: era outcomes are conditioned on the previous era's state only. The marginal distribution of era $k$ is

$$\pi_k = \pi_0 \, P_1 P_2 \cdots P_k. \tag{1}$$

Because every $P_k$ is row-stochastic, $\pi_k$ is automatically a probability vector: the cascade is coherent by construction in the sense of [4], requiring no reconciliation.

### 3.2 Expected value

Value accrues per era through a state-value vector $w = (w_O, w_B, w_P)^\top$, where $w_s$ is the value contributed by an era ending in state $s$ (for example, a capability index), and optionally a terminal value vector $u$ for the final state. Total lifecycle expected value is

$$\mathrm{EV} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \cdot w \;=\; \pi_0 \left(\sum_{k=1}^{K} P_1 \cdots P_{k-1}\right) w, \tag{2}$$

with the empty product equal to the identity. When only the final era carries value, (2) reduces to $\mathrm{EV} = \pi_K \cdot u$.

### 3.3 Choke nodes

Define the leverage of a conditional entry $p^{(k)}_{ij}$ as

$$L^{(k)}_{ij} \;=\; \left| \frac{\partial \mathrm{EV}}{\partial p^{(k)}_{ij}} \right| \;=\; \pi_{k-1,i} \, \bigl( P_{k+1} \cdots P_K \, v \bigr)_j, \tag{3}$$

where $v = w$ if value accrues only in the era immediately after the transition and $v$ accumulates downstream per-era and terminal value otherwise; the general form follows because $\partial \pi_k / \partial p^{(k)}_{ij} = \pi_{k-1,i} \, e_j$
