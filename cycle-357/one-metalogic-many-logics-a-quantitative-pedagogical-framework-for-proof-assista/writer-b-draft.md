# One Metalogic, Many Logics: A Quantitative Pedagogical Framework for Proof‑Assistant‑Based Logic Teaching with LogiKEy (revised)

## Abstract

Teaching logic to mixed cohorts of computer‑science, mathematics, and philosophy students is costly because curricula must accommodate divergent backgrounds and because traditional courses fragment into separate propositional, modal, epistemic, and deontic modules. The specific gap addressed here is the absence of a quantitative account that explains why each pedagogical transition in the LogiKEy sequence is forced. We combine shallow semantical embeddings of object logics in classical higher‑order logic (HOL) with elementary combinatorial analysis of the state spaces that arise at each stage. The principal results are: (1) the propositional Liars‑and‑Truth‑Tellers puzzle admits $2^{3}=8$ assignments with exactly one survivor; (2) the modal re‑modeling over $|W|=8$ worlds yields $2^{64}\approx1.84\times10^{19}$ arbitrary accessibility relations and $2^{56}\approx7.21\times10^{16}$ reflexive ones; (3) the Wise Men dynamic‑epistemic model follows the trajectory $8\to7\to6\to4$, a shrinkage factor of $7/4=1.75$ from the model after the king's announcement to the final model; (4) Boolos's curious inference generates a proof‑length tower $L_{5}\approx10^{19\,728}$; (5) a first‑order interpretation space of size $2^{100}\approx1.27\times10^{30}$ contrasts with the compact higher‑order treatment; (6) Gödel's ontological argument reduces to $2^{5}=32$ raw positivity‑predicate interpretations and at least $2^{2}=4$ consistent ones under a stated independence assumption; (7) a course‑time projection allocates $2.8$, $4.2$, $4.2$, $5.6$, $5.6$, and $5.6$ contact hours to the six stages; (8) the propositional‑to‑modal contrast expands the search space by a factor $2^{61}\approx2.31\times10^{18}$. These numbers turn pedagogical motivations into explicit quantitative checkpoints, clarifying when automation, richer semantics, or higher‑order reasoning become indispensable. This revision corrects the Wise Men trajectory and shrinkage factor reported in the published version.

## 1. Introduction

A logic classroom that contains only computer‑science students can assume comfort with formalism and an interest in verification; a classroom that contains only philosophy students can assume interest in argument analysis but not in type theory. A mixed classroom—computer‑science, mathematics, and philosophy students together—inherits all of the difficulties and none of the homogeneity. The cost is concrete: instructors must prepare parallel on‑ramps for students who program fluently but have never written a proof, students who write rigorous proofs but have never seen one checked by a machine, and students who argue subtly in natural language but freeze at a quantifier. Two failure modes dominate. The first is fragmentation: each logic (propositional, modal, epistemic, deontic) is taught as a separate subject with separate notation, separate tools, and separate exercises, so students leave with a portfolio of disconnected techniques rather than a unified competence. The second is monotony: a single logic (usually classical first‑order) is taught as if it exhausted the field, leaving philosophy students without the non‑classical logics their discipline demands and computer‑science students without the modelling resources (agents, knowledge, obligation, action) their discipline increasingly uses.

What would change if instructors had a quantitative account of each transition? Curriculum design currently rests on intuition about when students are "ready" for modal logic or for dynamic epistemic updates. If each transition in the teaching sequence could be tied to a computed quantity—assignment‑space size, relation‑space size, model trajectory, proof length—the instructor could state, with a number, why the next representational layer is needed, how much contact time it deserves, and at which point manual enumeration becomes infeasible and automation becomes mandatory. That is the capability this paper supplies for the LogiKEy teaching sequence.

The LogiKEy methodology proposes a third way between fragmentation and monotony: logico‑pluralism on a single technical substrate. Classical higher‑order logic (HOL) serves as a universal metalogic. Each object logic—classical or non‑classical—is encoded by defining its semantics in HOL: possible worlds become HOL individuals, accessibility relations become HOL relations on those individuals, modal operators become HOL functions quantifying over worlds. Through such shallow semantical embeddings, one proof assistant (e.g. Isabelle/HOL), with its automated theorem provers and (counter‑)model finders, becomes a single laboratory in which students define logics, prove their metatheorems, and compare their expressive power. The graded sequence of classroom examples—Liars‑and‑Truth‑Tellers, the Wise Men puzzle, Boolos's curious inference, Chisholm's paradox, and Gödel's ontological argument—illustrates how each representational upgrade expands a measurable quantity (search‑space size, model‑enumeration burden, proof length) and thereby forces a pedagogical transition.

This revision is a post‑publication correction of the published version [9]. The published abstract and one derivation stated a Wise Men trajectory $8\to7\to4$ with an inconsistent shrinkage factor; the corrected derivation, given in Section 4.3 and consistent across independent recomputations, is $8\to7\to6\to4$ with shrinkage factor $7/4=1.75$. The published abstract also stated a generalisation of the trajectory to $2^{8}=256$ agents; no derivation in the paper supports that generalisation, and it is withdrawn here (Section 6 discusses why the generalisation is non‑trivial). All other quantitative results of [9] were re‑derived from their stated inputs and stand unchanged.

## 2. Background and Related Work

The LogiKEy methodology is presented in [1] and [2]. Both works report an approach to teaching logic to mixed groups of computer‑science, mathematics, and philosophy students, based on the logico‑pluralistic LogiKEy methodology, used for more than a decade in courses, summer schools, and tutorials; LogiKEy uses classical higher‑order logic as a universal metalogic in which object logics, classical and non‑classical alike, are encoded by defining their semantics, so that a single proof assistant with its automated theorem provers and (counter‑)model finders becomes one environment in which students learn, experiment with, and compare logics [1], [2]. The same source articulates the mechanism this paper quantifies: in HOL one may quantify over functions and predicates, which makes it expressive enough to talk about other logics—their formulas, their notions of truth, their models—and $\lambda$‑abstraction makes the encodings compositional, so that a modal connective simply becomes a function on world‑dependent propositions [2]. Its conclusion distinguishes what is established (the machine‑checked formal results of the ladder of examples) from what is documented only by teaching experience (that a mixed cohort can be run through the course) [2]; the present paper adds a third category, derived quantitative checkpoints, and corrects one of them.

The placement of proof assistants and automation in the logic curriculum is supported by broader assessments of the field. The panel contributions collected in [3] report a discussion at the 2000 Association for Symbolic Logic meeting on the future directions of mathematical logic, in which applications to computer science were identified by many participants as of great importance; its section on proof theory and logic for computer science surveys active areas and closes with an exhortation for mathematical logicians to pay more attention to applications of logic in computer science [3]. A curriculum that trains students on automated proof assistants inside a single metalogic is a direct response to that exhortation.
